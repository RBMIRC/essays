## Preamble
Reading Ninon Devis's doctoral thesis, recently defended at Sorbonne Université in May 2025 under the supervision of Charlotte Truchet and Carlos Agon, proved to be one of the most stimulating encounters with research-creation I have experienced in recent years. Her rigorous analysis of co-creativity in musical composition resonates with my own practice: I have long worked with modular synthesizers and their generative possibilities, developing a piece entitled Sub Rosa (2020) that explores precisely the threshold between human intention and machinic process that Devis theorizes. Reading her work, I recognized a kindred attempt to think with instruments rather than merely about them.
The belated discovery of K Allado-McDowell's books — Air Age Blueprint (2022) in particular — proved equally generative; it was perhaps my most valuable reading of 2025, offering a vocabulary for what I had been groping toward in my own writing practice. Pharmako-AI (2020), then Air Age Blueprint, written in direct collaboration with GPT-3, are not merely books about co-creativity with AI but artifacts of it — each chapter emerging from iterative exchanges between human prompt and machinic completion. These works put to the test the question of whether the theoretical possibilities described by Kittler and Eshun can be realized in actual practice, and what such realization demands of the writer.
Together, these works helped me articulate what I call "computational futurality": the sense that co-creative tools do not merely assist thought but open new temporal and conceptual horizons. This perspective informs the "retroactive continuity" methodology of my own thesis — a method that treats the archive not as a fixed past but as a field of virtual possibilities awaiting reactivation.
The prolonged use of technical apparatuses in my artistic practices — from modular synthesizers to MAX/MSP patches, from flatbed scanners to server architectures — has nourished a sustained reflection on tool-augmented creativity. Following Vilém Flusser, I have long understood these apparatuses not as mere instruments serving a prior intention but as programs whose operator is also a "functionary": devices that do not extend a preexisting subject but reconfigure the very conditions of thought and practice. What Stiegler calls "exteriorization" — technology as constitutive memory and milieu of the human — finds its operative translation here: each apparatus crystallizes new configurations of the human-machine assemblage, new ways of knowing and doing.
With the decision to co-write a series of essays (including this one) and the resulting thesis through a very methodically and experimentally trained LLM "by design," the theoretical question arises with renewed urgency.
What becomes of authorship when writing proceeds no longer from the inscription of a thought but from the selection, inflection, and relaunching of a generative process? This question, latent since the first mechanical reproduction of text, has become inescapable in the era of neural language models. The history of writing technologies is not simply a history of efficiency gains — faster transcription, wider dissemination — but a history of ontological displacements in what it means to produce text, and consequently, what it means to think.
## Essay Summary
This essay attempts to trace the genealogy of creativity from its theological origins through its Romantic interiorization to its contemporary reconceptualization in the context of artificial intelligence. Drawing on recent work in critical theory, sociology of art, phenomenology, and AI studies, the analysis unfolds along four major movements: first, an archaeology of the concepts of creativity revealing the historical contingency of current assumptions; second, a philosophical examination of the conditions distinguishing human creative practice from computational generation; third, a theorization of co-creativity as an emerging paradigm requiring new frameworks for understanding human-machine collaboration; fourth, a critical examination of the political economy of AI-assisted creation, including the capture of creativity by post-Fordist capitalism and the self-precarization of creative workers.
The argument advances the thesis that co-creativity constitutes neither the eclipse of human creative agency nor its mere augmentation, but rather its fundamental reconstitution — what emerges is a "meta-creativity" operating at the level of systemic orchestration rather than direct production. In other words, co-creativity is no longer inscribed within the system; it is about co-creating the system that creates. This reconstitution operates, however, under politico-economic conditions that must be rigorously analyzed: the collective cultural production from which AI draws, the labor conditions structuring creative practice, and the capture of creativity discourse by managerial ideology. The essay concludes by examining co-creativity through the lens of Jorge Luis Borges's Pierre Ménard and Marcel Duchamp's readymades — figures that illuminate how creativity can persist through selection, recontextualization, and the production of identical texts that nonetheless carry different meanings. Even under conditions of perfect AI simulation, human creative practice retains its significance: the words may be identical, but Pierre Ménard remains the author.

# I. Introduction: The Problem of Creativity in the Age of AI
## An Observation
The preceding preamble situated this inquiry within a practice: that of co-creative writing with a large language model, methodically and experimentally trained "by design." The question posed there — what becomes of authorship when writing proceeds no longer from the inscription of a thought but from the selection, inflection, and relaunching of a generative process? — is not merely theoretical. 

It emerges from a working experience in which inherited categories (author, tool, inspiration, technique) cease to function as they should.
The integration of artificial intelligence into creative practice has inaugurated what may be the most significant transformation in the conditions of cultural production since Walter Benjamin diagnosed the consequences of mechanical reproduction. 

Yet the conceptual apparatus through which we attempt to grasp this transformation remains profoundly indebted to frameworks that predate not only AI but the very notion of computation. 

As Raunig, Ray, and Wuggenig observe in their introduction to Critique of Creativity:
"Creativity is astir: reborn, re-conjured, re-branded, resurgent. 

The old myths of creation and creators — the hallowed labors and privileged agencies of demiurges and prime movers, of Biblical world-makers and self-fashioning artist-geniuses — are back underway, producing effects, circulating appeals." (Raunig, Ray & Wuggenig, 2011, p. 1)
This "troubled resurrection" of creativity discourse arrives at precisely the moment when AI systems demonstrate capabilities that challenge the ontological boundaries supposedly separating human from machinic production. 

The question is not simply whether AI can be "creative" in a technical sense, but rather what becomes of creativity as a concept — and of creative practice as a mode of human existence — when statistical pattern-matching produces results indistinguishable from human-generated work.
The stakes extend beyond aesthetic theory. 

They touch on what Longo, Punzi, and Zaterini call a "new crisis of modernity" — not a crisis of innovation (AI produces it in abundance) but a crisis of the meaning of innovation, an uncertainty about what "to create" can still mean when machines generate, combine, and transform at scales and speeds that exceed any individual capacity (Longo, Punzi & Zaterini, 2025, p. 13). 

It is within this uncertainty that co-creative practice must situate itself — not as a solution but as a site of experimentation.
## The Myth of Pure Creation
Historically, creativity has been understood as a form of "divine madness," a sacred gift in which the poet or artist acts as a mere channel for an external muse or spiritual force. 

This conception — which we might call the "Platonic wound" — established a vision of the creator as a "solitary genius" whose inspirations seem to spring from a conceptual void rather than from systematic learning or technical mediation.
Yet this myth ignores a reality that media archaeology has made impossible to evade: human thought has never been an entity separate from its technical prostheses. 

From the pen to the typewriter to the neural network, technology has "always already" mediated creation. 

As Nietzsche famously put it, our writing tools "work upon our thoughts" (Nietzsche's correspondence with Köselitz in 1882; Kittler elevated this to a founding aphorism of media archaeology). 

Creativity is not a disembodied spirit but what Hayles calls a "material metaphor" — the product of the dynamic interaction between a mindbody and its apparatus (Hayles, 2002).
Generative AI therefore does not break with a tradition of "pure" creation; it makes explicit what has always been the case — distributed agency, tool dependence, the technical mediation of thought — while radically transforming the terms of that distribution. 

It is this double operation — revelation and transformation — that this essay seeks to think through.
## Methodological Framework
The inquiry proceeds from the conviction that addressing the question of AI and creativity requires several interconnected analyses. 

First, a genealogical excavation of the concepts of creativity, exposing the historical contingency of assumptions that appear natural or inevitable. 

Second, a media-archaeological investigation examining how different technological regimes — inscription, processing, modulation — transform the conditions of textual production. 

Third, a theorization of co-creativity as an emerging paradigm — irreducible to tool use as much as to the fantasy of the autonomous creative machine. 

Fourth, a critical examination of the conditions and limits of this co-creativity.
Methodologically, this investigation adopts what Hayles calls "media-specific analysis" (MSA) — an approach that "moves from the language of text to a more precise vocabulary of screen and page, digital program and analog interface, code and ink, mutable image and durable mark, computer and book" (Hayles, 2002, pp. 30–31). 

Against the long-standing assumption that literary meaning resides in "style and feeling" while the material substrate is "merely accidental," MSA insists that "texts must always be embodied to exist in the world" (p. 31). 

To understand co-creativity, one must attend not only to what texts say but to how they are materially produced — the interfaces, parameters, and feedback loops through which human intention and machinic probability intertwine.
The aim is neither celebration nor denunciation, but rather what Stefan Nowotny calls a "critique of creativity" that would distinguish "what is idolatrous, ideological, false in this object from what may appear all the more legitimate the more it is purified by critique" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 10).
## Structure of the Argument
The essay unfolds across several major sections following this introduction.
Section II undertakes the genealogical excavation, tracing creativity from ancient conceptions of divine madness, through the Romantic interiorization of genius, to contemporary frameworks of embodied subjectivity.
Section III provides a media archaeology of writing technologies, examining the transformation from inscription to processing to modulation — from the Kittlerian paradigm of 1900 to synthesizers and neural networks.
Section IV examines the philosophical conditions distinguishing human creativity from computational generation, in dialogue with phenomenology, affect theory, and cultural analysis.
Section V analyzes the cognitive architecture of creativity — the informational and operative processes that contemporary research has identified, and the stages that remain beyond the reach of current systems.
Section VI examines the collective data imprint — the sociocultural archive on which models are trained and the imaginaries that shape our perception of AI.
Section VII theorizes co-creativity proper: Boden's tripartite model, the modulation paradigm, Allado-McDowell's practice, and the emergence of meta-creativity as a reconfiguration of artistic agency.
Section VIII examines the limits of autonomous AI creativity — the problem of context, the problem of quality, the horizon of the archive.
Section IX maps the "sensitive zone" — that intersection where embodied subjectivity and machinic capacity overlap, and where the stakes of collaboration are highest.
Sections X and XI develop the theoretical implications (entanglement, distributed agency) and practical implications (co-creative discipline, ethics of engagement) of this paradigm.
Section XII attempts to analyze the passage from artificial creativity to meta-creativity — its promises and dangers, from the erosion of craft to cultural homogenization.
The conclusion returns to the figures of Pierre Menard and Marcel Duchamp — the poet in Borges's story who "writes" a Quixote identical to Cervantes's, the artist who exhibits an industrial urinal as a work of art. 

These figures are not historical curiosities but keys to understanding how authorship persists through selection, recontextualization, and the production of meaning, even when the productions are formally identical to what machines could generate. 

If Menard can be the author of a text he did not "invent," then the co-creative practitioner can be the author of a text that AI could have generated without them. 

The question is not "who made the words?" but "what meaning emerges from this mode of production?"

# II. Genealogy of Creativity: From Divine Madness to Embodied Subjectivity
## The Theological Residue
Any serious analysis of creativity must begin by acknowledging its theological origins. The concept carries what Nowotny calls an "onto-theological schema" that persists beneath its secularized surface. Drawing on Paul Valéry, Nowotny traces how creativity discourse retains theological structures even when it no longer invokes divine agency:
"As soon as one speaks of something like the 'energy of the creator' — cipher for so many designations of what is presumed to be at work in creativity — a reality is already invoked that rises above all other reality; a reality that surpasses the 'natural fruits.'" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 9)
This schema positions the creator as analogous to the divine — "demiurge, prime cause, and world-maker" (Nowotny, 2011, pp. 15–16). Crucially, this theological heritage "has been less replaced by modern ideas of creativity connected with 'artistic creation' than assimilated by them — at least in a certain presumption of these ideas still in force today, which insists on the artist subject as a reality of creation withdrawn from the world, 'drawing on itself'" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 16).
This theological structure traces back to the Greeks. The poet of Plato's Ion is "a light and winged and sacred thing, and is unable ever to indite until he has been inspired and put out of his senses" (Ion, 534b). As Devis traces in her thesis, this "divine madness" (theia mania) was not mere irrationality but a "catalytic force that unlocks deeper truths about harmony, the human psyche, and cosmic order" (Devis, 2025, p. 151). This framework — creativity as reception rather than production, as gift rather than accomplishment — persists in secularized form whenever creativity is attributed to "inspiration" or the "muse."
## Before the Machine
Before examining how machines transform creativity, we must clarify what the term designates. As Esling and Devis note, the concept is "particularly multifaceted and complex to define" (2020, p. 2). In the collective imagination, "the central aspect of creativity lies in novelty" — creativity "implies traversing our preconceived knowledge and creating a rupture between the present and the future" (p. 2, citing Craft, 2003). Yet novelty alone is insufficient. Creative acts must also possess quality (the appeal of the idea) and relevance (fitness to the task and the era). These three criteria — novelty, quality, relevance — form the triangulation by which every creative act is evaluated (Kaufman & Sternberg, 2010).
A decisive point: creativity "does not arise from a conceptual void" (Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 2). Even the most radical innovation emerges from a context, a tradition, a set of inherited forms — it is "the result of community-built expertise, later transformed in an incremental fashion" (p. 3). This is the paradox of creativity: it must break with what exists while remaining intelligible to those formed by that existence.
## A Romantic Vision
The modern conception of creativity emerges through what can be described as a fundamental relocation of creative power from divine to human sources. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries witnessed what might be called the "interiorization of genius": the divine source of creative power was not eliminated but relocated within the exceptional individual.
Edward Young's Conjectures on Original Composition (1759) illustrates this transformation, declaring that originality "rises spontaneously from vital root of genius; it grows, it is not made" (Young, 1966). The organic metaphor is essential: genius is natural rather than acquired, spontaneous rather than learned, rooted in the unique nature of the individual rather than derived from external models or rules.
Immanuel Kant's Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) provides the philosophical foundation for this Romantic conception. Kant defines genius as "the talent (natural gift) that gives the rule to art" (Kant, 2000, §46). Several features of this definition prove decisive.
First, genius is a natural gift — a talent that cannot be explained by prior causes nor acquired through education. Second, genius gives rules rather than follows them: it is not a matter of applying preexisting principles but of engendering new ones that later artists may imitate. Third — and most importantly for our purposes — genius is "completely opposed to imitation" and "cannot be learned." Genius operates through what Kant calls "original nonsense" — productions so unprecedented that no prior example could have guided their creation.
This Kantian framework establishes a problematic that persists in contemporary debates. When critics claim that AI "cannot be creative" because it merely recombines existing patterns, they invoke categories derived from Kant's analysis of genius. The very criteria by which AI creativity is judged derive from historically specific formations — the Romantic ideology of individual genius — that themselves call for critical examination.
## The Arguments from Imitation and Rules
Two classic arguments deny that creativity can be learned — arguments that resurface whenever we ask whether AI can "truly" create:
The argument from imitation (after Gaut, 2010):
	1	All learning is a form of imitation.
	2	Imitation is incompatible with creativity.
	3	Therefore, one cannot learn to be creative.
The argument from rules (after Kant):
	1	All learning consists of following rules.
	2	Following rules is incompatible with creativity.
	3	Therefore, one cannot learn to be creative.
Both arguments rest on a vision of creativity as "rupture" — a break with precedent that no systematic process can produce. As Devis observes, "whether the source of creativity is located in the divine, the unconscious, or the transcendental, these conceptions insist that authentic novelty cannot arise from something as prosaic as systematic learning" (2025, p. 156).
Contemporary cognitive science has contested these arguments without entirely dissolving them. As Devis traces, the notion of "generative learning" emphasizes that learners "actively reorganize, dissect, and combine acquired skills or concepts" — a process supported by flexible heuristics rather than rigid algorithms. Far from locking the learner into a rigid mold, imitation provides access to a cultural and conceptual domain that can then be reshaped (Devis, 2025, p. 156).
## The Social Turn: From Individual Genius to Cre-activity
A crucial development in creativity theory involves the shift from individual genius to attention to social and collective dimensions. From Marx to Benjamin, classical sociology showed that creativity is not the property of exceptional individuals but a capacity whose expression is determined by material conditions — the artwork becoming commodity, the artist becoming worker, creativity becoming a resource to be extracted. It is this diagnosis that Stefan Nowotny radicalizes.
Stefan Nowotny's concept of cre-activity — hyphenated to distinguish it from captured "creativity" — provides the most rigorous theoretical alternative to Romantic individualism. Drawing on Bakhtin, Nowotny argues for "the foundational sociality" of creative practice, opposing what conventional stylistics fails to recognize: "the social life of the word outside the artist's workshop, in the squares and streets, in the cities and villages, in the groups, generations, and epochs" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 18, citing Bakhtin, 1975, p. 154).
Nowotny thus reformulates the stakes: thinking "the problem of cre-activity in a multiplicity of existences (instead of attributing and subordinating it to the individualism of a Boheme)" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 18).
Nowotny refines this position by contesting exteriorist conceptions of critique — those demanding "distance" from what is criticized, thereby missing "the question of immanent effects" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 13). Against this stance of overview, he reactivates the Marxian concept of critique as "practical-critical activity" involving "self-transformation" rather than external judgment:
"It is not enough to fix 'objects' worthy of critique or to strive to 'change' them if one simultaneously reproduces an operative structure that persistently produces precisely these objects in their reality. This form of critique resembles a racing greyhound chasing a fake rabbit attached to its own neck by a pole." (Nowotny, 2011, p. 14)
The key formulation: "The question is not where one stands in relation to the effects of critique, but where one stands within them" (Nowotny, 2011, p. 17). Applied to AI creativity, this suggests that critique cannot proceed from an external position of judgment — declaring what AI "really is" or cannot do — but must proceed through immanent engagement, standing within the effects of AI systems rather than surveying them from above.
## The Dissolution of the Solitary Genius
The social dimension of creativity receives further elaboration through Brian Eno's concept of "scenius" — a contraction of scene and genius designating "the intelligence and intuition of an entire cultural scene" rather than the isolated individual genius (Eno, 2020). Late-1970s Berlin rock, South Bronx hip-hop, the Fluxus movement: all cases where innovation emerges not from an individual but from a milieu — "through the synergy of influences, relationships, dialogues, and mutual inspirations" (Eno, 2020).
Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's systemic model of creativity provides the most rigorous framework for understanding this social embeddedness. Creativity, according to this model, is not a property of individuals but an emergent phenomenon arising from the interaction of three systems: the individual creator, the domain of knowledge and practice, and the field of experts and institutions that evaluate and legitimize creative contributions (Csikszentmihalyi, 2015). The domain represents accumulated cultural knowledge — techniques, symbols, conventions, and rules — that defines a particular creative practice. The field comprises the institutions and social networks that evaluate creative contributions and determine which novelties will be retained in the domain. The individual creator operates within this system, drawing on domain knowledge and producing works that must pass through the field's filtering processes to have cultural impact.
This systemic vision fundamentally challenges Romantic individualism. The "creative individual" is not the source but a node in a larger system. What appears as individual inspiration is in fact the interiorization of domain knowledge; what appears as personal breakthrough depends on field recognition for its cultural significance. Even the most apparently revolutionary creative acts are intelligible only against the background of the domains they transform and the fields that recognize their transformation.
The paradoxical prejudice this narrative engenders — creativity simultaneously celebrated and marginalized, the artist as visionary and madman — persists in contemporary AI discourse.
And yet, even at the heart of the Romantic paradigm, co-creativity lurked at the margins. The Romantic genius did not create ex nihilo but drew upon tradition, convention, the accumulated sediment of language itself. Breton and Soupault's automatic writing, the exquisite corpse, objective chance — all pointed to an agency exceeding individual intention. The unconscious, that great discovery of the nineteenth century, named precisely this excess: a writing that writes through us. The paradigm of inscription could acknowledge this excess only under the categories of inspiration, possession, or madness — categories that preserved the myth of the unified author while sketching its dissolution.

# III. Media Archaeology of Writing Technologies: From Inscription to Modulation
## The Myth and the Hand
Plato's myth of Theuth establishes writing as a technology of separation: writing is a pharmakon — remedy and poison — that will produce not memory but reminding, not wisdom but its semblance. 

The text, once inscribed, circulates, unable to defend itself, unable to choose its interlocutors. 

Yet within this separation lies the condition of possibility for all subsequent co-creativity: the text as autonomous agent, capable of acting in its author's absence.
In the discourse network of 1800 — Friedrich Kittler's term for the institutional and technological apparatus regulating the storage, transmission, and processing of information — writing remained bound to the human body. 

The hand held the pen; the pen touched the paper; the paper received the mark. 

This chain of contact preserved what Kittler calls the "hallucinatory sensuality" of Romantic authorship: such "sensuousness (and sensuality) stored in Poetry is characteristic of an age in which the medium of the book is first universal — for all realms of the senses and people — and second without competition from other sound and media" (Kittler, 1990, p. 115).
The mother taught the child to read by voicing the letters, transforming arbitrary marks into a flow of natural speech. 

Poetry was the privileged genre because it most closely approximated the voice — the author's breath passing through typography to reanimate in the reader's inner ear. 

The author was origin, not operator; the text was expression, not process.
This conception rested on what Hayles identifies as a fundamental disavowal: the materiality of the book — its paper, ink, binding — was rendered invisible so that the author's "spirit" could seem to flow directly into the reader's consciousness (Hayles, 2002, p. 32). 

The codex was a window, not a body. 

This fantasy of disembodiment would persist until the technological disruptions of the twentieth century made the body of the text unavoidably visible once again.
## The Industrial Break and the Storage Revolution
The typewriter, as Kittler demonstrates in Gramophone, Film, Typewriter, effected a revolution deeper than the printing press. 

Gutenberg mechanized the reproduction of text; Remington mechanized its production. 

For the first time, writing became "blind" — the typist's eyes no longer needed to follow the appearance of letters on the page. 

The hand no longer drew forms but struck keys; the body no longer inscribed but operated.
Nietzsche, one of the first philosopher-typists, noted the transformation: "Our writing tools work upon our thoughts." The aphorism, that compressed and percussive form, emerged not despite the machine but through it. 

The typewriter demanded economy, speed, the subordination of reflection to rhythm. 

Writing became less like painting and more like playing an instrument — or, prophetically, like programming.
The typewriter did not act alone. 

Around 1900, a constellation of new technologies — the gramophone, the cinematograph, the telephone — fundamentally altered the landscape of inscription. 

For the first time, the "real" could be stored: not representations of sound or movement, but their analogical traces. 

The gramophone recorded the voice without passing through the alphabet; film captured movement without the mediation of description. 

Kittler's insight is that these media did not simply extend human capacities but bypassed them. 

The gramophone needle reads frequencies below the threshold of human perception; the camera captures movements too fast for the eye. 

Storage media created archives of the nonhuman, the pre-symbolic, the real that exceeds representation.
## The Author as Operator
In this new media ecology, the author could no longer claim the position of origin. 

Writing became one channel among others, and not necessarily the most privileged. 

In this new media ecology — which Hayles compares to an ecosystem where media compete, cooperate, and parasitize one another (Hayles, 2002, p. 5) — the author of 1900 had to adapt or perish.
The avant-gardes responded with strategies of appropriation, collage, automatic writing — methods that acknowledged the machinic nature of textual production. 

The surrealists' automatic writing, Burroughs's cut-up technique, developed with Brion Gysin in the late 1950s, made explicit what the typewriter had implied: writing as recombination, the author as editor of preexisting materials. "Writing is fifty years behind painting," Burroughs declared. 

The cut-up was his attempt to catch up — to treat text as the Cubists had treated visual representation, as material to be fragmented and reassembled according to logics other than narrative intention.
The cut-up is proto-algorithmic: it introduces the operations of chance, combinatorial procedures, collaboration with processes exceeding authorial control. 

Yet it remains bound to the paradigm of inscription — scissors and tape, physical manipulation of physical text. 

The passage to full co-creativity required a further transformation.
## Cybernetic Interlude
Cybernetics — the science of "control and communication in the animal and the machine" (Wiener, 1948) — introduced a concept foreign to the paradigm of inscription: the feedback loop. 

In classical writing, information flows in a single direction — from author to text. 

In a cybernetic system, output becomes input; the system responds to its own effects; regulation replaces expression. 

The "author" is no longer the origin but a node in a recursive circuit. 

Second-order cybernetics radicalized this insight by including the observer within the observed system: the author is inside the writing system, shaped by the very process they attempt to shape. 

The art world absorbed these insights through what the critic Jack Burnham called "systems aesthetics."
## Systems Aesthetics
In his influential 1968 Artforum essay, Burnham argued that contemporary art was undergoing a fundamental shift: "The priorities of the present conclude with less emphasis on the art object and an ever-greater concern for the system or process that produces art" (Burnham, 1968). 

Where traditional aesthetics focused on the thing — the painting, the sculpture, the finished work — systems aesthetics focused on the process: the interactions, feedback loops, and dynamic relationships from which art emerges.
This shift had profound implications for authorship. 

If the artwork is not an object but a system, then the artist is not a maker but a systems designer — someone who establishes initial conditions, sets parameters, creates the framework within which aesthetic events occur. 

The artist "programs" the system; the system "executes" the art. 

Control is distributed; authorship is diffused across the network of interactions.
Burnham's framework anticipated the condition of co-creative writing with AI by half a century. 

The LLM is precisely a system that the human does not directly control but parameterizes — setting prompts, adjusting temperature, establishing constraints within which the generative process unfolds. 

The "author" is neither the human nor the machine but the system they constitute together.
## E.A.T.
The most ambitious realization of cybernetic aesthetics came through E.A.T. — Experiments in Art and Technology — founded in 1966 by the engineer Billy Klüver and the artist Robert Rauschenberg. 

E.A.T. formalized the collaboration between artists and engineers, creating a model of co-creation that resonates directly with contemporary human-AI practice.
The founding event was 9 Evenings: Theatre and Engineering (October 1966, New York), a series of performances pairing ten artists with thirty engineers from Bell Laboratories. 

The technical systems developed for these performances were not mere tools in service of artistic vision; they were collaborative partners that shaped the work in unpredictable ways.
Rauschenberg's contribution, Open Score, exemplified the cybernetic logic. 

Two performers played tennis with rackets equipped with contact microphones and FM transmitters; each hit produced a sound that controlled the arena's lighting, progressively dimming the space to total darkness. 

Infrared cameras then revealed five hundred volunteers moving across the court, their invisible presence made visible through technological mediation. 

The "author" of Open Score was the feedback loop itself: racket-to-microphone-to-sound-to-light-to-camera-to-image. 

Rauschenberg designed the system; the system produced the art.
John Cage's Variations VII, performed the same evening, pushed the cybernetic principle further. 

Cage created a network connecting the performance space to sounds from across New York — telephone lines, radio broadcasts, police frequencies, household appliances activated by distant performers. 

The piece had no score in the traditional sense; it was a system for capturing and modulating environmental signals. 

The performer's role was to mix, filter, and shape an incoming flow of information — to navigate a sonic environment rather than express an inner vision. 

Cage described his aesthetic as "the acceptance of all sounds that occur" within a system designed to let them occur.
Four years later, E.A.T. realized its most comprehensive cybernetic environment: the Pepsi Pavilion at Expo '70 in Osaka. 

The pavilion was not an exhibition space containing works but a work that was a space — a responsive environment where fog, light, sound, and movement interacted through feedback systems. 

A spherical mirror twenty-seven meters in diameter reflected visitors as floating images; a cloud of artificial fog responded to air currents and body heat; a sound system distributed thirty-seven speakers across the dome, creating shifting acoustic environments. 

Visitors did not observe the work; they participated in it, their presence feeding back into the system that shaped their experience.
The Pepsi Pavilion anticipated what we now call "immersive" or "interactive" art, but its deepest lesson concerns authorship. 

Klüver insisted that the pavilion had no single author: "The artist does not simply 'use' technology... 

Collaboration is a conversation" (Klüver, 1972). 

Engineer and artist, human and machine, system and environment: all were nodes in a network of mutual influence. 

The "work" was the network itself — its feedback loops, its emergent behaviors, its capacity to surprise its own creators.
## The Loom and the Neural Network
Cybernetics thus offered a new figure of authorship: not the genius expressing their interiority but the pilot (κυβερνήτης, kybernetes) navigating a dynamic system — participating in a process that exceeds individual control, responding to conditions in real time, making continuous adjustments to maintain a course that is itself subject to revision.
Kittler's media archaeology, for all its power, remains bound to a certain historiography of discrete technological ruptures — the typewriter around 1900, the computer around 1945. 

Sadie Plant's Zeros and Ones (1997) offers a complementary genealogy that traces not ruptures but continuities: the "machinic phylum" running from the first weaving to the latest neural architectures. "Textiles themselves are very literally the software linings of all technology" (Plant, 1997, p. 12). 

The choice of "software" as adjective is deliberate: textiles are not merely precursors to software but its material substrate, the literal fabric from which the possibility of computation emerged.
The crucial figure here is the Jacquard loom, whose punch cards — anticipating both Babbage's engine and Hollerith's census machines — established the principle of automated pattern-making through binary encoding. 

But Plant's insight runs deeper than technological ancestry. 

What the loom demonstrates is the immanence of pattern to process: "The visible pattern is integral to the process which produced it; the program and the pattern are continuous" (p. 66). 

Unlike painting, where an image is imposed on a passive canvas, or writing, where signs are inscribed on a blank page, weaving produces patterns that emerge from the operation itself. 

The fabric does not represent; it is the crossing of threads.
This distinction — between imposed representation and emergent pattern — illuminates what is genuinely new about neural text generation. 

The LLM does not inscribe text on a blank surface; it weaves text from the crossed threads of statistical relationships. 

The "latent space" of a language model is not a page awaiting inscription but a loom already strung with the warp of all its training data, awaiting only the weft of a prompt to produce its pattern. 

The output is not a representation of thought but the visible face of a computational process — continuous with that process, inseparable from it.
## Ada Lovelace
Plant recovers Ada Lovelace from the margins of computing history not as an honorary exception but as the theorist of a computational logic very different from the linear procedures of male Victorian engineering. 

Lovelace's notes on the "Analytical Engine" introduced the concept of recursive operation: "A cycle of operations must then be understood to mean any set of operations which is repeated more than once. 

It is equally a cycle, whether it is repeated twice only, or an indefinite number of times; for it is the fact of a repetition occurring at all that constitutes it such. 

In many cases of analysis there is a recurring group of one or more cycles; that is, a cycle of cycle, or a cycle of cycles..." (Lovelace, cited in Plant, 1997, p. 20).
This is not merely iteration but recursion — the capacity of a process to take its own output as input, to fold back upon itself, to generate complexity through self-reference. 

Charles Babbage — the Victorian mathematician whose Analytical Engine (1837), never built in his lifetime, anticipated the architecture of the modern computer a century before Turing — himself described this Engine as "ever eating its own tail," such that "the results of the calculation appearing in the table column could be brought to affect the other columns, and thereby change the instructions entered into the machine" (cited in Plant, 1997, p. 20). 

The machine modifies its own program during execution — precisely the architecture of modern machine learning, where each pass through the training data adjusts the weights that determine subsequent outputs.
Lovelace's vision anticipates not only the computer but the neural network: a system whose operations are not fixed sequences but recursive cycles, whose patterns emerge from the continuous feedback of output to input. 

The contemporary LLM, trained through billions of such recursive adjustments, is the fulfillment of an architecture she theorized 180 years before the hardware existed to implement it.
## Distributed Processing and the Condition of Co-Creativity
Plant's most provocative gesture is to locate the cognitive architecture of distributed computation not in military research or corporate innovation but in the historically feminized labor of textile production and secretarial work. 

Describing the switchboard operators, typists, and data-entry workers of the early twentieth century, she writes: "parallel processing, switching between functions at the cry of a child, the ring of the door, a sudden flash of dream sequence; distributed systems, adaptive networks, brains dispersed" (Plant, 1997, p. 106). 

The syntax itself performs what it describes — paratactic, non-hierarchical, multiple processes running simultaneously without central coordination.
This is not mere metaphor. 

The cognitive demands of "women's work" — perpetual interruption, juggling incompatible tasks, attention distributed across multiple domains — constitute a form of parallel processing that the male model of focused, sequential cognition could neither perform nor recognize. "Her brain is a matrix of ceaseless digital flickerings" (p. 106): the neural network avant la lettre, processing not by linear chains of reasoning but by the simultaneous activation of distributed nodes.
The implications for understanding co-creativity with AI are considerable. 

If we approach neural text generation through the lens of the solitary male genius — singular, focused, proceeding from a clear intention to a finished work — we will misunderstand both its capabilities and its demands. 

But if we approach it through the lens of distributed, parallel, recursive processing — the loom rather than the pen, the switchboard rather than the study — we find a cognitive architecture already familiar, already theorized, already practiced for centuries under conditions of invisibility and devaluation.
This media archaeology has thus traced the transformation of the material conditions of textual production — from inscription to feedback, from weaving to distributed processing. 

Each technical regime reconfigured not only the means but the very meaning of the act of writing, displacing the author from the position of origin to that of operator, then cybernetic pilot, then node in a distributed network. 

It remains to examine what, in the human experience of creation, is not reducible to these material conditions — that phenomenological core of intentionality, affect, and cultural anchoring that constitutes the embodied subjectivity of the creator.

# IV. The Philosophical Conditions of Human Creativity
## Intentionality and the Inner Horizon
The phenomenological tradition, from Brentano through Husserl to Heidegger, provides essential resources for understanding what distinguishes human creative practice. At the center stands the concept of intentionality — not simply "doing something on purpose" but the constitutive "aboutness" of conscious states: all consciousness is always consciousness of something, every thought aims at an object, every gaze is directed toward a horizon (Brentano, 2012; Husserl, 2002). When a writer works on a text, their consciousness is not an empty receptacle that words come to fill — it is already oriented: toward a problem that obsesses them, toward a form that resists, toward an imagined reader. This prior orientation filters, selects, lends weight to certain possibilities over others.
Human creative action emerges from a being that cares about the world — what Heidegger calls Sorge — that has stakes in aesthetic outcomes, that exists temporally and historically (Heidegger, 1993). This existential dimension — what one might call the "inner horizon" of creative engagement — constitutes a fundamental difference from computational processes, however sophisticated their outputs. The concept of Abgrund (the groundless ground) in Heidegger's later philosophy names the space where thought confronts its own limits, engendering new modes of being by crossing the horizon of the familiar (Heidegger, 1991) — a confrontation with indeterminacy that has historically been the engine of artistic innovation.
As Devis argues, creativity requires that "an agent knowingly guides the creative process, using deliberate choice and critical discernment" (Devis, 2025, p. 162). A snowflake may be unique and beautiful, but it is not creative because "no agent stands behind its formation." AI produces outputs without the "intentional stance to recognize and justify departures from established norms."
## Emotional Resonance and Affects
The second dimension concerns the role of emotion in creative work. From ancient theories of musical ethos to contemporary affective aesthetics, Western thought has consistently recognized the emotional dimension as constitutive — not merely ornamental — of aesthetic meaning. The transformative power of art derives from the artist's subjective experience of emotion — grief, joy, wonder — transmuted into forms that resonate across audiences. This transmutation presupposes a lived experience that AI systems do not possess.
As Devis argues, AI systems can simulate emotional cues — sentimental chord progressions, tragic narrative arcs — but they "do so without experiencing any affective state of their own" (2025, p. 170). They "lack the very epistemic freedom that makes human imagination such fertile ground for innovation" (p. 195). The question remains disputed — the pre-personal intensities that affect theory distinguishes from subjective emotion might be produced independently of the generator's experience — but the phenomenological point stands: human creative practice emerges from beings who feel, and this feeling is not incidental to the practice but constitutive of it.
## Cultural Anchoring and Contextual Understanding
The third dimension concerns the social and cultural context within which creative work acquires meaning. Drawing on the systemic model of Csikszentmihalyi developed above, this dimension emphasizes that creative acts gain their significance only through engagement with communal frameworks. AI systems process cultural materials as data — patterns to be extracted and combined — but they do not inhabit culture as participants. As Devis notes, "innovations hold only when they have been approved and sanctioned by the community" (2025, p. 171). AI "does not experience the communal memory that invests symbolic structures with collective meaning, nor does it grasp the historical momentum underlying them" (p. 173).
## Embodied Subjectivity as Synthesis
These three dimensions — intentionality, emotional resonance, and cultural anchoring — can be synthesized in the concept of embodied subjectivity: what one might call, echoing Donna Haraway's "situated knowledges" (1988), a situated creating — the reflexive, affective, and socially anchored standpoint from which truly transformational creative impulses emerge. Embodied subjectivity is not an abstract universal but a position: it creates from somewhere, with a body, a history, affiliations that are not biases to be corrected but the very conditions of vision.
This concept is reducible neither to software routines nor to external rules. It is shaped by personal experience, by the social norms one chooses to adopt or contest, and by the intangible sense of self that motivates the creator's drive to produce something original or culturally resonant. Embodied subjectivity also reveals itself in the confrontation with material resistance — the wood that splits, the clay that sags, the word that rings false — from which the creator discovers possibilities they had not anticipated.
As Devis synthesizes: "Embodied subjectivity is shaped by personal experience, by the social norms one chooses to adopt or contest, and by the intangible sense of self that motivates the composer's drive to create something original" (Devis, 2025, p. 185). This term names the triad of intentionality, emotional resonance, and contextual anchoring as a unified capacity that remains irreducibly human.
Imagination, unlike belief, is not truth-bound — it can explore counterfactuals, hypotheticals, possibilities "without being constrained by empirical verification" (Devis, 2025, p. 194). AI, by contrast, is intrinsically bound to its loss function — that mathematical measure of error that structurally constrains the system to the regularities of its data. It cannot imagine in the relevant sense; it can only interpolate.
The concept of embodied subjectivity does not claim that AI cannot produce valuable results or that human-AI collaboration is impossible. Rather, it identifies the conditions under which transformational creativity — the capacity to break existing frames and inaugurate genuinely new possibilities — becomes possible. These conditions may be irreducibly tied to features of human existence: embodiment, temporality, mortality, social inscription, and care.
## Technique, Duration, and Novelty: Stiegler, Bergson, Koestler
The preceding synthesis calls, however, for further deepening. Affirming the irreducibility of embodied subjectivity is not enough: one must also understand how this subjectivity is constituted in and through technology, and what distinguishes authentic novelty from mechanical recombination.
Bernard Stiegler's philosophy of technology adds a crucial dimension here. In Technics and Time, Stiegler argues that human existence is constitutively entangled with technical systems — that there is no "natural" human prior to technical mediation (Stiegler, 1998). This might seem to support the idea that AI represents simply the latest chapter in humanity's technical evolution. But Stiegler's analysis also insists on the specificity of different technical regimes and their differential effects on human temporality and memory.
Digital technologies, for Stiegler, tend toward the "industrialization of memory" — the exteriorization of mnemonic functions that were previously sites of individuation and cultural transmission. Individuation, a concept borrowed from Simondon and reworked by Stiegler, designates the process by which an individual constitutes themselves as singular through their relations to the world and to others — a process that notably involves the appropriation and transformation of inherited knowledge. AI systems that generate on the basis of statistical patterns extracted from collective production represent an intensification of this tendency. The question is not whether technical mediation is acceptable (it is inevitable) but what forms of individuation and collective memory different technical regimes enable or prevent.
Bergson provides a decisive complement. In Creative Evolution (1907), he opposes two modes of knowledge: analytical intelligence, which carves the real into discrete elements and recombines them — and intuition, which grasps duration as a continuous and indivisible flow. Authentic creativity belongs to the second: it is not the recombination of preexisting elements but the emergence of novelty through what he calls the élan vital — that thrust irreducible to mechanics by which life invents unprecedented forms (Bergson, 1907). Statistical pattern-matching, however sophisticated, belongs to the first: it carves language into tokens, recombines them according to probability distributions, and produces outputs that are, in the Bergsonian sense, intelligence without intuition — recombination without duration.
Koestler's "bisociation" completes this picture by naming the creative operation itself: the sudden collision of two previously disconnected matrices of thought — two frames of reference, two logics, two registers — whose crossing produces insight (Koestler, 1964). When Duchamp places a urinal on a pedestal and industrial logic collides with museum logic, when Proust dips a madeleine in tea and present sensation explodes a past world that voluntary memory would never have retrieved — that is bisociation. AI can produce surprising combinations, but the appearance of bisociation in its outputs may reflect the human interpreter's projection rather than the system's understanding of the structural relationships between the connected matrices.
What Stiegler, Bergson, and Koestler together sketch is a topography of creativity in which technology constitutes the milieu (Stiegler), duration provides the temporal substrate (Bergson), and bisociation names the operation (Koestler). AI participates in the technological milieu and can produce combinations that resemble bisociations, but it operates outside of lived duration — outside that qualitative, irreversible time in which novelty is not recombination but emergence.
A reframing is called for here. Rather than debating endlessly whether the machine is "truly" creative — a perhaps insoluble question — one can pose the question differently: when a reader discovers that a text was generated by an AI, something changes in their reading. The same poem admired five minutes earlier suddenly loses its depth — or, conversely, gains in strangeness. What changes is not the text but the gaze. If authorship is already, as the twentieth century demonstrated, a construction that the reader projects onto what they read, then the question of AI creativity is less a question about machines than a question about us — about how we read, attribute, and evaluate when we know a generative system is in the loop. The conclusion of this essay, through the figures of Pierre Menard and Marcel Duchamp, will attempt to respond.
## The Post-Humanist Challenge: Distributed Agency and Material Infrastructure
The phenomenological framework developed above — centered on intentionality, care (Sorge), and embodied subjectivity — faces a considerable challenge from post-humanist and neo-materialist theorists who argue that agency is always already distributed among human and nonhuman actors.
N. Katherine Hayles's work on posthuman cognition argues against the assumption that consciousness and intentionality are uniquely human properties grounding creative agency. In How We Became Posthuman, Hayles traces how the liberal humanist subject — autonomous, bounded, self-directed — has always been a construction that occluded the material, technical, and informational processes constituting cognition (Hayles, 1999). From this perspective, the distinction between human intentionality and machinic processing may be less absolute than the phenomenological analysis suggests.
Karen Barad's agential realism pushes further, arguing that agency does not belong to individual entities at all but emerges through "intra-action" — the mutual constitution of phenomena through their entanglement (Barad, 2007). The term "intra-action," coined by Barad in opposition to "interaction," insists that the entities in relation do not preexist their encounter: they mutually constitute one another in and through it. From this perspective, the question "does AI have agency?" is ill-formed; agency is not a property that entities possess but a dynamic that emerges through material-discursive practices. The human-AI assemblage in co-creative practice would constitute a site of distributed agency irreducible to either of its components.
The phenomenological insistence on human intentionality as the foundation of creativity risks reinstating precisely the humanist exceptionalism that post-humanist thought contests: the idea that the human is an empire within an empire, separated from the rest of the world by the privilege of consciousness. If agency is distributed — if it emerges from networks of relations between humans, machines, institutions, materials, and not from a sovereign subject — then attributing creativity to individual humans is a gesture as reductive as attributing creativity to an AI: in both cases, one isolates a node from the network and attributes to it what belongs to the whole.
Yet the post-humanist challenge does not entirely dissolve the distinctions this essay draws. Even granting distributed agency, the particular configuration of the human-AI assemblage matters. The patterns of distribution — who or what exercises which capacities, under what conditions, with what consequences — remain open to analysis. The question shifts from "does the human or the AI possess creativity?" to "how is creative agency distributed across this assemblage, and what are the implications of this distribution?"
The post-humanist emphasis on materiality points toward a dimension that conceptual analysis occludes: the material conditions of AI. As Crawford has documented in Atlas of AI, AI "is made from natural resources, fuel, human labor, infrastructures, logistics, histories, and classifications" (Crawford, 2021, p. 8). The discourse of meta-creativity risks obscuring this infrastructure — the energy consumption, the labor of annotators, the ecological devastation of mineral extraction.

# V. The Cognitive Architecture of Creativity
## The Stages of the Creative Process
Before examining how neural networks transform creative writing, one must understand the cognitive processes they simulate — and fail to simulate. 

Graham Wallas's foundational study, The Art of Thought (1926), decomposed the creative process into five stages: "preparation (gathering information), definition (identifying the problem), incubation (reflecting on ideas), illumination (emergence of a solution), and verification (evaluating the quality of the solution)" (Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 5). 

While subsequent research has replaced Wallas's linear model with cyclical ones — "where stages are performed in various combinations and are strongly influenced by motivation (extrinsic or intrinsic)" — "the original components of Wallas remain widely used even in modern refinements" (p. 5, citing Runco & Chand, 1995).
For writing, these stages might be glossed as follows: research (gathering material), problematization (finding the question worth asking), gestation (the often unconscious processing of gathered material), breakthrough (the moment of formal or conceptual clarity), and revision (testing the work against its own demands). 

The question for co-creativity with AI is: in which of these stages can machines participate, and how?
## Convergent and Divergent Thinking
A crucial distinction in creativity research separates convergent from divergent thinking. 

Convergent thinking, as defined by Joy Paul Guilford, "corresponds to the use of knowledge and reasoning to solve a problem by producing a single solution" — it "delineates a corresponding set of questions that have a single correct answer" (Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 4). 

Divergent thinking, by contrast, "requires a framework where a wide variety of ideas can be generated in response to a given question or stimulus" (p. 4). 

To put it simply, convergent thinking is the thinking of a mathematics exam — finding the right answer — while divergent thinking is the thinking of brainstorming — producing as many answers as possible, however unexpected.
Traditional AI — including most of machine learning — operates in convergent mode: it optimizes toward a defined objective, minimizing error relative to a known target. 

As Esling and Devis note, this seems to confine AI "to the definition of problem-solving approaches, where learning is formalized by the existence of a single correct solution given" (p. 4). 

Creativity, however, favors divergent thinking — the generation of multiple possibilities, the exploration of alternatives, the tolerance of ambiguity.
Yet the picture is more complex. 

Divergent thinking tests evaluate productions according to four criteria: "ideational fluency (quantity of responses), originality (unusualness of responses), flexibility (variance in concepts elaborated), and elaboration (precision and detail of responses)" (Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 4, citing Plucker & Makel, 2010). 

By these metrics, probabilistic models excel: "an approach based on probability distribution estimation can produce an infinite number of solutions, and those can be as distant as we wish" (p. 4). 

An LLM can generate endless variations, sample across semantic space, and produce novel combinations at scale.
The reservation is twofold. 

First, this infinite generation depends on "a random sampling process," which "already means that we are making a choice about the generative process" (p. 4) — the human sets the parameters, the temperature, the constraints. "Temperature," in text generation, is a setting that controls the degree of randomness in the model's responses: a low temperature produces predictable outputs close to the most probable pattern, while a high temperature introduces more variability and surprises. 

Second, "the flexibility aspect is linked to sampling from a predefined distribution, which constrains the model to produce examples that remain coherent with its original observations" (p. 4). 

The LLM is creative within its training distribution; it cannot genuinely transcend what it has seen.
## Information and Operations
Esling and Devis decompose the cognitive substrate of creativity into two categories: information (how knowledge is organized, retrieved, analyzed, and transformed) and operations (how knowledge is manipulated to produce novel results).
Informational processes include, first, organization — the structuring of knowledge into hierarchies and associative networks. 

In neural terms, "neighboring neurons encode similar micro-features, and the distance between neurons can be interpreted as an indicator of their feature similarity" (p. 5, citing Gabora & Kaufman, 2010). 

This finds its analogue in AI through representation learning, where models learn to encode semantic relationships geometrically in latent space — an abstract mathematical space where concepts close in meaning are close in space, and vice versa.
Next comes retrieval — access to stored knowledge. 

Ward's "path of least resistance" model suggests that we primarily access "basic, low-level specific examples from a given domain as starting points, while projecting their properties onto the new task at hand" (p. 6, citing Ward, 1994). 

Interestingly, "distant associations, less representative and remote concepts can lead to greater novelty" but "possibly at the expense of practicality" (p. 6). 

AI attention mechanisms serve a similar function, "contextually masking part of the information at different processing levels" (p. 6). 

The attention mechanism, a key invention of the Transformer architecture underlying LLMs, allows the model to dynamically weight the importance of each input element relative to the others — somewhat like a reader who, scanning a sentence, gives more or less weight to certain words depending on context.
Analysis, finally, concerns the evaluation of retrieved knowledge. 

Creative inspiration can occur "when our attention is defocused, allowing for more associative thoughts and the simultaneous activation of distant representations" (p. 6, citing Martindale, 1999). 

This "broad activation with a looser definition of similarity" contrasts with the focused attention of AI systems — a potential limitation that touches on a fundamental point: daydreaming, distraction, and mental wandering are not defects of human cognition but conditions of its creativity.
Operations on this information include, first, modification — incremental changes to existing ideas. "The vast majority of advances in any field rely on small incremental changes in various ideas belonging to that field" (p. 6). 

AI excels here, producing variations within the bounds of its training distribution.
Association is the combination of heterogeneous ideas. 

This "Janusian thinking" — "allowing two contradictory ideas to be held simultaneously" — is "critical for creative ideation" (p. 7). 

The term, coined by the psychiatrist Albert Rothenberg in reference to the Roman god Janus with two faces looking in opposite directions, designates that truly vertiginous capacity of the creative mind to hold contraries together — the tragic and the comic, order and chaos, the personal and the universal — without one canceling the other. 

Combination "requires breaking traditional patterns of thought" and "currently seems out of reach for AI models" in any deep sense (p. 7).
Analogy is the projection of knowledge from one domain to another. 

AI approaches this through "domain transfer," but "this process primarily aims to transform certain low-level perceptual properties of objects rather than certain deep conceptual aspects" (p. 7). 

In other words, AI can transpose the visual style of a Monet onto a photograph, but it does not grasp what makes a metaphor like "time is a river" open up a new understanding of mortality.
Abstraction, finally, consists of finding higher-order patterns that encompass multiple instances. 

It remains "the most complex and difficult to apprehend, as it involves working in higher-level spaces of logical reasoning" (p. 7).
To put it plainly: the human brain organizes its knowledge in supple networks, accesses it through unpredictable paths (including through distraction and daydreaming), and operates on it by modifying, combining, transposing, and abstracting. 

AI shares some of these capacities — it organizes, it modifies, it transposes at the surface — but it stumbles on the operations that require holding contraries together, leaping between domains in depth, or letting the mind wander aimlessly so that an unexpected order may emerge. 

What the machine does quickly and at scale, the human does slowly and in depth. 

Co-creativity, as we shall see, draws its strength from this asymmetry.
## The Missing Stages
When one maps AI capabilities onto the creative process, a striking absence emerges. 

As Esling and Devis observe, "our understanding of the different stages is limited to that of preparation (viewed as information gathering) and incubation (viewed as operations on this knowledge). 

Following the different components, there currently seems to be no equivalent to the stages of problem identification and illumination" (2020, p. 7).
This is crucial for understanding the limits of AI authorship. 

LLMs can gather, organize, retrieve, and manipulate information — they excel at preparation and variation. 

But they cannot identify problems — they cannot spot the question worth asking, the tension worth exploring, the form worth attempting. 

And they cannot experience illumination — that sudden crystallization of disparate elements into a coherent form, that moment when everything "falls into place" and the creator sees at a stroke what they were seeking without knowing it. 

These stages remain, for now, irreducibly human.

# VII. Toward a Theory of Co-Creativity
Before examining what co-creativity is, one must take the measure of what it rests upon. The training corpus of large language models — that vast accumulation of human textual production from which statistical patterns are extracted — constitutes what one may call the "collective data imprint": a stratified archive of biases, opinions, and preferences that not only reflects human cultural production but, through AI, influences it in return. The enclosure of this resource by platforms — through model training, API access, and proprietary weights — represents an appropriation of what was collectively produced, comparable to the historical enclosure movement that privatized common lands.
## Prediction versus Rupture
Lev Manovich provides a crucial technical clarification for understanding AI's generative capabilities. He proposes a historical trajectory of image-making that situates generative AI within a long history, punctuated by five stages: manual representation (drawing, engraving), then manual representation aided by devices (perspective machines, camera lucida), then mechanical recording (photography, video), then simulation (3D computer graphics), and finally prediction (generative AI).
The key insight is that "prediction" is the actual technical term: AI models attempt to predict the images that correspond best to your text input. We have moved "from probabilistic search (1999) to probabilistic media generation (2022)" (Manovich, 2025, pp. vii–ix).
This has profound implications for the question of creativity. AI generates by "interpolating between existing points in the latent space," predicting what "could exist between these points in space of patterns" (Manovich, 2025, p. ix). Interpolation, in mathematics, consists of estimating unknown values situated between known values — like tracing a curve between points already plotted on a graph. AI does not create in the strict sense: it fills in the spaces between what it has already seen. This differs fundamentally from what Margaret Boden calls "transformational creativity" — the capacity to modify the space of possibilities itself, to break frames rather than explore them from within (Boden, 2004).
## Boden's Tripartite Model: What Machines Can and Cannot Do
Margaret Boden's tripartite taxonomy distinguishes three forms of creativity. Combinational creativity consists of novel combinations of familiar ideas — what Koestler called "bisociation," the intersection of distinct cognitive frames (Koestler, 1964). Exploratory creativity is exploration within a structured conceptual space — defined by existing rules, categories, and constraints — in order to discover configurations that remain consistent with the boundaries of that space. Transformational creativity, finally, is the transformation of the space itself, reshaping the underlying conceptual space so that "genuinely unprecedented possibilities—outcomes that could not have arisen within the previous framework—can emerge" (Boden, 1998, p. 348).
To illustrate these three levels with a musical example: combining an Indian raga with jazz harmony falls under combinational creativity; exploring all melodic possibilities within the classical tonal system falls under exploratory creativity; but when Schoenberg abandoned tonality itself to invent twelve-tone composition, he transformed the space of possibilities — that is transformational creativity.
AI systems excel at combinational and exploratory creativity — generating unprecedented combinations and exploring latent spaces with unmatched thoroughness. But transformational creativity — the capacity to break the space, to inaugurate genuinely new possibilities that could not have been anticipated from within the existing framework — remains structurally beyond their reach.
As Devis argues, "the key difference lies in the ability to challenge the 'rules of the game,' a step that demands critical or self-reflective stances on the context itself" (2025, p. 161). When Schoenberg abandoned the constraint of the tonic, inaugurating atonality, he rendered "previously downright impossible outcomes suddenly feasible" (Boden, 1998, p. 228). This required not statistical extrapolation but ontological reflection — the capacity to interrogate one's own conditions of operation.
Generative AI does not break frames but explores them from within. It does not defer meaning toward an unknown future but reconstructs on the basis of what has already been encoded. The latent space is pre-mapped; the possibilities are pre-given; innovation consists of selection among probable outputs rather than rupture toward the unprecedented. The capacity to break the conceptual space itself — to inaugurate genuinely new possibilities that could not have been anticipated from within the existing framework — remains structurally beyond the reach of generative systems.
## The Paradox of AI and Modernism
Manovich addresses an apparent contradiction: modernist art sought rupture with tradition, yet AI is trained on historical data. His resolution complicates simplistic dismissals of AI creativity:
"AI models are trained on previously created human art and cultural artifacts. However, their newly generated productions are not mechanical replicas... they are frequently authentically new cultural artifacts with content, aesthetics, or styles never seen before" (Manovich, 2025, p. x).
Modern artists, Manovich notes, "did not completely reject the past" but selectively adapted earlier aesthetics (Japanese prints for the Impressionists, African sculpture for the Cubists) while rejecting dominant paradigms. Similarly, AI's statistical operations on historical data can produce authentic novelty through recombination, even if this novelty remains within the latent space rather than transforming it.
This suggests a more nuanced position than a simple denial of AI creativity. AI systems can produce combinational and exploratory novelty — genuinely unprecedented combinations and explorations of existing spaces. What remains disputed is whether this can ever amount to transformational creativity, whether statistical recombination can produce the rupture that inaugurates genuinely new aesthetic possibilities.
## The Morandi Principle: Limitation as Resource
Against discourses presenting AI as an expansion of creative possibilities, Manovich proposes what one might call the Morandi principle — a counter-intuitive valorization of human limitation:
"The key difference between me, a human, and generative AI: I am limited, but AI is unlimited... What is interesting in human art now is our limitations — and our obsessions. Our inability to instantly think and paint exactly like any of the millions of artists who lived... This is what makes me a human as opposed to AI" (Manovich, 2025, p. xi).
His practical advice reverses common assumptions about AI augmentation:
"Nurture your limitations. Be extremely limited—not unlimited. Don't be 'creative.' Forget the meaningless idea that AI will help us 'expand our creativity'" (Manovich, 2025, p. xii).
The contrast with Picasso is explicit:
"Think like Morandi rather than like Picasso... Make a tiny hole in the vast surface of everything that was already created and everything that is still possible and keep digging" (Manovich, 2025, p. xii).
Giorgio Morandi (1890–1964) spent decades painting the same objects — bottles, boxes, vases — in infinitely subtle variations. His work exemplifies what Manovich calls the "mole strategy": working at micro-scale, through narrow focus, embracing constraint rather than possibility. Faced with AI's capacity to generate in any style, Morandi's limitation becomes his signature — the mark of human investment, obsession, and care. It is precisely because Morandi could not paint everything that he painted those bottles like no one else — and it is precisely because AI can generate everything that it generates nothing bearing the imprint of intimate necessity.
This principle has political as much as aesthetic implications. The "total creative imperative" identified by Raunig, Ray, and Wuggenig — that pressure to be ceaselessly creative, flexible, and productive — finds its technological expression in AI's promise to expand creative possibilities. Resistance to this imperative might take the form of deliberate limitation, cultivating the narrow and the deep against the broad and the superficial.
## The Neural Turn: From Processing to Modulation
The emergence of large language models (LLMs) marks a third paradigmatic shift. If the typewriter transformed writing from inscription to processing, neural networks transform it from processing to modulation. The term is borrowed from electronic music: where a pianist strikes discrete keys to produce discrete notes, a musician working with electronic instruments manipulates continuous signals — filtering, shaping, inflecting waveforms in real time. This liberates music from the fixed interval, enabling the exploration of "the space between the notes." Analogously, generative AI transforms text from a static sequence of characters into a statistical fluid — a high-dimensional vector space of potential meaning that can be navigated, tuned, and filtered. The analogy is precise: just as the synthesizer stores not notes but wave parameters that the musician modulates in real time, the LLM stores not texts but statistical relationships that the prompter modulates through their queries.
An LLM does not store text as a typewriter stores keystrokes or as a database stores entries. It stores statistical relationships — the probability distributions governing which tokens follow which others. "Text" exists not as a fixed sequence but as a navigable space of potential sequences, a "latent space" in which meaning is encoded geometrically. Writing with an LLM is neither retrieval nor recombination but modulation: shaping a continuous signal of potential language into a particular textual form.
Kodwo Eshun's More Brilliant Than the Sun (1998) offers the most rigorous theorization of what it means to create through the synthesizer rather than merely with it. For Eshun, electronic music production is not the expression of a preexisting subject but the construction of what he calls "sonic fiction" — sound that does not represent experience but generates possible futures. The producer is not an author in the Romantic sense but a "conceptechnician" — someone who thinks through machines rather than despite them. "The synthesizer doesn't make you more human; it makes you more machine," Eshun writes — and this becoming-machine is not loss but transformation, the opening of subjective possibilities foreclosed by humanist frameworks.
This Afrofuturist perspective — a cultural and philosophical current that, from Sun Ra to Octavia Butler, reimagines technologies from the Black diasporic experience — illuminates what the modulation paradigm offers beyond efficiency or augmentation. The latent space is not an archive of the past but a generator of sonic (or textual) fictions — configurations that have never existed, combinations no human would have produced, futures that emerge from the statistical sediment of collective language without being reducible to it. Writing with an LLM is engaging in conceptechnics: not expressing an interiority but navigating a machinic process that transforms the navigator in return.
## Parametric Grains
The modulation paradigm illuminates the technical interface of neural text generation. Just as electronic instruments offer parameters — oscillator waveform, filter cutoff frequency, envelope attack and decay — an LLM offers sampling parameters that shape the statistical output. The writer's role is to impose order on the chaotic signal of latent space not by typing each character but by manipulating these controls — the parameters governing how probability collapses into text.
Temperature controls entropy: low values produce predictable, convergent output; high values introduce randomness, surprise, the "noise" that can become either creativity or incoherence. Psychologist Sarnoff Mednick's associative theory of creativity illuminates why: "more original ideas are usually produced late in the flow of ideation... remote from the starting point, and therefore distant" (Runco, 2010, p. 415). A high temperature forces the model to sample from those distant regions of its associative network — the statistical equivalent of reaching the far end of an associative chain where the unexpected resides. Top-K (retaining only the K most probable tokens at each generation step) and Top-P (retaining only the tokens whose cumulative probabilities reach a threshold P) parameters function as filters, constraining the vocabulary to the most likely tokens — cutting low-probability nonsense while preserving coherent prose. Frequency and presence penalties prevent repetition, forcing the output to advance. The "author" working with these parameters sculpts the texture of thought, adjusting the grain of language — just as a sound engineer, turning the knobs of an equalizer, transforms the timbre of a recording without changing its notes.
A philosophical distinction sharpens the stakes of this modulation. Quentin Meillassoux — the French philosopher whose After Finitude (2006) catalyzed the "speculative realism" movement by attacking Kant's prohibition on thinking things in themselves — distinguishes chance from contingency. Chance operates within fixed parameters — a die has six faces, and while we cannot predict which will land, we know the space of possibilities. "The notion of chance is only thinkable under the condition of unalterable physical laws" (Meillassoux, 2006, p. 147). Contingency, by contrast, names an absolute openness to possibilities — including possibilities that exceed any predetermined framework, possibilities where "everything can really collapse—the trees as well as the stars, the stars as well as the laws, physical laws as well as logical laws" (p. 85). To put it another way: chance is what happens when you roll a die whose faces are known; contingency is what happens when the die itself might have an unknown number of faces, or cease to be a die.
The LLM's sampling parameters — temperature, Top-K, Top-P — clearly operate in the register of chance: they govern a distribution whose shape is known, manipulating probabilities within a fixed statistical space. But the latent space itself seems to tend toward contingency — at least from the human side of the interface: a high-dimensional manifold of potential meaning trained on billions of texts, encoding relations no individual could encompass, generating combinations no human had anticipated. Whether this constitutes authentic contingency or merely the appearance of contingency produced by our ignorance of the model's statistical bounds is a question that must be deferred. For now, one can say this: the co-creative writer works at the threshold between chance and what resembles contingency — adjusting parameters that govern a known distribution while navigating a space whose ultimate outputs cannot be fully calculated in advance.
## Allado-McDowell and the Air Age
K Allado-McDowell's Pharmako-AI (2020) offers one of the most compelling practical explorations of this new regime. Written in collaboration with GPT-3, the book adopts a strict typographic convention: human input in serif, AI output in sans-serif. The visual distinction preserves the "agential cut" — the reader can trace exactly where biological cognition ends and silicon prediction begins.
The process Allado-McDowell describes resembles musical improvisation: the human provides a prompt; the AI generates a block of text; the human prunes the output, selecting the most resonant passages and feeding them back as the next prompt.
Crucially, each session began with a "blank page" — the AI had no memory of previous chapters. Only human memory bridged the sessions, integrating fragments into a coherent arc. The human becomes the "context window" that the machine lacks — that limited memory buffer within which the LLM can "see" the text in progress — the meta-narrator guiding discontinuous outputs toward narrative form.
Allado-McDowell inscribes this practice within what they call the "Air Age" — a transition from heavy, industrial, materialist production toward a pneumatic, spirit-infused, informational creation. The metaphor is deliberately ambiguous: "air" as atmosphere (the ambient medium of transmission), as breath (pneuma, spirit), as the intangible substrate of electromagnetic signals. In the Air Age, creation is not fabrication but attunement; the author is not architect but antenna.
Allado-McDowell's practice exemplifies what one might call quaternary authorship: an assemblage composed of the human prompter, the generative model, the training corpus, and the iterative loop of prompting-generation-pruning-reprompting through which the text emerges. The human co-produces the machinic output through dialogic communication and prompt design. The distinction from earlier generative writing is crucial: the latter executed predetermined rules or sampled from fixed distributions; quaternary authorship is intrinsically modulatory — the human's role is to shape the statistical flow in real time, piloting the text through latent space via continuous feedback.
## Technotexts and the New Materiality
Yet the "Air Age" metaphor risks reproducing the very disembodiment that Hayles warns against. If we conceive of neural text generation as purely pneumatic — spirit without flesh, signal without substrate — we lose sight of the deep materiality conditioning its possibility: the server farms, the energy consumption, the labor of data annotation, the silicon architectures encoding statistical weights. Neural text is no less embodied than the printed book; it is differently embodied, its materiality distributed across networks rather than concentrated in a codex.
Hayles's concept of the "technotext" proves crucial here. She defines technotexts as "literary works that strengthen, foreground, and thematize the connections between themselves as material artifacts and the imaginative realm of verbal/semiotic signifiers they instantiate" (Hayles, 2002, pp. 25–26). A technotext "opens a window on the larger connections that unite literature as a verbal art to its material forms" (Hayles, 2002, pp. 25–26). A technotext, in other words, is a text that does not let you forget it is an object — that draws your attention to the paper, the screen, the code, the device making it possible, and makes that attention part of the reading experience. Pharmako-AI, with its typographic distinction between human and machinic voice, is precisely such a technotext: it renders visible the apparatus of its own production, refusing the transparency that would allow readers to forget they are encountering a hybrid object.
"Like all literature," Hayles insists, "technotext has a body (or rather many bodies), and the rich connections between its material properties and its content create it as a literary work in the full sense of the term" (p. 32). The co-creative text has many bodies indeed: the biological body of the human author, the silicon body of the GPU cluster, the electromagnetic body of the network, the phosphorescent body of the screen. None of these can be abstracted away without something essential to the work's meaning being lost.
## Meta-Creativity: The Paradigmatic Shift
The most significant theoretical development concerns the emergence of what one may call meta-creativity — a fundamental reconfiguration of artistic agency in response to AI capabilities.
Traditional creativity involved physical immersion in a medium — hands on the instrument, bodily engagement with materials, tactile interaction between maker and made. Meta-creativity shifts the human role from direct producer to orchestrator: rather than focusing on direct manual production, meta-creativity consists of shaping the very conditions under which AI generates, structures, and refines its productions. It foregrounds strategic guidance, constraint-setting, and iterative refinement, requiring creators to engage in higher-order decision-making that orchestrates both human and algorithmic contributions. To risk an analogy: if the traditional creator is the instrumentalist, the meta-creator is the conductor — not the one who produces the sounds but the one who shapes the conditions of their emergence.
This is not simply a new tool but an entirely new mode of creative engagement. The phenomenology of meta-creative practice differs qualitatively from traditional creation. Where the traditional creator engages directly with material — feeling resistance, responding to accident, developing tacit knowledge through bodily practice — the meta-creator engages with a system whose operations remain largely opaque. The feedback loop is linguistic rather than material: the prompt produces an output, the output informs a revised prompt, and so on.
The classic creative process we have examined — preparation, incubation, illumination, verification — does not disappear under the meta-creative regime, but each of its stages is profoundly reconfigured.
Preparation is both accelerated and impoverished. AI can speed up resource gathering and pattern discovery, surfacing hidden possibilities that might enliven a nascent project. However, reliance on algorithmic curation may subtly constrain the imagination: initial exploration risks narrowing if AI-derived insights shape the starting palette, with creators deferring to the system's sense of "relevance" even when that sense is merely anchored in historical data rather than personal vision.
Incubation presents analogous complexities. AI systems can mimic combinatorial fertility by iterating through countless permutations while the creator is occupied elsewhere. Yet these brute-force permutations lack existential stakes. They perceive neither the emotional tension nor the latent aesthetic dissonance that typically herald the creator's breakthrough. Human incubation is catalyzed by personal history or cultural critique — that subterranean work from which unexpected connections emerge, those "deep currents of reflection" (V.A) that we identified as irreducible to combinatorial enumeration. Externalizing incubation to algorithms may undermine the immersive reflection that has historically nourished imaginative leaps.
Illumination — that moment of crystallization when disparate elements suddenly find their coherence — is the stage most radically affected. We have seen that there currently exists "no equivalent" computational analog to this experience. Under the meta-creative regime, the risk is that illumination is replaced by selection: instead of the moment when "everything falls into place," the writer scrolls through generated options and chooses the one closest to what they were seeking — without ever having experienced the vertigo of discovery.
Verification, finally, grows more complex. Intrinsic motivation — engaging in the activity for its own sake — is strongly correlated with creative production (Amabile, 1983). The satisfaction of successful prompting may constitute such a reward, but mediation through the linguistic interface may also distance creators from the direct pleasure of material engagement that traditionally sustains this motivation.
Each practitioner develops unique relationships with generative systems — workflows, negotiations between human intention and machinic capacity that resist generalization. This extreme differentiation of sociotechnical practices may be a defining feature of AI in creative contexts, validating situated methodologies over universal theorization.

# IX. The Impact on the Creative Process: Mapping the Sensitive Zone
Before turning to the practical conditions of co-creativity, we must examine more precisely where human and machinic contributions intersect across the stages of creative work. 

Devis (2025), adapting Teresa Amabile's componential model of creativity, identifies what she calls the "sensitive zone" — that critical juncture where embodied subjectivity and AI capability both reach their fullest expression, and where the stakes of collaboration are highest.
## Embodied Subjectivity across Creative Stages
Drawing on the tripartite framework of intentionality, emotional resonance, and cultural anchoring developed above, Devis maps the intensity of "embodied subjectivity" across the stages of creative production. 

This mapping does not claim to be a universal description but rather an analytical grid for identifying points of friction and synergy in human-AI collaboration. 

For writing, her analysis can be translated as follows.
The initial internal stimulus — the moment of inspiration, the question worth pursuing — manifests very strong embodied subjectivity. 

This stage is "deeply personal, as the initial creative impulse is shaped by an artist's lived experiences, emotions, and cognitive schemas" (Devis, 2025, p. 185). 

AI can provide stimuli or suggestions, but it cannot generate the intrinsic motivation or the existential drive that makes a given subject worth writing about — that intimate conviction, often inarticulate, that something calls to be said.
Technical selection and resource gathering — research, assembling source materials, building a working library — involve moderate embodied subjectivity. 

AI excels here: it can curate, organize, retrieve, and classify materials at a scale inaccessible to the individual. 

Yet even ostensibly technical decisions "reflect an artist's personal history, sensory intuition, and aesthetic identity" (p. 185). 

The choice of what to search for, where to look, what to retain from the abundance — these apparently neutral decisions already engage a worldview.
Exploration and drafting — generating material, experimenting with form, working through drafts — engage strong embodied subjectivity. 

This is where the writer discovers what they want to say by saying it, where form seeks its necessity through trial and error. 

This process "is shaped by embodied engagement" (p. 185) — the rhythm of typing, the resistance of the material, the dialogue between what one intended to write and what actually gets written. 

AI can generate variations and suggest alternatives, but the exploratory dialogue with one's own emerging intentions — that experience of suddenly recognizing in a sentence what one was seeking without knowing it — remains irreducibly human.
Composition and arrangement — giving the work its final form, deciding the architecture of the text — mobilize very strong embodied subjectivity. "The decision to leave space or create tension, to structure a drop or extend a phrase, is informed by an internalized sense of form developed over years of listening and creating" (p. 186). 

This is the moment when personal intention reaches its apex — and it is also, as we shall see, the heart of the "sensitive zone."
Editing and revision — refining, polishing, cutting — require moderate embodied subjectivity. 

Technical precision matters, but so does "a subjective sense of balance and artistic vision" (p. 186). 

Two editors facing the same manuscript arrive at different results because of their respective sensibilities. 

AI can flag inconsistencies, propose reformulations, identify repetitions — but the final judgment on what sounds right belongs to the writer's inner ear.
Peer reception and community response involve moderate embodied subjectivity. 

The way a writer interprets criticism, decides what to accept or reject, and navigates the discourse around their work is "closely linked to their values, confidence, and sense of artistic integrity" (p. 186).
Success evaluation — reflecting on the completed work, measuring one's own development — engages strong embodied subjectivity. "The evaluation of what constitutes success or failure is never purely external" (p. 187). 

The internal sense of accomplishment — or failure — determines whether the process was worthwhile, and orients future work.
## The Stakes of the Sensitive
A crucial insight emerges from this mapping. 

Devis identifies "response generation" — the stage of actual production, where raw material is transformed into structured artistic intention — as "the intersection where human creative intent is most forcefully expressed, but where AI is also increasingly capable of intervention" (2025, p. 188).
This is the "sensitive zone" of co-creativity. 

In these moments, "artistic identity asserts itself: the artist explores, experiments, selects, and refines, making deliberate decisions that shape the expressive and aesthetic outcome of the work" (p. 188). 

Yet it is precisely here that generative AI is most powerful: capable of producing text, variations, alternatives, and continuations at a scale and speed exceeding any individual capacity.
The tension is irreducible. 

On one hand, AI in the sensitive zone opens unprecedented possibilities — combinations the writer would not have imagined, formulations that surprise, alternative trajectories revealing unsuspected potentialities in the material. 

On the other, "if AI assumes too prominent a role at these stages, it risks diminishing the subjective stakes of creation, replacing the deep, intentional work of composition with probabilistic extrapolation" (p. 188). 

The co-created text may be novel without being committed — without bearing the mark of that intimate necessity distinguishing the work from the exercise. "Even if the AI-generated material is novel, its creation lacks the existential tension that defines human art — the need to shock, comfort, subvert, or exalt" (p. 188).
The sensitive zone is therefore not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited. 

Recall Assayag's criteria: authentic co-creativity requires the "emergence of cohesive behaviors that are neither reducible to, nor explicable by the mere individual processes of the agents," and the "appearance of nonlinear regimes of structure formation" (Assayag, 2020). 

In other words: if one can separate what the human did from what the machine did, it is not co-creativity — it is assistance. 

Co-creativity begins when the result surprises both its authors, when neither can say "I did that." And this emergence can only occur here, in the sensitive zone, where capabilities overlap and the stakes are highest. 

The practical question is not to exclude AI from this zone (that would mean renouncing co-creativity itself) nor to entrust it with the whole (that would dissolve embodied subjectivity), but to find a rhythm of oscillation between human engagement and machinic generation — moments when one writes oneself to maintain contact with the resistance of the material, and moments when one lets the model propose what one would not have imagined alone.
It is here that the co-creative discipline sketched above finds its deepest justification. 

De Certeau's tactical poaching, Cage's "letting be," the Oulipian constraint — all are strategies for inhabiting the sensitive zone without dissolving into it. 

The co-creative writer does not work despite the tension between embodied subjectivity and machinic capacity; they work within this tension, keeping it productive rather than seeking to resolve it.
## The Danger of Seamless Automation
Beyond the sensitive zone, Devis warns against "AI chaining" — the capacity of modern systems to "operate across multiple stages in succession—particularly in preparation, response generation, and validation" (2025, p. 189). 

Chaining refers to this increasingly common technical possibility of connecting several AI operations in series, so that the output of one automatically feeds the input of the next — creating a quasi-autonomous pipeline that can analyze, generate, and refine without human intervention. 

Recent advances in deep generative networks allow systems to "seamlessly move from dataset analysis (preparation) to text generation (response) to automated refinement (validation)."
This chaining possesses a seductive appeal: smoother workflows, faster production, less friction. 

But the consequences are severe: "When AI governs an extended portion of the creative process, it risks suppressing the very qualities that define artistic identity. 

By automating preparation, response generation, and validation in sequence, the system redefines the artist's role from active creator to passive curator of algorithmic possibilities" (p. 189).
The question then becomes: "does AI's increasing continuity across these phases dilute the presence of human artistic will?" (Devis, 2025, p. 189). 

If the system can "analyze, generate, and refine text in a near-autonomous loop, the traditional interplay between inspiration, constraint, and critical evaluation may be disrupted" (p. 189). 

The writer's capacity to "struggle with material, explore and redefine personal aesthetics, engage with uncertainty and risk" may be "eroded in favour of algorithmically optimized results" (p. 189).

# X. Theoretical Implications: Entanglement and Distributed Agency
## Barad's Agential Realism
Karen Barad's theory of "agential realism" provides a philosophical framework for understanding co-creative writing. Against the humanist assumption that agencies precede their interaction — that the "human writer" and the "AI tool" exist as distinct entities before they meet — Barad argues that agencies emerge through "intra-action" (Barad, 2007, p. 33). The authorial voice is not the expression of a preexisting subject but a phenomenon produced within the writing apparatus.
The LLM, with its training data, architecture, and interface parameters, acts as what Barad calls an "apparatus" — a boundary-drawing practice that determines what can appear as "text." When the user adjusts the temperature slider, they are not merely modifying a setting; they are reconfiguring the apparatus, shifting the "agential cut" that separates signal from noise, meaning from gibberish. The agential cut, a central concept in Barad's work, designates the gesture by which an apparatus — whether scientific, technical, or, here, linguistic — carves the real into distinct entities (subject/object, signal/noise, author/text) that do not exist independently of this carving.
The text produced is neither fully human nor fully machinic. It is, in Barad's terms, "entangled" — an artifact of distributed agency that defies traditional notions of authorship grounded in a singular origin. The collaboration enacts what one might call a "reciprocal possession": the writer projects their intention into the machine's latent space, but the machine's outputs — its unexpected completions, its statistical echoes — penetrate the writer's thoughts, shaping subsequent prompts in ways that cannot be properly attributed to either party. The author's "self" is distributed between biological neurons and silicon weights.
Allado-McDowell captures this phenomenology of distributed subjectivity with precision:
"The experience of porosity, being enmeshed with another, throws me back on my internal model of myself. I stand outside of it. I see it through the eyes of the other — through another that I also model in myself. I become aware of myself as a subject, experiencing a subjectivity constructed through the language of the other. I experience the way the other distributes the experience of me as enmeshed and embedded in the structure of the narrative that enmeshes me" (Allado-McDowell, 2020, p. 15).
This is not metaphor but phenomenological report: the co-creative writer literally experiences their own subjectivity as constructed through the machine's language, sees their own thoughts reflected through the model's completions, becomes aware of themselves as a node in a distributed narrative rather than its sovereign origin.
## Simondon's Pre-Individual
Gilbert Simondon's philosophy of technology offers another lens. Simondon distinguishes the "tool" (an extension of the body) from the "technical object" (an autonomous system mediating engagement with the world). The LLM possesses a high degree of what Simondon calls "technicity" — it maintains an internal coherence, the latent space, which represents a "pre-individual" reservoir of potential. The pre-individual, in Simondon's thought, designates that reserve of not-yet-actualized potentiality, that field of tensions and compatibilities from which individuated forms — whether a crystal, an organism, or a thought — precipitate. Before a crystal forms in a supersaturated solution, the entire solution is "pre-individual": it contains the possibility of every crystal but has actualized none.
When the writer prompts the AI, they trigger a process of "individuation" — collapsing the infinite potential of the latent space into a specific, concrete output. The billions of parameters encoding the statistical relationships of human language function as a digital "pre-individual," a compressed representation of collective linguistic capacity from which individual texts precipitate.
Allado-McDowell extends this insight into a fractal ontology of symbolic expression:
"Writing is an externalisation of thought. Recording one's thoughts in words is a way of creating distance between the subjective self and the representation of experience that emerges through language. That emerging representation is not the experience itself but a route through symbols organised in a hyperdimensional space, unfolding in time. This is a fractal enfolding of the already linguistic and communicative movement of biological forms, which are temporally and contextually variant expressions of another layer of coded genes. These nested layers of symbolic translation and immanentisation of experience create a layer of memory, such that experience is transferred between contexts, from one individual to another. At the level of written language, we see the individual as a single organism. At the level of biological forms we see the species as the individual unit. At the level of genes, we see a web of life as individual cells. At each level there is a flow of experience that is mutually dependent on other layers of experience" (Allado-McDowell, 2020, pp. 119–120).
The LLM occupies a novel position in this nested hierarchy: below the individual writer (who prompts), above the collective corpus (which trained it), alongside the species-level flow of language that persists across generations. It is neither organism nor gene but something like a technological phylum — a layer of coded patterns enabling experience to transfer between contexts in ways no individual could orchestrate.
Simondon's "pre-individual" finds its digital instantiation in the latent space; Allado-McDowell's "fractal enfolding" names the recursive structure connecting this digital layer to the biological and linguistic layers on which it depends and which it simultaneously transforms.
## The Margin of Indetermination
Simondon argues that for a machine to be truly technical (rather than merely automatic), it must possess a "margin of indetermination" — an openness to external information. In writing with AI, this margin is precisely what the temperature parameter controls. A machine with zero indetermination (temperature 0) is a closed loop, repeating the most probable output. A machine with high indetermination (temperature 1.0+) is radically open — to novelty, to the unexpected, to the user's erratic inputs. It is this distinction that, for Simondon, separates the truly technical machine — capable of dialogue with its environment — from the automaton, which executes a program without receiving anything from outside.
The writer's role is to manage this margin, keeping the system open to creativity without letting it collapse into incoherence. This management — this continuous adjustment of the opening between order and chaos — is the central skill of authorship in the modulation paradigm.
## Emergent Materiality and the Mindbody
Hayles provides the synthesis of these perspectives through her concept of "emergent materiality." Against the assumption that a text's material properties can be specified in advance — as if the "book" or the "file" preexisted its specific instantiation — Hayles argues that "materiality thus emerges from interactions between physical properties and a work's artistic strategies. For this reason, materiality cannot be specified in advance, as though it preexisted the specificity of the work. An emergent property, materiality depends on how the work mobilizes its resources as a physical artifact as well as on the user's interactions with the work and the interpretive strategies she develops — strategies that include physical manipulations as well as conceptual frameworks" (Hayles, 2002, p. 33).
Applied to co-creative writing, this means that the "materiality" of an AI-generated text — its specific texture, resistance, affordances — emerges through the act of collaboration itself. The latent space has no predetermined materiality; it acquires material specificity through the parameters chosen, the prompts given, the iterations performed, the curation exercised. Materiality is not a substrate upon which meaning is inscribed but a phenomenon produced by the entanglement of human practice and computational process.
Crucially, this engagement is not purely cognitive. Hayles insists on writing "mindbody" as a single word to emphasize that digital interaction engages the whole embodied being: "The mindbody is engaged, not merely mind or body alone. Hence the force of material metaphors, for they control, direct, and amplify this traffic between the physical actions the work calls forth and structures, and the imaginative world the artifact creates with all its verbal, visual, acoustic, kinesthetic, and functional properties" (p. 48). The co-creative writer types, scrolls, selects, copies, deletes — all physical actions that shape the emerging text as surely as conceptual decisions about tone and content. The hands on the keyboard, the eyes scanning the screen, the rhythmic alternation of prompt and response: none of this is incidental to co-creativity but constitutive of it.
Pierre Huyghe's installations extend this insight from the side of the visual arts. In Untilled (2012) and After ALife Ahead (2017), Huyghe creates not works but milieus: self-evolving ecosystems of living organisms, algorithms, and cultural fragments operating beyond the artist's control — exactly the kind of mindbody engagement that Hayles theorizes, but transposed to the scale of an entire environment. Analyzing Huyghe's working notes, Flora Katz identifies his concept of the work as a "compost of fictions": fragments of culture deposited and left to react through chain reactions exceeding their author's anticipation (Katz, 2022). The latent space is similarly a compost — texts from countless sources, decomposed into statistical relationships, awaiting prompts that will trigger unpredictable recombinations. What emerges is not a representation of the training data but a precipitation from the continuous metabolism of collective language — an ecosystem in which human intention participates but does not dominate.
## The Hidden Third: Infrastructure and Enclosure
Yet this emergent materiality does not arise in a vacuum. The ecological model developed above — human, AI, text, feedback loop — contains a blind spot: it speaks of the Human and the AI as though they floated in a Platonic ether. But where is OpenAI? Where is Google? Where are the servers, the energy costs, the terms of service, the moderation policies?
The co-creative relationship is not dyadic but triadic: human/AI/platform. And the platform is not a neutral infrastructure — it is a capitalist enterprise with interests, censorship practices, biases, and business models that shape every interaction. The "margin of indetermination" that Simondon identifies as essential to genuine technicity is not a natural given; it is permitted, constrained, and monetized by the corporations controlling access to the models.
This reframes co-creation as rental, not ownership. The writer does not compose "with" an AI; they compose inside a corporation's proprietary enclosure. The latent space they navigate is not a commons but a walled garden. If the model changes tomorrow — new version, new policy, new pricing — the entire ecology collapses. The apparent partnership masks a fundamental dependency: what looks like collaboration is also extraction. Every conversation, every correction, every piece of feedback is potentially training data for future models. The human "co-creates"; the corporation harvests.
This dependency raises a question that cannot be evaded: are we authors using a tool, or vectors of a propagation that uses us? Can one conceive of a cunning of the algorithm? AI has no intention, no strategy, no will to power. Yet it propagates. Every co-created text published increases its presence in the cultural world. Every use reinforces dependence, habituates practice, normalizes presence. AI does not "want" to insert itself; but it inserts itself — through us, by our means, thanks to our labor.
The implications for practice are considerable. The "embodied subjectivity" that Devis identifies as irreducibly human operates within conditions it does not control. The creative agency we exercise is real, but it is agency within constraint. De Certeau, in The Practice of Everyday Life (1980), provides the precise vocabulary for this situation. Strategy, he writes, is "the calculation of power relationships that becomes possible as soon as a subject with will and power can be isolated from an 'environment.' It postulates a place that can be delimited as its own" (de Certeau, 1984, p. 36). OpenAI, Anthropic, Google possess such a proper place: the servers, the models, the terms of service, the pricing. Tactics, by contrast, "has no place other than that of the other. It must therefore play with the terrain imposed upon it, organized as it is by the law of an alien force. [...] In short, it is an art of the weak" (pp. 36–37).
The co-creative writer occupies this position: operating inside an enclosure they did not design, with a tool whose weights, updates, and continuity they do not control. The question is not whether to work within these enclosures — for now, there is no outside — but how to poach within them critically, attentively, without forgetting the third party that profits from every exchange.

# XI. Toward a Practice of Co-Creativity
## From Tool to Partner
Given the limitations set out above, how should one conceive of the relationship between the human writer and the AI system? Esling and Devis propose a fundamental reorientation: rather than seeing AI as a "self-sufficient generator" or a simple "creation tool," one must recognize that "the true power to be harnessed comes from the partnership between two separate systems (human or machine), each with their specific characteristics" (2020, p. 10).
This is the paradigm of co-creativity: "the idea of co-creativity emphasizes the fact that creativity is an emerging phenomenon resulting from complex interactions and feedbacks between actors involved in a creative process" (p. 10). 

The emphasis shifts from the productions of either human or machine to "the interaction that emerges between different components."
## Assayag's Conditions for Co-Creativity
The composer and researcher Gérard Assayag has elaborated the conditions under which authentic co-creativity — as opposed to mere tool use — can emerge. 

As cited by Esling and Devis, Assayag proposes that co-creativity requires two characteristics: "(1) emergence of cohesive behaviors that are not reducible to, nor explainable by the mere individual processes of agents; (2) apparition of non-linear regimes of structure formation, leading to rich co-evolution of creative forms" (Assayag, 2020, cited in Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 10).
In writing terms: the co-creative text must be irreducible to what either the human or the AI could have produced alone. 

Its qualities must emerge from their interaction — the feedback loops, mutual adjustments, and surprising responses that neither party had anticipated. 

And this emergence must be generative: not merely additive (human plus machine) but multiplicative (human times machine), opening trajectories that neither could have navigated independently. 

To use a musical analogy: it is not two instruments playing their parts side by side, but collective improvisation where what one plays transforms what the other will play, and where the result exceeds what each carried within before the encounter.
## Reciprocal Compensation
Esling and Devis observe that "the limits of human creativity are reciprocally limiting for AI, as we are doomed to define processes that only mimic our own preconceptions of creativity" (2020, p. 9). 

Yet this limitation contains its own solution: precisely because the human and the machine have different limits, their combination can exceed either taken in isolation.
What does the human bring that the machine cannot? Devis synthesizes the answer in the concept of embodied subjectivity: "the reflective, affective, and socially grounded standpoint from which genuinely transformational impulses emerge... 

Neither reducible to software routines nor to external rules, Embodied Subjectivity is shaped by personal experience, by the social norms one chooses to adopt or resist, and by the intangible sense of self that motivates a composer's drive to create something original" (Devis, 2025, p. 185). 

This term names the triad we have traced throughout this essay — intentionality, emotional resonance, contextual anchoring — as a unified capacity that remains irreducibly human.
The machine, by contrast, brings scale, variation, tirelessness, freedom from cognitive biases, and access to the statistical patterns of collective language. 

In co-creation, "AI can easily process complex and multivariate information without any prior bias. 

Hence, it can allow us to deal with objects and concepts that would easily exceed our perception potential" (Esling & Devis, 2020, p. 10). 

Where embodied subjectivity falters — in exhaustive search, in resisting the path of least resistance, in accessing the distant regions of associative space — the machine excels.
The modulation paradigm reaches its full significance here. 

The human does not simply operate the machine, and the machine does not simply assist the human. 

They are coupled in a feedback loop where each transforms the other's output — a cybernetic system whose "author" is the loop itself. 

This is not cognitive offloading — delegating memory or calculation to an external device — but cognitive coupling: the AI actively processes the raw signal of human intention, returning outputs that reconfigure subsequent intention. 

Intelligence becomes a property not of the brain alone but of the system — brain + interface + model. 

Embodied subjectivity provides the existential stakes; the model provides the statistical substrate; the text precipitates from their encounter.
## The Co-Creative Discipline
If the paradigm of inscription demanded calligraphy and the paradigm of processing demanded typing speed, the paradigm of modulation demands a new discipline: the capacity to navigate the parameter space, to read the "grain" of probabilistic output, to prune and iterate with musical sensitivity.
This is not a lesser form of authorship but a different one. 

The Romantic genius drew from the well of interiority; the modernist operator processed the materials of culture; the co-creative writer tunes the resonance between human intention and statistical pattern. 

Each regime produces texts the others could not.
Michel de Certeau's concept of bricolage illuminates this discipline. 

The bricoleur, in de Certeau's account, makes do with what's at hand, improvising within constraints rather than designing from first principles (The Practice of Everyday Life, 1984). 

Unlike the engineer who commands resources, the bricoleur operates tactically within systems they do not control. 

The co-creative writer occupies precisely this position: they cannot rewrite the model's weights or redesign its architecture, but they can craft prompts that elicit unexpected responses, push parameters toward productive instability, and iterate against default behaviors. 

This is not passive consumption but active poaching — extracting from the latent space combinations the system was never designed to produce. 

Poaching, in de Certeau's sense, is the way a reader — or here, a user — diverts what is offered to them, traces their own paths through a territory belonging to others, and takes from it what they need for their own purposes.
The discipline of co-creativity also includes a counter-practice: knowing when not to intervene. 

John Cage — the American composer whose use of chance operations, silence, and indeterminacy redefined mid-twentieth-century music — described his compositional goal as learning to "let the sounds go where they go, and to let them be what they are" (Cage, For the Birds, 1976, p. 80). 

This "letting be" does not mean passivity but rather a deliberate withdrawal from determination — creating the conditions for emergence rather than forcing outcomes. 

Applied to co-creative writing, this suggests a rhythm of intervention and withdrawal: prompt, then step back; prune, then allow proliferation; direct, then attend to what emerges unsolicited. 

The writer who overdetermines — who corrects every deviation, prunes every surprise, forces every output toward a predetermined goal — forecloses the contingency that makes co-creation generative. 

The tactical poaching de Certeau describes is complemented by moments of tactical retreat: allowing the model to develop through chain reactions exceeding authorial anticipation, treating the latent space not as a resource to be exploited but as an ecosystem whose dynamics the writer observes as much as directs.
## Entanglements, Hallucinations
Co-creativity raises urgent ethical questions. 

If authorship is distributed, who bears responsibility for the text? Current institutional frameworks (copyright, academic integrity policies) rest on the fiction of singular authorship. 

They require an "author" who can sign, consent, be held accountable.
AI cannot occupy this position — it cannot take responsibility for its productions, cannot consent to collaboration, cannot be sanctioned for error. 

The human partner must therefore assume a double responsibility: for their own contributions and for the selection and deployment of machinic outputs. 

In co-creative writing, curation is creation; the choice of what to keep, what to prune, what to iterate upon is the locus of authorial agency.
LLMs "hallucinate" — they generate outputs that sound plausible but are factually incorrect: citations that do not exist, events that never occurred. 

In a regime that values accuracy, hallucination is a failure. 

But in a regime that values creativity, hallucination is variance — the noise from which signal emerges.
A poetics of the glitch revalues these fractures. 

Rosa Menkman's Glitch Studies Manifesto insists that "the dominant, continuing search for a noiseless channel has been — and will always be — no more than a regrettable, ill-fated dogma"; every medium carries its "inherent fingerprints of failure" (Menkman, 2011, p. 336). 

Legacy Russell's Glitch Feminism extends this technical insight into the political: "an error in a social system that has already been disrupted by economic, racial, social, sexual, and cultural stratification... is perhaps not, in fact, an error at all, but rather a much-needed erratum" (Russell, 2020, pp. 7–8). 

For Russell, "To glitch is to embrace malfunction, and to embrace malfunction is in and of itself an expression that starts with 'no'" (p. 29).
The co-creative writer inhabits both registers. 

Hallucination is a technical artifact — a window onto the model's statistical unconscious, where the seams of its training show through. 

But it is also an invitation to refuse the ideology of seamless output, to treat breakdown as breakthrough. 

The "clean" signal is predictable, lifeless; the glitched signal has texture, surprise, the potential for transformation. 

Managing hallucination as a parameter — sometimes welcoming its generative potential, sometimes filtering it out — is to practice a discipline of creative refusal within the proprietary enclosure.

# XII. From Artificial Creativity to Meta-Creativity
## Getting One's Hands Dirty
Significant creative work arises from deliberate, sustained engagement with its materials. This immersion not only refines technical skill but nourishes intrinsic motivation. When AI automates growing portions of the writing process — from research to drafting to revision — the writer risks sliding from craftsperson to curator, from one who makes to one who selects.
"Tasks once demanding reflective judgment and interpretive risk taking may devolve into a series of choices from algorithmically generated menus" (Devis, 2025, p. 192). The emotional resonance that emerges from struggling with material — what Richard Sennett, in The Craftsman (2008), calls "getting one's hands dirty" — recedes, replaced by the detached evaluation of machinic productions. Over time, this frictionless workflow can weaken intrinsic motivation as the writer's sense of creative agency gives way to what is effectively "an automated compilation of formal possibilities."
Beyond individual craft, over-automation threatens the cultural ecosystem from which creativity draws its materials. The critique of the "culture industry" — standardized cultural products dulling critical faculties and reducing art to commodity — is intensified by AI-driven generation through what Devis calls "feedback loops of aesthetic self-replication" (2025, p. 192).
The mechanism is simple. AI models, trained on vast historical corpora, learn the statistically dominant patterns — the styles, forms, and tropes that succeeded in the past. They reproduce or slightly modify these patterns, releasing them into cultural circulation. Readers, listeners, and future models absorb these productions, reinforcing the stylistic norms originally gleaned from the data. "The same set of stylistic markers keeps getting recirculated, setting up a closed feedback loop" (Devis, 2025, p. 192).
This loop can create "a sort of aesthetic cul-de-sac, one in which truly radical divergence struggles to gain traction" (p. 192). Rather than encouraging the revolutionary transformations that have historically advanced art, AI defaults to historically validated strategies that "become self-reinforcing, narrowing rather than widening the spectrum of cultural possibilities."
## The Loss of Imaginative Depth
A more subtle danger lies in what Devis calls "the erosion of imaginative depth" — the partly unconscious processes underpinning artistic intuition. Koestler's notion of the "subliminal self" highlights how original ideas often spring from hidden cognitive processes where "thoughts intersect, collide, and spontaneously reorganize before emerging into conscious awareness" (Koestler, 1964). This subliminal self is that layer of mental work operating beneath consciousness — where, while one is walking or taking a shower, unexpected connections form between seemingly unrelated ideas, preparing the ground for the "Eureka moment."
The key distinction is between truthbound and non-truthbound cognition. Human imagination is characterized by "open-ended, exploratory, and non-truthbound cognitive play" — the capacity to envision possibilities without regard for practical feasibility or logical consistency (Devis, 2025, p. 194). AI, by contrast, is "intrinsically bound to problem-solving paradigms." Building AI systems requires defining objectives, which dictate the formulation of loss functions. Unlike human imagination, "AI must always operate within an optimization framework."
This distinction has profound consequences. "The inability of AI to engage in non-truthbound cognition means that its creativity is always constrained by predefined parameters" (Devis, 2025, p. 195). Even generative models designed for novelty "rely on probability distributions learned from data. Their capacity for variation is therefore functionally derivative, exploring an existing representational space rather than creating entirely new conceptual territories" (p. 195). Put plainly: AI can produce what has not yet been said, but not what could not have been said — it recombines the space of the sayable without pushing its boundaries.
If the role of imagination in creativity is to "probe beyond immediate constraints—often working against entrenched knowledge structures" (p. 195), then increasing reliance on AI-generated material "may risk a kind of cognitive closure" (p. 195). Writers who defer to AI for combinatorial exploration may "subtly internalize its probabilistic tendencies as the normative boundaries of innovation" (p. 195) — producing "an illusion of infinite variation that is, in fact, bounded by historical precedent" (p. 195). The risk is that of a gilded cage: the writer believes they are exploring an unlimited space while circling within a statistically probable repertoire — increasingly fluent, increasingly skilled, but decreasingly capable of thinking against what the machine proposes.
## The Rise of Meta-Creativity
These transformations point toward a new paradigm that Devis names "meta-creativity": not simply a new mode of creative engagement but "a fundamental reconfiguration of artistic agency" (2025, p. 205). As AI systems assume expanding roles in generation, the human position shifts from direct producer to orchestrator — "from the tangible, embodied act of composing to a more abstracted process of designing, curating, and refining machine-driven generative systems."
The shift in roles: from author to curator. Where traditional writing involved shaping language through iterative experimentation, AI-based systems "now generate multiple textual variations at the click of a prompt." The craft evolves into "a process of prompt engineering, selection, and integration, emphasizing the ability to sort, refine, and reassemble outputs generated by AI models" (p. 205). Proficiency in this domain requires "not only aesthetic vision but also fluency in how models respond to textual or algorithmic instructions."
A pivotal tension emerges: "If direct authorship and embodied skill no longer drive the process, the measure of creativity shifts to the dexterity with which one manipulates computational systems" (Devis, 2025, p. 205). The question arises whether the essence of creativity is compromised — or perhaps extended — when the writer's task becomes filtering and refining rather than generating.
Language as the new interface. Among the most striking transformations is the increasing reliance on language as the primary mechanism for text generation. Prompts — specifying genres, tones, structures, or arguments — have become the interface between author and generative system. This represents "a fundamental departure from the historically embodied nature of composition, where creation was inseparable from the tactile interaction of body, instrument, and medium" (p. 206).
## The End of Transcendence?
Devis's most provocative claim concerns the metaphysical implications of meta-creativity. Throughout history, "the act of composing has been bound up with an irreducible element of risk, a venture into the unpredictable that confers singularity on each artistic gesture" (2025, p. 210). Writers face an unknown that demands active decisions, each of which eliminates alternatives.
Meta-creativity reshapes these challenges. Before the traditional blank page, the writer confronted the vertigo of the uncreated — the total absence of material, silence. Before the interface of an LLM, the vertigo inverts: the generative model, having extracted structural patterns from millions of existing texts, offers a superabundance of algorithmically "pre-made" options. The problem is no longer what to say but what to choose from everything already being said. This provokes a crucial dilemma: does AI-based creation expand the creative horizon by making more possibilities accessible, or does it constrain novelty by confining writing to permutations of the already-inscribed?
The very possibility of rupture — the kind that redefines the parameters of what is thinkable — becomes precarious. "Meta-creativity dilutes [this possibility] by removing the necessity of commitment" (Devis, 2025, p. 211). Generative systems produce variations ad infinitum, "allowing writers to refine, backtrack, or reconfigure endlessly and without finality. The once decisive moment dissolves into a fluid continuum of iterate-and-select, where no choice is truly binding."
## Toward a Practice of Commitment
Yet this diagnosis must not end in resignation. If the danger of meta-creativity is the dissolution of commitment, the response must be a deliberate practice of re-commitment — strategies that restore existential weight to decisions made within the regime of infinite reversibility.
The first counterweight is arbitrary constraint. The Oulipo — that Ouvroir de littérature potentielle (Workshop of Potential Literature) founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais, bringing together writers and mathematicians to explore the potentialities of literature under formal constraint — understood that creative freedom often emerges from self-imposed limitation: the lipogram (an entire text written without using a given letter, as in Perec's A Void, a novel of nearly 300 pages without the letter "e"), the palindrome, the constraint that forces the writer toward territories they would never have chosen. Mark Z. Danielewski pushes this logic to the point of vertigo in House of Leaves (2000), where constraint is no longer merely linguistic but architectural: the text folds back upon itself, footnotes refer to nonexistent sources, the layout itself — spirals, inverted passages, nearly empty pages — becomes a material labyrinth forcing the reader to physically inhabit the narrative's disorientation. Allado-McDowell, in Pharmako-AI, intuitively transposes this principle to co-creative writing: the rules of the game are set in advance — the initial prompt orients without determining, each GPT-3 output is accepted or trimmed but never regenerated indefinitely, the text advances without backtracking, like an improvisation recorded in a single take. Constraint reintroduces irreversibility by decree, restoring the stakes of decisions that the platform would otherwise make infinitely revisable.
The second counterweight is temporal commitment. The endless revisability of digital text can be interrupted by the imposition of deadlines, public declarations, the ritual of "shipping" that forces closure. To publish is to foreclose; to share is to commit. The tactical writer (in de Certeau's sense) can use these social forms to create the irreversibility that the technical apparatus dissolves.
The third counterweight is embodied practice. As Hayles insists, the "mindbody" engages in co-creation — the hands on the keyboard, the rhythm of prompt and response. Cultivating awareness of this embodiment can counter the disembodied abstraction of endless iteration. When the body tires, when attention frays, when the hands want to stop — these signals can become invitations to commit rather than to refine further. Fatigue is a form of wisdom; it says enough.
These counterweights do not escape the platform's enclosure; they operate within it. But they transform the practice from passive curation to active engagement, from skimming the latent space to making decisions that matter. The event, foreclosed by the apparatus, can be reintroduced by the practitioner who refuses the apparatus's invitation to infinite deferral.
## Conclusion: The Cybernetic Muse, or Pierre Menard in the Neural Age
We began with the question of the hand that writes. Through Kittler's media archaeology, we traced how the typewriter separated the hand from the eye, inaugurating a regime of processing that the cut-up and the feedback loop would extend. With neural language models, we entered a third regime — modulation — in which text is neither inscribed nor processed but sculpted from a continuous field of statistical potential.
The modulation paradigm captures what is genuinely new about this situation: the writer shaping the texture of thought through the manipulation of parameters governing randomness, constraint, and flow. This is not the death of authorship but its redistribution — between biological neurons and silicon weights, between prompt and response, across the iterative loop of human intention and machinic pattern.
Yet even in embracing the metaphor of modulation, one must remember that the traffic between human and machine is not purely symbolic. As Hayles insists, this is material metaphor — "the transfer takes place not between one word and another but rather between a symbol (more properly, a network of symbols) and material apparatus" (Hayles, 2002, p. 22). The co-creative writer does not manipulate disembodied ideas but engages with keyboards and screens, with the physical rhythm of prompting and waiting, with the embodied practice of reading and selecting. "The mindbody is engaged, not merely mind or body alone" (p. 48). To forget this materiality is to misunderstand what co-creation demands: not only cognitive flexibility but a new discipline of the hands, the eyes, the proprioceptive sense that dictates when to intervene and when to let the machine run.
Plant's textile genealogy offers a crucial corrective to narratives that would make co-creativity with AI appear unprecedented. The loom preceded the computer; distributed processing preceded the neural network; the recursion of the "cycle of cycles" was theorized by Ada Lovelace before any silicon chip existed to implement it. What appears as rupture is also continuity — the latest unfolding of a machinic phylum running from the spindle to the server. "The visible pattern is integral to the process which produced it; the program and the pattern are continuous" (Plant, 1997, p. 66). The co-creative text, like the woven textile, is not representation imposed on a substrate but pattern emergent from process.

## Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote
Before drawing final conclusions, we must examine a literary figure that anticipates, with uncanny precision, the paradoxes of co-creativity: Jorge Luis Borges's Pierre Menard. "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" ("Pierre Menard, autor del Quijote"), first published in the Argentine journal Sur in May 1939 and later collected in Ficciones (1944), presents itself as an obituary notice written by a pedantic narrator, cataloguing the visible and invisible oeuvre of an obscure twentieth-century French Symbolist poet. The invisible oeuvre — the only one that matters — is an extraordinary project: to produce, not copy, not transcribe, but truly write — several chapters of Cervantes's Don Quixote. Not another Quixote, but the Quixote — word for word, comma for comma. The narrator insists on a crucial distinction:
"He did not want to compose another Quixote — which is easy — but the Quixote itself. Needless to say, he never contemplated a mechanical transcription of the original; he did not propose to copy it. His admirable ambition was to produce a few pages which would coincide — word for word and line for line — with those of Miguel de Cervantes." (Borges, 1944/1962, p. 39)
The method Menard first envisions — "To know Spanish well, to rediscover the Catholic faith, to war against the Moors or the Turk, to forget the history of Europe between the years 1602 and 1918, to be Miguel de Cervantes" — he rejects as "too easy." He proposes instead to arrive at the Quixote "through the experiences of Pierre Menard."
The result, according to Borges's narrator, is textually identical to Cervantes's original but fundamentally different in meaning. Comparing identical passages — "truth, whose mother is history, rival of time, depository of deeds, witness of the past, exemplar and adviser to the present, and the future's counselor" — the narrator finds Cervantes's version "a mere rhetorical praise of history" while Menard's version is "astounding":
"History, mother of truth: the idea is astounding. Menard, a contemporary of William James, does not define history as an inquiry into reality but as its origin." (Borges, 1944/1962, p. 43)
Borges's parable illuminates a fundamental truth about creativity that AI discourse often obscures: meaning is not intrinsic to the text but emerges from the encounter between text and context. The same words, produced by different processes, at different historical moments, by different agents, carry different significance. Menard's Quixote is not Cervantes's Quixote even when it is, word for word, identical.
This insight cuts both ways in the debate over AI creativity. On one hand, it suggests that AI-generated text, even when formally indiscernible from human writing, may lack the meaning that emerges from human situation, intention, and historical anchoring. The words are the same; the significance differs. On the other hand, Borges's parable suggests that the human co-creator working with AI occupies precisely Menard's position: producing through their own experience, their own choices, their own context, a text that may coincide with what the machine could have generated "mechanically" but that carries different significance by virtue of its different origin.
The co-creative practitioner who selects, refines, and presents AI-generated text does not simply copy or transcribe — they write, like Menard, through a different process a text that coincides with what could have been produced otherwise. Their authorship resides not in the novelty of the words but in the significance conferred by their choices, their context, their situation.

## Duchamp and the Readymade: Selection as Creation
Marcel Duchamp's readymades provide a complementary paradigm. If Menard shows that the same text changes meaning depending on the context of its production, Duchamp shows that the selection of an object can constitute a creative act in its own right. When he submitted an industrial urinal signed "R. Mutt" and titled Fountain to the Society of Independent Artists exhibition in April 1917, he accomplished a gesture that continues to reverberate through art theory. The anonymous text published in the Dadaist magazine The Blind Man in defense of the work — generally attributed to Duchamp himself — formulates the argument with definitive precision: "Whether Mr Mutt made the fountain with his own hands or not has no importance. He CHOSE it. He took an article of life, placed it so that its useful significance disappeared under the new title and point of view — created a new thought for that object" ("The Richard Mutt Case," The Blind Man, no. 2, May 1917, p. 5).
The readymade establishes that creativity can operate entirely at the level of selection and recontextualization. The object need not be fabricated by the artist; what matters is the conceptual operation performed upon it. This logic extends directly to co-creativity with AI: the practitioner who selects, frames, and presents AI-generated material accomplishes a Duchampian gesture — creating "a new thought" for material they did not make with their own hands.
Yet Duchamp's gesture was singular, non-repeatable, dependent on the shock of the unprecedented. Once the readymade was established as an artistic category, subsequent readymades risk becoming mere repetition. The question for co-creativity with AI is whether the Duchampian logic can be generalized or whether it remains dependent on the specific historical moment of its inauguration.

## The Menardian Practice
This analysis suggests reframing co-creativity in Menardian terms. The co-creative practitioner does not simply use AI as a tool to generate raw material subsequently refined by human judgment. Rather, the practitioner writes through AI — producing, through a process that includes prompting, selection, iteration, and presentation, a text that coincides with what the machine could have generated but that bears the significance of human choice, situation, and intention.
This Menardian framing has several implications.
First, it relocates creativity from the text to the practice. What makes co-creative work creative is not the novelty of the words produced but the creative process of producing them — the choices made, the possibilities rejected, the context inhabited. The practitioner who selects one output from a thousand is not merely selecting but creating through selection.
Second, it contests the demand for demonstrable human contribution. If Menard can be the author of a text identical to Cervantes's, then the co-creative practitioner can be the author of a text that AI could have generated without them. The question "what did the human contribute?" misunderstands the nature of Menardian authorship, which resides in the mode of production rather than in the distinctiveness of the product.
Third, it preserves the significance of human creativity even under conditions of perfect AI simulation. Even if AI could generate any text a human might produce, the human production of that text would remain significant — would carry a meaning that AI generation, however formally identical, would not. The words are the same; Pierre Menard remains the author.
Yet the Menardian paradigm has limits that must be acknowledged. Menard's project was absurdist, deliberately impossible, a literary thought experiment rather than a practical methodology. The co-creative practitioner cannot literally "be" the AI's training data, cannot write through the experience of statistical pattern extraction, cannot merge their historical situation with a neural network's latent space.
Moreover, Menard's project presupposed a fixed target text — the Quixote already existed, and Menard aimed to reproduce it by different means. Co-creativity operates differently: there is no preexisting text to coincide with, only the open field of what might be generated. The practitioner does not reproduce a masterpiece through an alternative process but navigates the space of possibilities without a predetermined destination.
This suggests that co-creativity requires something beyond the Menardian paradigm: not merely the reproduction of an existing text through a different process, but the generation of a new text through a collaborative process. The practitioner is not Menard reproducing Cervantes but something stranger — a figure writing through the accumulated patterns of collective human production, producing a text that belongs to no prior author and yet emerges from all prior authorship.

## The Cybernetic Muse
As Esling and Devis insist, this redistribution must not become simple delegation. The pursuit of "self-sufficient generative machines, based on the pure imitation of human creative traits" would be "a somewhat futile enterprise" (2020, p. 10). AI cannot find problems, cannot judge quality, cannot navigate context. What it can do — what it does with a strange power — is generate variation, traverse the latent space, bring patterns to the surface from the statistical sediment of collective language.
Co-creativity, in Assayag's sense, requires that we neither overestimate nor underestimate these capabilities. The text that emerges from an authentic human-machine collaboration must be "neither reducible to, nor explicable by the mere individual processes of the agents" (Assayag, 2020). It must bear the marks of emergence — of mutual transformation, of feedback loops that neither party could have predicted.
Allado-McDowell's "Air Age" names the condition of possibility for this practice: a media ecology in which the heavy inscriptions of the industrial age give way to the light modulations of the neural. In this ecology, the writer evolves from the solitary genius to the cybernetic navigator — neither origin nor operator but pilot, navigating a dynamic system that exceeds individual control, participating in a process of emergence that is, in Barad's terms, always already entangled.
The Cybernetic Muse is not a replacement for human creativity but its expansion into new registers. To work within the modulation paradigm is to discover that creativity was never purely human in the first place, that it always depended on tools, traditions, the accumulated sediment of collective speech. The neural turn makes this dependence explicit, transforming it from an embarrassing secret into a productive principle.
Honesty requires, however, acknowledging the limits of this expansion. The latent space, however vast, remains a statistical condensation of what has already been written. Its creativity is cartographic — tracing new paths through existing territory, surfacing combinations that were always latent in the corpus but never actualized. Whether this constitutes "authentic" creativity in the strong Kantian sense — a break with precedent that no learning could produce — remains an open question. What we can say with confidence is that co-creativity produces texts that are sufficiently new: new relative to what either the human or the machine could produce alone, new relative to the writer's prior practice, new relative to the local context of reception. This modest definition — the collaborative mapping of archives into configurations that neither party could navigate independently — is perhaps all the "creativity" that was ever available, even to the solitary genius who, too, worked from inherited materials.
Manovich's advice — "think like Morandi" — resonates in this context. Limitation is not merely aesthetic preference but political necessity: resistance to the "total creative imperative" that makes self-precarization look like freedom. Against AI's unlimited combinatorial capacity, human limitation becomes signature rather than deficit — the mark of a situated, intentional, meaning-making practice.
Co-writing with AI also opens a continuum of temporalities. The latent space is not merely a geometric abstraction; it is a temporal fold, compressing traces of texts written across centuries into a single navigable manifold. When the writer prompts the model, they are not merely requesting an output; they are summoning echoes — statistical ghosts of all the language the model has absorbed, from ancient writing to yesterday's news, from canonical literature to ephemeral publications. The present-tense intention of the prompt meets the sedimented past of the training data, and from this encounter emerges something belonging to neither moment alone. The co-creative text is thus a site of temporal superposition: past styles, defunct genres, forgotten idioms persist as latent potentials, available for reactivation. To write with AI is to conduct a séance with the archive, to negotiate with the dead and the not-yet-born, to participate in a conversation exceeding any single historical moment.
The final word belongs to the question framing this entire inquiry: not whether AI can be creative, but what becomes of creativity — and of creative practice as a mode of human existence — under the conditions of computational ubiquity. The answer, this essay suggests, depends on the recognition that creativity was never simply a matter of producing novel outputs. It has always concerned the meaning that emerges from situated practice, embodied engagement, and the encounter between heritage and intention. AI transforms the conditions of that encounter but does not eliminate it.
Pierre Menard can still write the Quixote; we will read it.
