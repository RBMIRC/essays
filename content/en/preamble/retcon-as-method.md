## 1. Observation: The Retcon Is an Ancient Cultural Operation

In *Retcon Game: Retroactive Continuity and the Hyperlinking of America* (2017), Andrew J. Friedenthal offers the first academic study devoted to retroactive continuity as a cultural operation. His thesis is twofold. First, a definition: the retcon is "a narrative process wherein the creator(s) and/or producer(s) of a fictional narrative/world deliberately alter the history of that narrative/world such that, going forward, future stories reflect this new history, completely ignoring the old as if it had never happened"¹. Second, a genealogy: the retcon, as a practice, is as old as narration itself. Oral cultures were unaware of it as a concept but practiced it structurally — every bard, every storyteller modified the epics at each performance, not by error but by necessity: "Originality consists not in the introduction of new materials but in fitting the traditional materials effectively into each individual, unique situation and/or audience"², as Walter Ong already wrote in describing how oral narratives function. The Bible, in the passage from the Old to the New Testament, accomplishes a massive retcon: the vengeful God is reconfigured into a God of love, without the earlier text being erased. The rabbinical tradition of *Midrash* pushes this logic further: one does not modify the Torah; one adds interpretive narratives that retroactively reconfigure its meaning — collectively, cumulatively, within a sacred continuity one does not claim to replace. Midrash is, structurally, of the same order as contemporary fandom — a collective reconfiguration of a canon it reveres without owning. Conan Doyle retcons Sherlock Holmes by resurrecting him after having killed him — under pressure from his readers, inaugurating the Sherlockian "Great Game" in which generations of fans treat the stories as historical documents and "reconstruct" what "really happened." This is the first modern fandom — and the first collective practice of retcon.

But the very expression "retroactive continuity" has an origin that predates comic books. As Joshua Clover notes, the term appears for the first time in 1973 — the year of Black-Scholes and the financial turn — in a work by theologian E. Frank Tupper on Wolfhart Pannenberg. In a Hegelian key, Pannenberg argues that history is the unfolding of God's self-revelation, and that this unfolding can only be understood by reading *backwards* from its endpoint. Tupper formulates: "Pannenberg's conception of retroactive continuity ultimately means that history flows fundamentally from the future into the past, that the future is not basically a product of the past"³. The concept then migrates to comic books with Roy Thomas and *All-Star Squadron* in the 1980s. This migration — from theology to popular culture — is itself a retcon: the same term, displaced into a new context, produces a meaning its inventor had not foreseen.

What Friedenthal shows is that the retcon is not an accident of the cultural industry: it is a *survival technique for long narratives*. When a narrative continuity becomes too complex, too contradictory, too laden with accumulated strata, the retcon intervenes to *reconfigure it without destroying it*. It is thus distinguished from the *reboot* (which erases and starts over), the *remake* (which reproduces with fidelity to an original), the *remaster* (which improves without modifying), the *spin-off* (which extracts without retroactivity), and the *remix* (which recomposes in synchrony). What makes the retcon specific rests on three characteristics: it is *endogenous* (it operates from within the continuity, not from outside), it is *retroactive* (it modifies the meaning of what precedes, not only what follows), and it maintains the *continuum* (it does not break the series; it extends it by transforming it).

Friedenthal distinguishes three degrees: *reinterpretation* (which changes the reading of a prior event without materially modifying it), *reinscription* (which fixes this new reading as canonical), and *revision* (which materially alters the text itself — as when Lucas re-edited *Star Wars*). These three operations share a single gesture: "recontextualizing prior narrative elements"⁴.

But Friedenthal also raises the political question. The inaugural reference for the retcon as an operation of power is Orwell's *1984*⁵: the Ministry of Truth practices the retcon as a technique of totalitarian domination — erasing the past to control the present. Friedenthal reverses this association: in an open society, the retcon is on the contrary a tool for *historiographic emancipation*. Revising history is not falsifying it — it is opening it to narratives that had been excluded from it. "Shedding light on the stories of underprivileged, underrepresented Americans is not a means of undercutting the importance of [the Founding Fathers], but rather a way of understanding that everybody's story, everybody's life, matters"⁶.

Friedenthal situates the retcon within narratives — oral cultures, the Bible, comic books, television series. But the retroactive reconfiguration of meaning is not specific to narration. Art history has its own operations, with their own names and specific logics — and it is by distinguishing them from the retcon that one best understands what the retcon does and does not do. Three terms, in particular, designate three ways of intervening on an existing image: *damnatio memoriae* (erasure), *overpainting* (covering over), *pentimento* (repentance). Each shares with the retcon the temporal structure — the present acts on the past. None shares with it the position — collective, transparent, non-sovereign.

Roman *damnatio memoriae* is the oldest of these operations — and the most brutal. A face is scraped from a fresco, an imperial bust is recarved so the effigy takes the features of a successor. The *Severan Tondo* (c. 200 CE) is the textbook case. This family portrait painted on round wood shows Emperor Septimius Severus, his wife Julia Domna, and their two sons — but where the face of Geta, the younger son, should appear, there is only a brown stain. After Geta's assassination by his brother Caracalla in 211, the face was erased from all official representations: portraits, inscriptions, coins. The void left in the tondo *is* the message — and that message says: this man never existed. Damnatio memoriae shares with the retcon its temporal structure: the present modifies the meaning of the past. But it differs radically in its gesture: it *erases*. The retcon does not delete — it reconfigures by adding. Damnatio memoriae is closer to Orwellian revisionism than to the retcon in Friedenthal's sense.

Post-Tridentine overpainting works differently. After the Council of Trent (1545–1563), the Church undertakes to purge religious art of what it deems indecent. When it orders the nudity of the figures in Michelangelo's *Last Judgment* to be covered, it is Daniele da Volterra who takes on the task — and who will earn the nickname *Il Braghettone*, the breeches-maker. Volterra paints veils and drapery over the fresco, and the original theological meaning (nudity as a state of purity before God, bodies nude because at the Judgment there is nothing left to hide) is annulled in favor of a moralistic reading. But the work is not destroyed — it is *overpainted*, and the prior version remains materially present beneath the layer of censorship. Modern restorations have in fact removed some of these veils, revealing the bodies Volterra had dressed. Overpainting covers without erasing — this is what distinguishes it from damnatio memoriae. But what distinguishes it from the retcon is the absence of transparency: one overpaints in silence, one does not show the seam, one presents the result as if it had always been there.

The *pentimento* — the repentance — is something else again. The painter modifies his own canvas in the course of work: he covers over the position of an arm, shifts a face, removes an entire figure. For centuries, these modifications remain invisible. Then infrared reflectography reveals them — and suddenly, beneath Velázquez's *Portrait of Philip IV*, another Philip IV appears, slightly offset; beneath Vermeer's *Girl with a Pearl Earring*, a green curtain the painter ultimately removed. These buried versions are folded into the materiality of the object and continue to inform the surface: the pentimento is the gesture closest to the retcon in its structure. But the pentimento remains *individual* (it is the artist alone who repents) and most often *involuntarily revealed* (it is contemporary technique that brings it to light, not a decision by the author). The retcon is *collective* and *deliberately assumed*. This difference is not a detail — it is political.

Finally, at the scale of the discipline itself, art historiography produces retroactive reconfigurations that illuminate the retcon without being reducible to it. The invention of the "Renaissance" by Michelet and Burckhardt in the nineteenth century is a rupture narrative imposed *a posteriori* that reorganizes the perception of thousands of works⁷. Foster shows the same structure for the avant-garde: it is the neo-avant-garde that *produces* the historical avant-garde as origin⁸. These historiographic operations are *structurally analogous* to the retcon — the present reconfigures the meaning of the past. But they are carried out by historians, with the methods and conventions of art history. The retcon, as practiced here, is neither historiography nor damnatio memoriae nor pentimento — it borrows from each of these operations a dimension (temporal retroactivity, the materiality of traces, the reconfiguration of meaning) while distinguishing itself from them through its own milieu: the collective, the protocol, the transparency of the gesture.

Each of these operations thus has its name and its specificity. But they all share a structural proximity: the retroactive modification of the meaning of prior material. It is this proximity that makes the retcon thinkable — and it is the difference from each of them that makes it necessary as a distinct concept.

Two misunderstandings, in particular, must be addressed. The first is the *palimpsest*. The palimpsest — the scraped and rewritten parchment, become an omnipresent metaphor since Genette (*Palimpsests*, 1982) — is the word one uses when one wants to name the coexistence of versions within a single object. But the palimpsest and the retcon do not work in the same way. The palimpsest presupposes superimposed *layers* whose stratigraphy can be reconstituted: it is an archaeological, vertical model, where one digs toward a presumably more authentic original. The retcon does not dig — it *reconfigures*. Meaning does not circulate from bottom to top (from deep layers to the surface) but from present to past: it is the new episode that modifies the meaning of the old one. The difference is political as much as formal: the palimpsest implies a hierarchy of layers (the original is more "true"), where the retcon refuses this hierarchy. The pentimento is the point where the distinction is most visible: what is buried beneath the layer of paint is not an "original" more authentic than the surface — it is a version that continues to inform the form without dominating it. The palimpsest model would say: one must dig to recover the truth. The retcon says: all versions coexist, and it is this coexistence that is the object.

The second misunderstanding is *revisionism*. Friedenthal has shown how Orwell associated the rewriting of the past with totalitarian control. But the accusation of revisionism is also the classic weapon of those who refuse any revision of history — Bancel, Blanchard, and Vergès showing that French colonial revisionism betrays less a historiographic divergence than a denial, a difficulty in thinking the colonial past in all its contradictions⁹, the proponents of a fixed national narrative accusing critical historians of falsification. As historian James Horton, cited by James Grossman, recalled: revision is the historian's very work — "Would you go to a heart surgeon who isn't reading revisionist medicine?"¹⁰

The transparency of the protocol is a first criterion of distinction: revisionism erases its traces; the retcon exhibits its own. But this is not sufficient. The decisive criterion is the *position* from which the operation is exercised. Damnatio memoriae is a legal act ordered by the emperor. The erasure of Nikolai Yezhov from official photographs after his execution in 1940 is a state falsification coupled with physical liquidation. Post-Tridentine overpainting is commissioned by the Church. These are, in each case, operations of *subtraction* exercised from a position of *sovereignty*: a power that amputates, covers, or substitutes — and that does not account for its gesture. The retcon does the opposite. It *adds* to a continuity that it *extends*: prior episodes remain; their meaning is reconfigured but their existence is not denied. And it operates without sovereignty, without mandate, without institution, without monopoly: showing its seams. This is not a nuance; it is a difference in kind. The image, in these histories, is not merely reconfigured — it is condemned.

The retcon arises precisely where this sovereignty is *absent*. And this is where its origin in comic books is not an anecdotal detail but a structural datum. The retcon appears in a *multi-authorial* system — dozens of writers succeed one another on the same title, none controls the entirety of the continuity, and reconfigurations are negotiated between authors and readers, in letters pages and conventions. Roy Thomas, who first publishes the term in *All-Star Squadron* in the 1980s, dialogues with his fandom line by line. Fan fiction pushes this logic further still: communities without any legal or authorial mandate seize a continuity — sometimes against the wishes of rights holders — and reconfigure it from a marginal, tactical, non-sovereign position. The retcon is, from its very origin, the operation of those who do *not* have power over a continuity and who reconfigure it anyway — not by decree but by cunning, by love of the material, by collective necessity.

### Retroactive Statues

When the Black Lives Matter movement, after the murder of George Floyd in May 2020, produces a global wave of challenges to commemorative monuments, the gesture takes varied forms: the statue of slave trader Edward Colston is thrown into Bristol harbor by protesters in June 2020; in the United States, dozens of Confederate monuments are removed by municipalities or torn down by crowds; in Belgium, statues of Leopold II are splashed with red paint. The gesture is not sovereign (these are protesters, not the state — even if the state, in some cases, ultimately accompanies the movement) and it shares with the retcon the refusal of the official narrative. But it does not *reconfigure* the continuity — it *breaks* it. The activists do not claim that Colston never existed; on the contrary, they hyper-visibilize the slaving past in order to strip the statue of its honorific status in the present. The empty pedestal is an act of moral reassessment, not of narrative reconfiguration. It is an operation of *judgment* — a popular tribunal of public space — where the retcon is an operation of *complexification*.

The work of artist Iván Argote on public monuments illuminates this gap from within art. The Colombian artist does not topple statues — he reconfigures them *in situ*. In *Etcétera* (2018), he covers the statue of conquistador Francisco de Orellana in Bogotá with mirrors: the colonizer disappears visually, replaced by the reflection of the surrounding park — the land that colonization despoiled returns to occupy the conqueror's symbolic space. In the *Turistas*, he dresses statues of colonial figures in ponchos — the indigenous and peasant garment recontextualizes the colonizer as a stranger on his own host land. In *Wild Flowers, Augustus* (2024), he reproduces fragments of imperial statues in bronze — a torso, a hip, hands — hollows them out and transforms them into planters where local wild plants grow, anticipating a future in which nature reclaims the vestiges of power. None of these operations destroys the monument: each *reconfigures* it by adding a layer that displaces its meaning. Argote is closer to the retcon than to toppling — but his gesture remains that of a singular artist intervening in public space. The retcon, for its part, is collective, archival, and operates over a long duration. It says something else: this archive is here, and we are going to make it signify otherwise — without destroying it, without hiding it, without throwing it into the harbor.

The retcon is thus defined by two joint criteria. The *transparency of the protocol*: it does not claim to tell what really happened; it shows what it does with the past and how it does it. And the *absence of sovereignty*: it operates from a marginal, tactical, collective position — not by decree but by cunning and by companionship with the material. Revisionism *reduces* the complexity of the archive; the retcon *increases* it. The border is porous, and the retcon must patrol it constantly. But it exists.

It is in this space — between the pentimento and the damnatio memoriae, between assumed reconfiguration and concealed erasure — that what I have been doing for several years with the archives of Black Mountain College is situated. The stakes are not the same as those of decolonial practices or struggles for the visibility of marginalized identities — the BMC remains an object of the Western tradition, produced by predominantly white and educated artists and pedagogues. But the political context in which this research developed — from the mid-2010s onward, the commons movement, the crisis of cultural institutions, the debates over access to archives and the ownership of the past — has profoundly influenced its methodology.

To work on Black Mountain College today is to do so in awareness of the narratives that have been minored there, the voices that were not heard, and the forms of collectivity that were not documented. The retcon, here, does not repair a historical injustice — it opens a space where hitherto secondary narratives can take their place in the continuity, without asking permission from those who hold the canonical narrative. The BMC is a terrain where the dimensions of the retcon converge: an object massively reconfigured by fifty years of institutional mythographies since Martin Duberman's founding monograph in 1972¹¹, with a spectacular acceleration in the 2000s — the exhibitions at the Hammer Museum, the Hamburger Bahnhof, the ICA Boston, the residency programs that proliferate, the publications that succeed one another — and an archive dispersed across dozens of repositories, which makes research complex. What comes out of this work is not art, not research, not pedagogy. It is something produced *between* the three. *Retcon Black Mountain* is about making possible this meta-narrative within the continuum of the college's history, while offering those who take it up the possibility of appropriating the utopian narratives that run through it — to inscribe themselves fully, collectively, and to make that inscription the condition for a critical discourse on what "renewal" can mean today.

## 2. The Phenomenology of the Gesture: Entering the Movement

In *Relationscapes: Movement, Art, Philosophy* (2009), Erin Manning develops an ontology of relational movement starting from dance — tango, improvisation, the work of William Forsythe. What she describes between two dancing bodies is, almost word for word, what the retcon does between a subject (individual or collective) and History. Manning did not think the retcon — but her phenomenology provides its language¹².

### 2.1 Entering the Interval

Manning posits that all movement is *preaccelerated*: it has no zero moment; it is always already underway. "Preaccelerated because there can be no beginning or end to movement. Movement is one with the world, not body/world, but body-worlding"¹³. And it does not unfold *within* a pre-existing time — it *makes* time¹⁴. The retcon enters History as a dancer enters movement: it does not *begin* it; it *intervenes* in it. An archive like that of the BMC does not await it in a state of rest — it is already in motion, already reconfigured by decades of exegeses, exhibitions, mythographies. Every historian, every curator, every artist who has touched it has left *stubborn facts* there — intensities that persist and condition the movements that follow.

Relational movement resides in neither of the two dancers: it occurs in the *interval* between them. "The interval is not mine alone to lead"¹⁵ — to lead is "initiating an opening, entering the gap, then following her response"¹⁶. The time of the interval is a "future-past that is prolonged at the interface of the becoming-actual of the virtual"¹⁷ — a future anterior: what *will have been*. The retcon inhabits this temporality. It resides neither in the subject-researcher nor in the archive-object. It occurs *between* the two, in a space that neither controls. And Manning specifies: "The displacement is not the event. The event is the composition of space-time that qualitatively alters the topological dimensions of our sensing bodies in movement"¹⁸. For the retcon: relocating an archival document is not the event. The event is the topological reconfiguration that this relocation produces.

The most directly operative concept is *the elasticity of the almost*: "The next movement has not yet come, the past movement is passing. No step has been taken, and yet in this elastic the microperception of every possible step can almost be felt"¹⁶. This is the space where the retcon works: between the archival document and the new form that the workshop will produce, there is an elastic moment where the archive is no longer simply what it was and not yet what it will become. Manning insists: elasticity does not last indefinitely. If one lets it dissipate, "the elastic point of the movement will evaporate and only the step will be felt"¹⁹ — one falls back into reproduction. The retcon must *sense* the moment when elasticity crystallizes into form. What is at stake is not a knowledge about the archive but a *perception* of what it is becoming.

### 2.2 The Refrain: Repeating the Future

Reactivation does not happen once. It *repeats* — and it is in repetition that the retcon constitutes itself as territory. Deleuze and Guattari, in *A Thousand Plateaus*, define the ritornello as the operation by which "one draws a circle around the fragile and uncertain center, one organizes a limited space"²⁰ — this is the child singing in the dark to reassure herself, the gesture that, by repeating, creates a home. Manning takes up this concept: "This shape-shifting of the interval is a rhythm that returns as a refrain, emphasizing that which populates the in-between"²¹. The structure of the refrain is not that of resemblance — it is that of *difference*²². Manning condenses: "Moving is repeating the future: dancing the not-yet"²³.

The British artist Tris Vonna-Michell (born 1982), nominated for the Turner Prize in 2014, gives this structure a precise artistic form. In *Finding Chopin* (2005–), he sets out in search of sound poet Henri Chopin from a laconic indication left by his father — and from this seed a project is born that he has been *re-performing* for nearly twenty years: monologues delivered at a speed that borders on unintelligibility, accompanied by 35mm slides and found objects, where the narrative branches, drifts, and reconfigures at each iteration. From one performance to the next, images from his own archive constantly reappear in new configurations — the same material, but never the same form. What persists from one iteration to the next is not a text but a *territory*: a circle traced around a fragile center, which holds precisely because it is never identical to itself.

The retcon operates like a choir trying to agree on a refrain they have heard, reproduced from memory, and to arrange it for the harmonic reality of the group. Each person has heard something slightly different — no one possesses the original version — and it is in the collective negotiation between these partial memories that a form emerges, recognizable but transformed. Years of returns to the same terrain, the same archives, the same fundamental question — each return is a repetition with difference, because one must adapt to contingent realities: the group has changed, new documents have appeared, a technology has imposed itself, a question has matured or shifted. But something returns — and it is this return that territorializes. This is also what distinguishes the retcon from the punctual gesture. An isolated workshop is not a retcon — it is an intervention. The retcon demands *recurrence*, the progressive sedimentation of a territory of practice. It has meaning only *within a series* — it is a *necessary parasite*: it needs a host (the series, the archive, the institutional narrative) to exist, and in return it transforms that host. We will return to this.

### 2.3 The Fold: Neither Layer nor Stratum

Manning, via Deleuze reading Leibniz, thinks movement as *fold*: "Folding undoes the finality of form. Form becomes a folding-into, a force-toward that is a threshold"²⁴. And on memory: "Remembering is the activation of a contrast that inflects the differential of experience unfolding such that the *then* is felt as an aspect of the nowness of experience"²⁵.

The retcon is an operation of *folding*. The archive folds into the collective workshop — it inflects, densifies in a new space-time. Then it unfolds in the productions that deploy what the fold had compressed. But the unfold is never a return to the prior state. This operation distinguishes the retcon from the palimpsest: the palimpsest *stacks* layers; the retcon *folds*. There is neither above nor below, neither original nor copy, only successive inflections that change the curvature of the whole. The retcon does not dig into the archive (archaeological model) — it *folds with* the archive (topological model)²⁶.

The word "continuum" that one can draw from *continuity* in *retroactive continuity* takes on its full meaning here. It is an ontological position: past, present, and future form a *continuous field*, not a succession of discrete moments — a Bergsonian thesis (duration as indivisible flow), a Simondonian one (individuation as a never-completed process), an archival one (the *records continuum* of McKemmish and Upward, where documents are "always in a state of becoming")²⁷. To affirm the continuum is to refuse periodization as a mode of knowledge, the closure of the archive, and the separation between production and reception — this is what Hal Foster shows for the avant-garde: the neo-avant-garde does not "receive" the historical avant-garde; it *produces* it retroactively⁸.

The proper temporality of this continuum is the *future anterior*: what *will have been*. Not what is, nor what will be, but what, at a later moment, will reveal itself to have been. It is a strange grammatical tense — it designates a past that does not yet exist, a past produced by the future. Manning grasps it in dance: the time of the interval is a "future-past that is prolonged at the interface of the becoming-actual of the virtual"¹⁷. The dancer's gesture takes meaning only retroactively, when the following movement illuminates what the preceding movement was becoming.

Joshua Clover, in "Retcon: Value and Temporality in Poetics," published in 2014 in the journal *Representations*, shows that this temporal structure is limited neither to dance nor to narrative — it traverses apparently disjoint fields²⁸. His starting point is a coincidence of dates that may not be one. In 1973, three things happen simultaneously: the theologian Tupper publishes the term "retroactive continuity" to describe Pannenberg's thought (history "flows fundamentally from the future into the past"); the Black-Scholes equation opens the financial options market; and the global economy tips into what will be called financialization. Clover takes these three events seriously and demonstrates that they share the same structure.

In the Black-Scholes equation, the model does not predict the future — it does the reverse. One starts from a given future price (the option's price at maturity) and retroactively recalculates what the option should be worth *now*. This is what traders call "implied volatility": a backward reading, from the future toward the present. Black-Scholes is a financial retcon — it restructures the present so that the future comes out right.

For Marx, the value of a commodity works the same way. The quantity of labor incorporated in an object is not known at the moment of its production — it only "realizes" itself at the moment of sale, when the price retroactively fixes what the labor "was worth." Žižek, reading Karatani, formulates this lapidarily: value "does not *immediately* exist; it *will only have been* — it is retroactively actualized, performatively enacted." The future anterior is the temporality of value as much as that of the retcon.

But Clover pushes the argument further still — all the way to language itself. The Lacanian *point de capiton* designates the moment when a sentence is fixed: it is the period, the punctuation, that retroactively stabilizes the meaning of all the words that precede. As long as the sentence is not finished, its meaning remains open, floating — each new word can reconfigure what the preceding words meant. Clover draws a radical thesis from this: "Reading is retcon." Every act of reading is an operation of retroactive continuity — the reader assembles meaning by constantly going back, reinterpreting the beginning in light of the end.

And it is poetry that makes this operation visible. In prose — from the Latin *pro-vorsa*, turned forward — the retroactive movement of reading is concealed beneath the appearance of linearity: one advances, sentence by sentence, and meaning seems to build from left to right. Verse — from the Latin *versus*, the turning — does the opposite. The line break interrupts the syntax and forces the reader to go back: the meaning of a verse is not complete until one has read the next, which may confirm, displace, or contradict it. Poetry formalizes what prose naturalizes: the retroactivity of meaning.

### 2.4 Archaeology and Topology: Two Models of the Archive

It remains to be seen what this temporality does to the artifacts of the past, to the archive itself — and first, which archive we are talking about. Not the institutional repository, nor the state document in the legal sense. Something more elementary: the *index* of an initial state. A photograph fixes an arrangement of light at a given instant; minutes fix an agreement between parties; an architect's plan fixes a spatial intention. These are traces that record a *moment* — and that time, by separating them from their context of production, makes available for something other than what they were produced for. It is this "something other" that is at stake. The question the retcon poses to the archive is not: *do these traces tell the truth?* It is: *what do they become when they are relocalized?* Classical archival science — the one Tom Nesmith summarizes in a formula — rests on a positivist model: the archivist is the neutral "keeper and preserver" of documents that possess a "special, even unique integrity as a means of access to the past"²⁹. In this model, the archive is a mirror — it reflects the world, and the archivist contents herself with "listing, describing, copying and retrieving the documents and, through them, the knowledge already contained in them, in a neutral, unobtrusive and simply factual manner." Verne Harris shows that this conception rests on an optical metaphor: knowledge is light, ignorance is darkness. "What we remember, we keep in the light; what we forget is consigned to the darkness. To remember is to archive"³⁰. The archive belongs to the regime of vision: one illuminates or one leaves in shadow, one retrieves or one loses. This is the archaeological model in its purest form — digging to bring to light what is buried, illuminating what was in the dark.

Foucault destroys the innocence of this model. In *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969), he reverses the relation between document and monument: where traditional history turned monuments into documents (it made them speak), the new history "transforms documents into monuments" — it describes them in their materiality and their interrelations rather than interrogating them about their truth³¹. And the archive is no longer a collection of interpretable pieces — it is "the general system of the formation and transformation of statements," "the law of what can be said"³². The archive does not *contain* knowledge: it *defines the conditions of its possibility*. Laura Ann Stoler extends this rupture into colonial archives: "archives are often at once documents of exclusion and monuments of particular configurations of power"³³. And Derrida, deconstructing the very etymology of the word, recalls that *arkhè* names both the *commencement* (the place where things begin) and the *commandment* (the place from which order is given)³⁴. There is no power without control of the archive — and the retcon distinguishes itself from the sovereign operations examined above precisely because it acts *without* that control.

But Foucault describes the system — he does not reconfigure it. The retcon would be the next active step. It does not dig toward a buried original (positivist model), it does not describe the conditions of the sayable (Foucauldian model) — it *operates*. It takes the elements of the archive and *displaces* them: from one context to another, from one medium to another, from one temporality to another. This is the topological model. Knowledge is not measured here by the depth reached (how many layers have you traversed?) nor by the exhaustiveness of the description (how many discursive formations have you identified?) but by the quality of the connections produced by displacement. The retcon does not bring the archive "to light" — it *folds* it elsewhere, and it is the folding that reveals what the prior configuration rendered invisible. Not: "here is what the archive was hiding." But: "here is what the archive becomes when it is relocalized." It is a practice of speculative creation, and it should be considered as such.

### 2.5 The Absent Partner: Dancing with History

Let us return to the metaphor of dance to describe what is concretely at play in the relation to history and its artifacts. In Manning's tango, the two partners are co-present, synchronous, alive — each senses the other's preaccelerations, each responds in real time. In the retcon, the situation is radically asymmetrical: one of the two "bodies" is an archive — a set of partial, dispersed, contradictory traces, produced by absent actors, sometimes dead for decades. The archive does not preaccelerate like a living body. It does not respond to a gesture with a counter-gesture. It responds otherwise — through its silences as much as its documents, through its gaps as much as its coherences, through what it has not preserved as much as what it has kept.

Dancing with an absent partner — this is the retcon's proper condition. The elasticity of the almost is always haunted by the possibility of loss: the archive may remain silent, the interval may not form. The retcon must *learn to sense the preaccelerations of an absent body* — through long and repeated familiarity with the archive, through intimacy with its textures, its rhythms, its silences. This is why the retcon can only be thought through the haunting of its operator and through the technicity of its emergence. The retcon is technical as a musician plays an instrument: not by applying an abstract method to the material, but through the intimacy acquired with the resistances and resonances of this particular material. The musician does not feel the music *then* play — she feels *while playing*, through the instrument, through the memory of the repeated gesture. Likewise, the retcon's operator does not understand the archive *then* reconfigure it — she understands *while reconfiguring*, through the tools, the protocols, the collective exchanges that constitute her practice. The retcon's technicity is not a knowledge *about* the archive — it is a knowledge *with* the archive, forged over time.

Manning: "No things are 'together' except in experience"³⁵. The archive and the retcon's operator are "together" only in the experience of the workshop, the residency, the writing. Outside of this, the archive is an inert repository and the operator is a researcher without a terrain.

## 3. A Scene

Twenty-five students in a room. The workshop's title refers to speculative narratives — it announces that what is about to happen is neither a history class nor a fiction exercise, but something between the two. On the screens, four interfaces created for the occasion: a double helix that braids the daily weather of the Blue Ridge Mountains with the events of the college day by day, from 1933 to 1957; a network of verified connections between people — teachers, students, families, co-presences, shared productions — that can be filtered by discipline, type of link, or year; an environmental timeline that renders the ecology of the site — the species, the seasons, the farm, the altitude, the forest composition; and a daily frieze that superimposes what was happening at the college, what was playing on the radio, what was happening in the country and in the world. Beside the screens, a collection of archival images accessible via blockchain and peer-to-peer — the photographs from the Dreier collection, the documents from the Western Regional Archives, the digitized correspondences.

Each student chooses a person. Not one of the "big names" — not necessarily. A mathematics professor, a weaving student, an Austrian refugee who arrived in 1938, a farmer who tended the garden. From the interfaces, the images, the descriptions, the co-presence relations, the national news of the chosen week, the weather of that day, each student must narrate *one day* — or a moment of one day. Not reconstruct: *narrate*. The protocol is simple and demanding: everything that is verifiable must be verified (the temperature, the class given that day, the song playing on the radio, the attested presence of such or such person); everything that is not can be invented, on the condition of showing the seam — of signaling where the document stops and where speculation begins.

It is here that the elasticity of the almost takes form. The student is before a photograph showing three people on the porch of the Studies Building — but one does not know what they are talking about, nor why one of them is laughing, nor what happened just before or just after. The connection network indicates that these three people took the same course that semester. The timeline says it rained that day and that Truman gave a radio address that evening. The ecology reports that the rhododendrons were in bloom. From these fragments — some verified, others deduced, others invented — a narrative composes itself. It is not true. It is not false. It is *plausible*, and its plausibility is constructed piece by piece, with materials whose origin can be traced.

By evening, there are twenty-five narratives. Twenty-five days at Black Mountain College that never took place but that could have — and that, henceforth, exist as forms. But these narratives are not historical reconstructions: what slips into them, inevitably, are fragments of the strangeness of the present. The student who narrates the day of an Austrian refugee in 1938 puts into it — without always knowing — the political affect of 2026, the contemporary anxiety about borders, the precarity he or she knows from the inside. The speculative narrative of a BMC farmer carries within it today's ecological questions, the consciousness of soil, the attention to non-human life that the farmer herself may not have had. The retcon does not produce the past — it produces *the present folded into the past*. What is added to the archive is not one more interpretation — it is the deposit of current subjectivities into ancient forms, the trace of a contemporary collective in the materials of a vanished one. The archive has been *refolded*. It now contains one more *form of life*, produced by people who do not own it, from its own materials — and from their own present. Without formulating it in these terms, the students have done something precise: they have established the conditions for Black Mountain College to exist in the present tense. Not as a memory nor as a model, but as an active continuity — they have extended the line of its continuum, added a segment to the series, prolonged the refrain with their own voices.

## 4. Proximities: From Text to Installation

What has just been described — a group that spatializes the time of an archive, that makes documents from different eras coexist with new forms, that works within a non-linear temporality — is not isolated. The retcon as a cultural operation traverses very different milieus today. In literary text, the postcolonial rewritings of Jean Rhys (*Wide Sargasso Sea*³⁶, which retcons *Jane Eyre* from the point of view of the woman locked in the attic) or of Kamel Daoud (*Meursault, contre-enquête*³⁷, which retcons *The Stranger* from the Arab victim's perspective) produce retroactive continuities that reconfigure the originals without erasing them. In comic books — the term's milieu of origin — the retcon remains a permanent industrial practice, from *Crisis on Infinite Earths* (1985) to the successive reboots of the Marvel universe. Japanese manga and anime have developed their own forms of retroactive reconfiguration — from *Dragon Ball* to *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, where successive *rebuilds* reconfigure the meaning of the original series. On Reddit and fan forums, entire communities devote themselves to producing "fan theories" that retcon existing fictions — retroactively explaining inconsistencies, adding layers of meaning that the original creators had not foreseen. And fan fiction, that immense parallel production that the cultural industry long scorned, is structurally a retcon: it works from inside a continuity it does not own, it reconfigures it without mandate, it shows its seams. In all these cases, the same structure: the present acts on the past, from a non-sovereign position, adding complexity rather than subtracting.

But others still do it in exhibition space — and the form they invent merits a detour, because it illuminates the retcon from a milieu that is neither text nor forum, but the visitor's body in space.

The classical fresco — Masaccio, Michelangelo, the narrative cycles of churches — was already a machine for defeating temporal linearity. In Masaccio's *Tribute Money* (Brancacci Chapel, 1425), the apostle Peter appears three times in the same frame: at center receiving Christ's command, at left fishing for the miraculous coin, at right paying the tax collector. Past, present, and future coexist on the same plane — what the fresco spatializes is the continuum. This is not a retcon: no one retroactively reconfigures the meaning of the narrative. It is a *temporal omniscience*, the "time of God" in which all epochs are simultaneously visible. But the formal structure — the coexistence of times within a single space — is the one the retcon practices. The retcon is an operation of total knowledge of the temporal spectrum, with the exception of the result of the reconfiguration.

The contemporary immersive installation — driven by the renewal of biennials, monumental formats like Monumenta, and by the trend toward the spectacular in contemporary art — functions *like* a fresco in a precise sense: it spatializes time. But where the mural fresco imposed a fixed surface and a spectator who looks, the contemporary installation *environmentalizes* time — the spectator no longer watches chronology unfold; she walks inside a heterochrony. And among artists who work on diasporic, queer, or decolonial identities, this heterochrony is not an aesthetic choice — it is a political necessity. Linear time (Western, chronological, archival) is perceived there as an ordering technology that has served to classify, hierarchize, erase. To spatialize time is to substitute for the arrow that orders a space where those it had separated coexist. Linear chronology produces hierarchy — the primitive and the modern, the archaic and the contemporary, the lagging and the advanced — and spatialization undoes it by making simultaneous those it had made successive. Several contemporary practices illuminate this gesture, each from its own milieu.

The collective Black Quantum Futurism (Rasheedah Phillips and Camae Ayewa / Moor Mother) starts from this premise. By fusing quantum physics and African cosmologies, their spatio-sonic installations deploy altered clocks, star maps, soundscapes that materialize what they call *retrocausality* — the idea that the future can influence the past. The Western clock is an instrument of control; the sabotage of chronology is an act of emancipation. What BQF practices is not a retcon in the strict sense — they do not reconfigure a preexisting narrative continuity. Their term is retrocausality, their framework is physics and cosmology, their gesture is a technological ritual. But the temporal structure is the same as that of the retcon: the present acts on the past, and this action is transparent, collective, non-sovereign. They are artists without institutional mandate who intervene in a history — that of the diaspora — that they did not write and that erased them.

Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley starts from an archival observation: the official archive has erased or poorly documented the existence of Black trans people. Her installations — immersive video games, interactive projections, spaces wallpapered with pixels and injunctive texts — are total frescoes of the digital age in which the visitor makes choices in real time. Brathwaite-Shirley does not speak of retcon — she speaks of archiving, protection, survival. But her gesture is structurally proximate: she adds to a lacunary archive elements that retroactively modify its meaning. Forgotten ancestors are reintegrated into the fabric of history not as victims but as architects of memory. What Saidiya Hartman calls "critical fabulation"³⁸ — filling the voids of the archive through radical imagination — is the method. This is not a retcon (there is no preexisting narrative continuity to reconfigure); it is an *invention of archive*, a production of past that had not been documented. But the kinship is deep: in both cases, the present produces the past — not to falsify it, but to make it habitable.

Josèfa Ntjam deploys enveloping environments — monumental wallpapers, photomontages on canvas, ceramic sculptures, videos — where figures extracted from African history, anticolonial struggles, animist deities, and microscopic organisms coexist in subaquatic and cosmic landscapes. Photomontage is her technique of temporal manipulation: on a single wall, a 1960s activist coexists with an abyssal creature and a futuristic cyborg. Often drawing on the myth of Drexciya — the underwater civilization founded by the children of African women thrown overboard during the Middle Passage — Ntjam practices what one might call a retroactive mythopoiesis: drowning is rewritten as speciation, an aquatic birth. Here again, this is not a retcon in the strict sense — it is a mythology of mutation, with its own rules and its own sources. But the temporal gesture is the same: the future modifies the past, and this modification produces a habitable space in the present.

With Walid Raad and The Atlas Group, the proximity to the retcon changes in kind — we move from the spatialization of time to the *archival operation*. The Atlas Group presents itself as a foundation that "produces, locates, and preserves" audiovisual, photographic, and literary documents on the contemporary history of Lebanon. Except that these documents are in part *fabricated* by Raad — invented and presented with the full apparatus of the archive: call numbers, descriptions, datings, attributions to fictional characters such as Dr. Fadl Fakhouri, who supposedly kept a notebook of horse racing bets during the civil war, or the operator Souheil Bachar, presented as the eighteenth Western hostage in Lebanon. What is added to the archive does not fill a void (as in Brathwaite-Shirley) — it *deregulates* the documentary regime. One no longer knows what is "true" and what is fabricated, and this indistinction makes palpable what the official archive cannot document: the absurdity of war, the persistence of the everyday within trauma, the structural inadequacy between the event and its trace. Raad exhibits the protocol — he shows, in his lecture-performances, that these documents are constructed; he plays with doubt; he does not falsify. The gesture is that of the retcon at its closest to its definition: adding to a preexisting documentary continuity elements that retroactively modify its meaning, from a non-sovereign position (an artist of the Lebanese diaspora, without institutional mandate), increasing the archive's complexity rather than reducing it.

Ho Tzu Nyen shifts the cursor further still — from the fabricated archive to the archive *reconfigured by the machine*. His long-term project, *The Critical Dictionary of Southeast Asia*, is a video encyclopedia whose every entry — a letter of the alphabet — is generated from fragments of films, colonial texts, archival documents, and animation sequences, recombined by algorithm. The narratives produced by British, Japanese, and Malay administrators about Southeast Asia are displaced from their original contexts and relocalized in new constellations of meaning that no one had foreseen. Ho does not fill the archive's voids (Brathwaite-Shirley), does not fabricate false documents (Raad), does not mythologize trauma (Ntjam) — he subjects the existing archive to a *computational reconfiguration* that reveals its buried structures, its latent connections, its transtemporal resonances. *Phantoms of Endless Day* (2025), the central piece of the exhibition *Phantom Day and Stranger Tales* at the Fondation LUMA in Arles, radicalizes this method: an unfinished film about the Japanese occupation of Singapore whose faces are generated by AI models, whose dialogues rewrite themselves at each loop, whose historical figures — double agents, forgotten revolutionaries — appear and dissolve in an algorithmic fog. The visitor enters a space where colonial fragments are projected, recombined in real time, recontextualized by a system that is neither the artist nor the spectator but an *associated milieu* — in Simondon's sense — between the archive and the machine. The proximity to the retcon is here the closest and the most technical. The computational model is not a tool applied to the archive — it is an agent of reconfiguration that co-produces new meaning. This proximity directly prepares the question of technogenesis.

But a difference must be marked here that is not only one of milieu — it is one of *position*. BQF, Brathwaite-Shirley, Ntjam, Raad, and Ho all work from diasporic, postcolonial, or decolonial positions, on archives produced by powers that erased, enslaved, or administered them. Their gesture of reconfiguration is an act of resistance to the archive itself — to its regime of truth, its categories, its silences. *Retcon Black Mountain* works in a different landscape and must assume it. The BMC is an object of Western art history, constructed as such by American historiography: the pragmatic experience of utopia, the laboratory of experimental pedagogy, the place where Albers, Cage, Cunningham, and Fuller forged the artistic modernity of the postwar period. But this narrative is doubly constructed — in its time and *a posteriori*. The successes are twofold, and the blind spots likewise. The place of women, for instance, was minimized twice: first during the college's years of operation — one need only read the unpublished texts of M.C. Richards, abundant, lucid, traversed by a thought proper to BMC as a form of life, to measure that she was not granted the possibility of narrating Black Mountain and becoming its historian; these texts exist, but no one published them, and it was Duberman, not Richards, who wrote the monograph — then after the fact, when one considers the belated recognition of Anni Albers's genius, long reduced to the status of Josef's wife. It took until the 2000s — half a century after the facts — for Albers to be exhibited and studied as a major artist in her own right, and not as the companion of a more famous man. The canonical narrative is not false — but it is partial, and its partiality is itself a construction that must be unfolded.

The retcon as I practice it is situated *within* the mechanics of this Western narrative — it inherits from it, it uses its materials, it frequents its archives. But it also works to unfold its occluded dimensions: the BMC as proto-narrative of the refugee community (the Albers and so many others fleeing Nazism), as a cybernetic experiment avant la lettre (Fuller, the *Black Mountain Studies*), as an environmentalist laboratory (the farm, the lake, the inscription in Appalachian territory), as an avant-garde cell in the strong sense — not the myth of the solitary genius, but a precarious community experimenting with forms of life. The retcon does not reproduce the BMC's canonical narrative — it reconfigures it by adding lines of force that official historiography has minored or ignored, and inscribes our contemporary affect, our desire for futurity. The question arises: is *Retcon Black Mountain* a fan fiction of the BMC's history? Yes, in a sense — it acts without institutional mandate, from a marginal position, with a love of the material that is that of the fan. No, because fan fiction recognizes itself as *exterior* to official continuity, whereas the retcon intervenes *within* the continuity and reconfigures it from the inside. In this, despite the difference in position, the gesture rejoins that of Raad and Ho: reconfiguring an archive from within its own tradition, increasing its complexity rather than reducing it.

What links these five practices to the retcon is not an identity of method — each has its own terms (retrocausality, critical fabulation, mythopoiesis, fictional archive, computational dictionary) and its own milieu (sonic ritual, interactive archive, cosmic photomontage, lecture-performance, algorithmic encyclopedia). It is a structural kinship that rests on three traits: *non-sovereignty* (these artists operate from marginal positions — diasporic, queer, decolonial, postcolonial — on histories they did not write), *transparency* (the photomontage, the hacked code, the glitch, the exhibited false document, the visible algorithm are the marks of reconfiguration — they show that history has been cut and re-stitched), and the *continuum* (past, present, and future form a continuous field where meaning circulates in all directions). The retcon as I practice it from the collective workshop and the BMC terrain shares these three traits — but from a different milieu: the institutional archive, the pedagogical document, the collective protocol. What the fresco-exhibition environmentalizes through immersion and the visitor's body, the workshop does through the collective manipulation of documents and the production of new forms. Two milieus, one same temporal proximity — and, with Raad and Ho, one same archival and computational proximity.

## 5. Technogenesis: The Machines of the Retcon

Brian Massumi, in *Architectures of the Unforeseen* (2019), formulates a principle that condenses everything Manning describes: "Recursive filiation furthers intensive continuity"³⁹. Each new form is the offspring of what it reconfigures, and in return it transforms its source. Recursive filiation does not extend a series by linear extension but by *intensification*: each return to the material increases its density, its charge, its complexity — not by adding an episode *after* the preceding ones, but by passing a new intensity *through* them. The retcon does not lengthen the series — it intensifies it.

And: "With fusion, approached recursively, comes fissionability. Any element, form, or technique can become a reentry point into the form-generating process"⁴⁰. Fissionability is the principle according to which no point in the process is terminal: an archival photograph can become the seed of a chatbot, a chatbot the seed of a graphic novel, a graphic novel the seed of a collective workshop — and each of these bifurcations in turn opens new reentry points. The retcon has no privileged point of entry — it has a virtual infinity of angles of return. But fissionability does not operate barehanded. It requires machines — digital archives, peer-to-peer networks, language models, blockchain systems — and the question of their status is decisive. Manning responds with the concept of *technogenesis*: "ontogenesis of the bio-technological not as a technical additive to the biological but as an emphasis on originary technicity"⁴¹. "Rather than mapping the technology — as a prosthesis — onto a moving body, it is necessary to incite the movement to appear out of the technological process that is the machinic assemblage of individuation"⁴².

A chatbot trained on a corpus of archives is not a tool applied to the corpus — it is the archive itself undergoing technogenesis, reconfiguring itself in a joint becoming with the language model. The corpus is not an object on which one runs an algorithm — it is an *associated milieu*, in Simondon's sense, where the archive and the machine co-evolve⁴³. The problem is not: "how to apply AI to archives?" (prosthetic model) — a question that belongs to techno-solutionism, the belief that every cultural problem calls for a computational solution. The problem is: "what associated milieu is constituted when a language model and an archival corpus individuate together?" The interfaces presented in the preceding scene — the double helix, the network of connections, the ecological timeline — are a first state of this: neither tools applied to the archive, nor the archive itself, but a third thing born from their co-individuation. The essays that follow in this thesis explore more advanced forms — and themselves become, materially, training corpus for these machines. The loop is recursive: the text that describes technogenesis feeds the model that pursues it.

Manning and Massumi, in *Thought in the Act* (2014), posit that every practice is *already* a mode of thought: "Every practice is a mode of thought, already in the act"⁴⁴. The concepts mobilized in this work — Manning, Friedenthal, Clover, Simondon — are not theoretical frameworks applied to a terrain. They are *materials* of the retcon, on the same footing as archival photographs: the thesis relocalizes them toward Black Mountain, and this displacement modifies their meaning as much as it illuminates the terrain. Manning takes up from Deleuze and Guattari the concept of *machinic phylum*: "materiality, natural and artificial, and both simultaneously; it is matter in movement, in flux, in variation, matter as vector of singularities and traits of expression"⁴⁵. The *phylum* is not a series of objects but a lineage of technical problems that transmit themselves by transforming. When a practice secretes over several years a succession of technical devices — encrypted networks, peer-to-peer databases, chatbots, blockchain systems, interactions with language models — these are not successive "tools" but a lineage where the same singularity (*how to make the archive active?*) transmits itself by transforming. The retcon has not "adopted" technologies — it has *secreted* a proper technological lineage. Each machine in the phylum poses a problem (centralization, accessibility, fidelity, control) that the next machine resolves while posing a new one. Each machine is a retcon of the preceding machine.

## 6. *Tactical Method of Collective Re-localization*

Each word of this expression carries a specific weight.

*Tactical*, first, as opposed to *strategic*, in Michel de Certeau's sense in *The Practice of Everyday Life* (1980)⁴⁶. Strategy presupposes a *proper place* — a territory, an institution, a position of overview. Tactics operate *without a proper place*. The retcon is tactical: it has no institution, no stable territory, no designated discipline.

But the retcon complicates the Certalian opposition: it is tactical *and* it has duration. Years of returns to the terrain, an accumulation of materials, a network of collaborators. This is not the punctual cunning of the Certalian consumer — it is a *sedimented tactic*, one that has accumulated knowledge, materials, relationships. Perhaps one should speak of a tactic that, through repetition, has secreted its own milieu — without ever becoming strategy.

This sedimented tactic connects the retcon to the *commons*. An archive dispersed across dozens of repositories functions as a commons: no single institution controls it entirely. The retcon's technical tools — peer-to-peer networks, blockchain, shared libraries — are the material infrastructure of the absence of sovereignty posited above. The retcon poses a political question: *who owns the past?*

And the retcon is a *parasite* — in the sense Michel Serres gives this word in *The Parasite* (1980)⁴⁷. The parasite is not an accident of the system: it *is* the relation. All communication passes through a third party that extracts, diverts, transforms. The parasite introduces noise into the signal — and it is this noise that forces the system to reorganize, to produce novelty. Without the parasite, the system repeats identically; with it, it differentiates. The retcon is noise in the historiographic signal: it introduces into the archive unforeseen elements — a graphic form, a language model, a collective of artists — that force the narrative to reorganize. And this noise is *productive*: it is what makes audible the connections that the "clean" signal rendered inaudible. The parasite needs its host — the retcon does not produce its own continuity; it installs itself in an existing continuity and reconfigures it from the inside. Without a host, no retcon: this is what distinguishes it from creation *ex nihilo*. The parasite does not destroy the host — that is the difference from damnatio memoriae — it transforms it by passing through it. And the parasite, by definition, is not the master of the house: it enters by the service door. The retcon is a parasite that shows it parasitizes — and it is this transparency, once again, that distinguishes it from falsification.

*Re-localization*, next. Not *re-reading*, not *re-interpretation*, not *re-writing*. *Re-localization*: displacing elements from one context to another, from one place to another, from one medium to another, from one epoch to another. The fundamental gesture of the retcon is not hermeneutic (understanding otherwise) — it is *topological* (placing elsewhere). New meaning is *produced* by displacement.

But to re-localize is not merely to displace documents. It is to invent a common territory — what Manning, after Whitehead, calls a *worlding*: not occupying a pre-existing space but *creating* it through the very movement. "We move not to populate space, not to extend it or to embody it, but to create it"⁴⁸. The collective workshop is this worlding: a space that does not exist before the participants engage in it, and that emerges from their mutual displacements. Each person re-localizes their reading of the archive, and this reading finds itself immediately exposed to those of others, displaced by them, reconfigured on contact with subjectivities it had not foreseen. The workshop is a border zone where each person objectifies herself for the Other — where the subjectivity of each participant becomes material available to the collective, on the same footing as archival photographs and texts. To re-localize the archive is also to re-localize oneself — to accept that one's own position in the narrative is not fixed, that it is as available for reconfiguration as the documents one works on. The retcon is irreducibly collective: it is not a matter of projecting an individual interpretation onto a material, but of producing a space — a common world — where subjectivities mutually displace one another.

*Method*, finally. The word is chosen against "methodology." Methodology is the method that watches itself doing — it reflects on its own conditions of possibility from a position of overview. Method is *in* the gesture. It transmits itself through practice, verifies itself through results, corrects itself through failure. To say that the retcon is a method is to affirm that it is systematic, transmissible, reproducible in its protocols — but it is also to affirm that it does not think itself first and execute itself afterward. It thinks itself *in doing itself*. A text like this one — which describes the retcon while attempting to practice it, which relocalizes Manning, Friedenthal, Clover toward the terrain of an archive — is not a methodology that precedes practice. It is the method in the process of explicating itself, a retcon of its own operations. And it is also, materially, a future training document: the essays that compose this thesis serve and will serve as corpus for the customized language models at the heart of *Retcon Black Mountain* — the text that describes the method becomes the material that feeds the machine, which in turn reconfigures the archive.

But if the retcon is a method, it must be able to fail — and one must be able to say *by what* one recognizes that it has worked. The transparency of the protocol and the absence of sovereignty are necessary conditions, not criteria of success. Three criteria allow one to distinguish a retcon that produces something from a retcon that spins its wheels. The first is *fissionability*: a retcon has worked if it opens new reentry points — if the forms it produces (a graphic novel, a chatbot, a collective narrative) in turn become materials available for further reconfigurations. A sterile retcon closes the continuity instead of opening it. The second is *densification*: a retcon has worked if it increases the archive's complexity — if it makes visible connections, layers of meaning, tensions that the canonical narrative did not render visible. This criterion is verifiable: do a workshop's productions make elements emerge in the archive that no one had seen there, or do they reproduce what was already known? The third is *appropriability*: a retcon has worked if other collectives can take it up, modify it, *fork* it. This is the criterion of the commons — does the reconfiguration remain available or does it close into property? And there is an anti-criterion: *completeness*. A retcon that claims to resolve the archive, to give the definitive reading, has failed — it has fallen back into sovereignty. The successful retcon is the one that leaves the question open while making it more precise.

## 7. Conclusion: Return to the Gesture

The retcon, writes Friedenthal, is "not done out of disrespect for the past, but rather out of a love for past works, a desire to play with them, and a drive to modernize, update, and incorporate them into new works"⁴⁹.

The retcon resolves nothing. It has not "recovered" the true Black Mountain College beneath the layers of mythography. It has not produced the definitive theory of retroactivity. It has not saved the archive from its dispersion — it has made it more dispersed still, by folding it into new forms, machines, collective workshops, texts like this one.

What it has done to the *narrative* of the BMC is perhaps more decisive than what it has done to the archive. The canonical narrative — the laboratory of American modernity, the pedagogical utopia, the place of the great names — has not been destroyed. It has been *reconfigured*: the occluded dimensions (the refugee community, the cybernetic experiment, the environmentalist laboratory, the precarious avant-garde cell) have been unfolded and added to the continuity, not to contradict the existing narrative but to augment it. The retcon has done to the BMC what BQF, Brathwaite-Shirley, Ntjam, Raad, and Ho do to their own archives — from a different position (within the Western tradition rather than against it), but with the same gesture: adding complexity rather than subtracting, showing the protocol rather than concealing it, operating without sovereignty rather than by decree.

What it has done to the *gesture* is to *inhabit the interval* — the space between an artist-researcher and a dispersed archive, between a group and documents it does not own, between a question (what to do with Black Mountain?) and the absence of a definitive answer. It has sensed the elasticity of the almost each time the BMC materials began to become something other than themselves, and it has repeated the refrain year after year until a territory formed and the moment came to leave it.

What remains is a phylum: a lineage of machines, gestures, forms, encounters where the same question transmitted itself by transforming. How to make the archive active? The question is not resolved. It is *relocalized* — it passes from one medium to another, changes collective, changes place. And at each relocalization, it produces new meaning: not the answer, but the condition for the question to remain alive.

But the retcon also has a limit — and this limit is crisis. Clover shows that in the circuit of value (M-C-M')⁵⁰, the retcon functions so long as retrojection manages to smooth the "price surface," to maintain coherence between value and price through continuous reinterpretation. Crisis occurs when this operation fails — when the gap between value and price becomes too great to be retconned, when fictitious credit can no longer bridge the gap with real labor. Crisis is then a "hyper-retcon": a massive destruction of value that attempts to realign the system by force, by retroactively annulling what "will have been" without ever having been realized⁵¹. The archival retcon knows its own version of this limit: the moment when the archive resists, when the materials will not fold, when the elasticity of the almost dissipates without any form crystallizing. Manning had said it: if one lets the moment pass, "the elastic point will evaporate and only the step will be felt." The retcon is not a guaranteed operation — it is a wager on the plasticity of the past that can always fail. And when it fails, what remains is not nothingness but raw material: the archive intact, the documents still there, the question still open — ready for another collective, another relocalization, another retcon.

But there is a form of failure graver than the dissipation of elasticity — it is irreversible amputation. Not all of the archive's lacunae are creative silences. Some are suffered destructions: burned documents, erased names, lives of which literally nothing remains — not even the meaningful void of the brown stain on the Severan Tondo. Before these lacunae, the retcon must guard against a temptation: that of imaginary consolation, of fictional reparation that would fill the gap by making it habitable. Hartman names this risk with surgical precision: critical fabulation does not *replace* what has been destroyed — it *marks the place of the destruction*. The retcon, if it wants to remain honest, must do the same: not fill the void, but render it visible as void — show that something is missing, that this lack is not an accident but a violence, and that reconfiguration does not repair it. Folding is not healing.

Friedenthal ends his book by observing that "the continuity of our world is always ever retroactive, and must not be assumed"⁵². *Retcon Black Mountain* is an attempt to think, in the neuronal society — the one in which computational networks and social networks have indistinguishably merged — the notions of temporality, common work, and recursivity as antidotes to the massive destructions of value — those that Clover calls the hyper-retcon, but also the quieter ones that dissolve collective memories, flatten archives into data, and reduce the past to an exploitable stock. It is a process-work that must — and will have to — be understood as verifiable, modifiable, *forkable*, in negotiation with history and always for the attention of new spectators.
