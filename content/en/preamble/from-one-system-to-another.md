## Abstract

> *This essay examines what happens when an artist-researcher convokes, within a single gesture, radically heterogeneous theoretical traditions. Drawing on Brian Massumi's process philosophy, on the practice of sampling as theorized by Kodwo Eshun, and on Caroline Busta's analysis of the shift from a linear to a neuronal cultural paradigm, it proposes the notion of* prismatic method*: not an eclecticism but an operation of refraction in which each tradition reveals a frequency of the problem that none could render visible alone. The essay argues that this method constitutes the form of thought adequate to an epistemological mutation currently underway.*

**Keywords:** *research-creation, prismatic method, beings of relation, in-formation, heterogenesis, sampling, neuronal paradigm, fabulation, knowledge graph*

## Introduction

"The world is not only made of already articulated structures and fully formed beings. There are also *beings of relation*." Brian Massumi poses this proposition at the beginning of the second couplet of *Couplets*, and something shifts. <sup>1</sup> For if we take it seriously — if we accept that there exists an ontological dimension proper to what is *in the process of becoming*, populated by what Massumi describes as a spectrum of differentiations without separation: tensions, gradients, thresholds, tendencies, torsions, in a state of mutual inclusion — then the question of transdisciplinarity changes in nature. It ceases to be a problem of method and becomes a question of ontology.

I write this from within an artist's thesis. From within a body of work that convokes, in a single gesture, Stiegler's philosophy of technology, Warburgian iconology, new materialisms, Stengers's cosmopolitics, Kodwo Eshun's sonic fiction, Yuk Hui's recursivity — and many other authors who come to inhabit and illuminate, sometimes fleetingly, an experience spanning several years of research on the work *Retcon Black Mountain*. It is legitimate to question this apparent flitting about, this constant displacement of conceptual frames of reference. This questioning must be taken seriously, and first by naming the doubts it carries. The first is epistemological: if each convocation reconfigures the terms it mobilizes, if the Stiegler who comes into contact with Warburg is not quite the same as the one who comes into contact with Haraway, then the minimal conditions of verification collapse — and the method is merely an elegant name for the absence of method. The second is critical: how does one distinguish a genuinely emergent third-concept from a rhetorical artifact, two words side by side that sound good but do no work? The third is political: if the researcher is caught up in the assemblage they describe, if they renounce the view from above, from where does one evaluate? The reproach of *papillonnage* — of flitting — condenses these three doubts into a single gesture of disqualification. And it is right on one point: convocation can fail. The juxtaposition of names does not produce thought. Flitting exists. Anyone who has sat through a doctoral seminar knows this.

But the reproach is wrong about the plane on which it poses the problem. It reasons at the level of what Massumi calls already articulated structures — the plane where systems of thought are indeed incompatible, where Bennett's materialism contradicts Latour's constructivism, where Stiegler and Haraway occupy incommensurable positions. At this level, yes, the systems are not compatible. But this level is not the only one. Massumi, following Simondon, posits the existence of a plane of *in-formation* — not a content transmitted but a process of taking form, the dimension of the not-yet-formed, governed by mutual inclusion rather than reciprocal exclusion. <sup>2</sup> It is on this plane that convocation operates when it does not fail.

What it produces there, I propose to call the *prismatic method*. The prism does not destroy white light; it decomposes it into a spectrum where each frequency becomes visible. Likewise, the passage from one system of thought to another — from a Warburg to a Simondon, from a Stiegler to an Eshun — does not dissolve the traditions convoked: it refracts them around a common problem, revealing from each a wavelength that the others make perceptible by contrast. I have developed elsewhere the notion of *prismatic archive* to designate the corpus itself — the texts, documents, and written thoughts that constitute the raw material of this refraction; the prismatic method is its operation, the prismatic archive its milieu. This is neither a synthesis nor a collage: it is what Guattari called a *heterogenesis* — a reciprocal becoming-different whose effect is the emergence of a thirdness, something that existed in none of the traditions convoked. <sup>3</sup>

This essay attempts to think this operation at the intersection of three diagnostics: Brian Massumi's process ontology, developed notably in "On the Right to the Noncommunication of Cultural Difference" (2000), reprinted in *Couplets: Travels in Speculative Pragmatism* (Duke University Press, 2024), which provides the plane; the practice of sampling as Kodwo Eshun theorized it in *More Brilliant than the Sun: Adventures in Sonic Fiction* (1998), which provides the gesture; and the analysis by theorist Caroline Busta (New Models, with Lil Internet) of the shift from a linear to a neuronal cultural paradigm, which provides the historical context. It takes at its word Yves Citton's proposition that "research-creation should be conceived as an accelerator of transformation for our methods and agendas of investigation" <sup>30</sup> — and proposes the notion of *prismatic method* to name what this acceleration produces.

## The Sample, the Chiasm, and Agency

In 1987, in New York, Marley Marl discovers by accident that one can isolate an individual sound — a snare, a kick — from an existing record and resequence it in an entirely new context. Sampling is born. Or rather: it is born *technically*, since the gesture already existed — in cubist collage, in Rauschenberg's *Combines*, in Situationist détournement, in Burroughs and Gysin's cut-up, in Schaeffer's musique concrète. But the sampler radicalizes it: the extracted fragment preserves the *grain* of its source — its texture, its own temporality, its world of origin — while functioning within an assemblage that transforms it. <sup>4</sup>

Kodwo Eshun, in *More Brilliant than the Sun*, understood that sampling was not one technique among others but a mode of thought — what he calls *conceptechnics*, the fusion of concept and sonic technique. When Eshun traverses cybernetics, Sun Ra, Kraftwerk, and Drexciya in a single movement of writing, he does not render them compatible — he *samples* them. Each tradition preserves its alterity; it is this alterity that is productive. <sup>5</sup> Eshun does not cite, he extracts. He does not argue, he edits. And what the editing produces is neither the sum nor the synthesis of its components: it is what Deleuze and Guattari call a *double becoming* — an encounter where both terms are altered without either becoming the other, and where a thirdness emerges at the chiasmatic crossing point. <sup>6</sup>

This is where the transposition to the epistemological plane ceases to be a metaphor. When Warburgian *Nachleben* — the survival of forms across epochs <sup>7</sup> — and Stieglerian *tertiary retention* — memory carried by technical objects <sup>8</sup> — are convoked in a single research gesture, a theoretical double becoming occurs. *Nachleben* differentiates itself from itself upon contact with tertiary retention: it discovers a technical dimension it did not know it carried. Tertiary retention differentiates itself from itself upon contact with *Nachleben*: it discovers a spectral, surviving dimension that exceeds its original phenomenological framework. Neither has Warburg "become" Stiegler, nor has Stiegler "become" Warburg. But a third-concept has emerged — something like a *technical survival* or a *haunting of retentions* — that existed in neither tradition. This is the prismatic method in action: refraction has made visible a frequency that each tradition carried within itself without being able to see it alone.

But we must still understand how this refraction operates. Manuel DeLanda reminds us that the components of an assemblage maintain *relations of exteriority*: they are not defined by the whole they compose and can be detached from it to enter other configurations. <sup>9</sup> This is what distinguishes the prismatic method from dialectical synthesis: the convoked traditions are not dissolved in a third term that would sublate them — they preserve their exteriority, their autonomy, their grain. Jane Bennett pushes this logic further by attributing to things themselves an *agency* — a capacity for action that exceeds the intention of the one who mobilizes them. <sup>10</sup> Each convoked theoretical framework possesses such agency: it does something to the problem, something the researcher cannot entirely anticipate. To convoke Gilbert Simondon in a work on digital archives is to accept that the thought of individuation will *act* on the problem in unforeseen ways. The framework is not a passive tool; it is an actant. Karen Barad, with her notion of *intra-action*, adds that the terms in relation do not preexist their being brought into relation: they constitute each other mutually. <sup>11</sup> It is not the same Bernard Stiegler who enters into refraction with Aby Warburg and with Donna Haraway. Each convocation reconfigures the terms. The artist-researcher who performs these convocations is not a sovereign subject disposing of their tools; they are a component of the assemblage, transformed by the *beings of relation* they help bring into being.

But I must be honest. The analogy with sampling has a limit. The sampler operates on material whose parameters are adjustable: one chooses a start point and an end point in the source, one cuts the fragment, transposes it, applies an envelope to it — attack, decay, sustain, release — that sculpts how it appears and disappears in the new context. The grain of the material is known; the cut is precise; the result, if not predictable, is at least audible in real time. The artist-researcher who convokes Simondon has no such parameters. They do not extract an isolable fragment — they extract a field of potential, a grammar of attention, a way of cutting up the real. Where is the start point in a thought? Where does one exit? At what speed does one traverse it? And above all: what envelope does the convocation impose on the extracted fragment — how it attacks the problem, how long it stays, how it withdraws? Theoretical sampling is more imprecise, more risky, more likely to fail. This is why the question of *technique* is decisive — and why Massumi is right to speak of quasi-causal techniques: gestures that do not mechanically produce an effect but create the conditions for an event to occur. <sup>1</sup>

## The Graph and the Survival of Systems of Thought

The prismatic method has a temporal dimension that the sampling analogy alone cannot think. The sample is a reframing — a zone of attention cut from a sonic flow. But unlike the cinematographic or photographic frame, it leaves no off-screen: no before or after, no residual context. The extracted fragment *is* all that it is. Theoretical convocation does something stranger: it makes heterogeneous temporalities coexist — the cybernetics of 1948, the *Mnemosyne* of 1929, process philosophy of 2024 — not by cutting them from their context in the manner of the sample, but by activating what Massumi describes as a *permanent reserve of potential modulation*: everything that passes leaves a trace, returning to the germinal state, becoming virtual again, becoming tendency and potential again, matter for becoming. <sup>1</sup> The convoked tradition preserves its off-screen — it is precisely this off-screen that makes it productive.

Warburg had a name for this: *Nachleben*, survival. Not influence — which is linear, causal — but the unpredictable reappearance of forms in contexts that did not expect them. <sup>7</sup> Didi-Huberman showed how this idea constituted a break with the linear time of art history: no longer a succession of periods but an entanglement of temporalities where the ancient resurfaces in the contemporary according to *symptomal* logics, neither causal nor chronological. <sup>12</sup> Benjamin, for his part, spoke of *dialectical images*: the Once-Was meets the Now in a flash to form a constellation. <sup>13</sup> Stiegler showed that technical objects function as *tertiary retentions* — memory supports carrying a temporality that exceeds that of the individual and the collective. <sup>8</sup> Transposed to the plane of theory, this idea suggests that theoretical frameworks are themselves tertiary retentions: they conserve and transmit grammars of attention, cognitive gestures, ways of cutting up the real. To convoke them together is to activate their *Nachleben*, to make them enter what Yuk Hui calls a *recursivity* — not a simple feedback loop but a spiral movement in which the system returns upon itself in order to redetermine itself, each return being open to contingency, that is, to the irruption of what the system could not foresee. <sup>14</sup>

Eshun, in "Further Considerations on Afrofuturism," draws a distinction that illuminates this process: between the recovery of the past and the programming of the future. <sup>15</sup> To activate the *Nachleben* of a system of thought is not disciplinary nostalgia. When Wiener's cybernetics is convoked in a contemporary work on digital archives, it is not the cybernetics of 1948 that is mobilized — it is a germinal potential of cybernetics that 1948 could not see and that only the present awakens from its dormancy (in the sense that a seed germinates only months or years after being sown, when the conditions of the soil call it back to life).

The method of investigation I propose thus unfolds as a journey through a *graph* — a topological space whose nodes are concepts, authors, gestures, works, epochs, and whose edges designate not filiations but *operative affinities*: zones where a double becoming is possible, where two systems can enter into heterogenesis around a given problem. This graph is traversed in two complementary ways. *Diachronically*: one follows the survival of a problem through its successive transformations — the archive from Warburg to Derrida to Stiegler to Hui, each station revealing a frequency the previous one left in shadow. *Synchronically*: one deploys around a single problem a spectrum of contemporary perspectives from traditions that ignore each other — Bennett, Latour, Simondon, Haraway facing the agency of non-humans — and it is the contrast between these perspectives that makes visible what none could see alone.

Tim Ingold calls *wayfaring* this exploratory walk that does not follow a pre-traced path but engenders the path by walking. <sup>16</sup> The graph of the prismatic method is not a preestablished plan; it is *produced* by the traversal. It is the practice underway — the work, the archive, the field — that calls for this detour through Simondon or that passage through Glissant. Each edge is a double becoming in potentia: two nodes linked not by resemblance but by the possibility of a heterogenesis. What emerges from the traversal is neither a synthesis nor a panorama: it is a theoretical *quasi-object*, a hybrid being that belongs to no discipline and circulates between them. <sup>17</sup>

## From the Linear Paradigm to the Neuronal Paradigm

Up to this point, the argument might seem to be merely a local plea for transdisciplinarity. Caroline Busta has identified a mutation that makes it at once more urgent and more general: the shift from a *linear* to a *neuronal* cultural paradigm. <sup>18</sup> From the sample to the token.

The linear paradigm — that of print, of the canon, of copyright, of filiation — organizes knowledge in chains: one author cites another who cites another, disciplines are transmitted through lineages, value is measured by originality and attribution. The demand for systemic coherence addressed to the artist's thesis is a product of this paradigm: it presupposes that theoretical frameworks must articulate into an argumentative chain, each deriving from the previous, all converging toward a conclusion. The reproach of flitting is a linear reproach: it demands filiations where there are contagions, sequential coherence where thought operates by refraction.

The neuronal paradigm operates differently. Contents circulate by contagion rather than transmission. The source dissolves into the network. Connections are lateral, not hierarchical. Recommendation systems, feeds, language models: all of this already functions by assemblage, by statistical sampling, by convocation without filiation. The knowledge graph that the prismatic method proposes, contemporary technical infrastructure already practices — but without consciousness, without politics, without ethics.

This is where the stakes shift. The question is no longer *whether* transdisciplinary convocation is legitimate — the neuronal paradigm has already made it inevitable. The question is *how* it is practiced: with what awareness of the chiasm, what sense of double becoming, what care for the opacity of the convoked components. The artist-researcher who convokes heterogeneous traditions is not doing something eccentric — they are doing something that corresponds to the very form of contemporary knowledge production. But they do it — or should do it — with an attention to process that the neuronal paradigm, in its machinic version, does not possess.

## Being an *Intercessor*

Massumi tells us that truth is never what is truly at stake in the ecology of practices. What is at stake is the continuation of the process — the capacity of a set of practices to keep producing difference, to survive by transforming. <sup>1</sup> Deleuze calls this the *powers of the false*: not the lie but fabulation — the production of narratives that do not represent the real but relaunch it. <sup>19</sup> The reproach of flitting rests on a linear regime of truth: the mobilization of a framework is justified only by its capacity to produce verifiable propositions. Massumi identifies this regime as an exercise of disciplinary power: the summons to prove is itself an operation of capture that neutralizes the generative force of difference by forcing it into the mold of determinable content.

The prismatic method belongs to another logic — that of fabulation. It does not claim to establish a truth about the relations between Warburg and Simondon. It *fabulates* these relations — it invents them, in the double Latin sense of *invenire*. In doing so it produces *beings of relation* — theoretical thirdnesses that existed in none of the traditions convoked. A becoming-Warburg of Simondon and a becoming-Simondon of Warburg have taken place — without there being a Warburg-Simondon that has become. <sup>6</sup> Haraway makes speculative fabulation the mode of existence of a thought that refuses to resign itself to the conditions of the present. <sup>20</sup> Manning and Massumi show that thought emerges *within* the creative gesture, not before or after it. <sup>21</sup> Eshun practices it: his *Sonic Fiction* is a fabulation that traverses cybernetics and Afrofuturism to produce third-concepts — *Mythscience*, *Sonic Futurism* — that are beings of relation in their nascent state. <sup>5</sup>

But in the neuronal paradigm, fabulation encounters a new adversary. It is no longer only the institution that demands transparency — it is the infrastructure itself. Yves Citton, extending Randy Martin's analysis of the social logic of financial derivatives, has described the exact mechanism of this operation: surveillance capitalism disassembles our attention into variable elements, disperses these elements to rebundle them with those of other attentions, and reassembles the whole according to opportunities for capture — producing *attentional derivatives* that are worth more than the singular attentions from which they are extracted. <sup>29</sup> Language models generalize this operation on the epistemological plane: they disassemble traditions of thought into tokens, disperse these fragments to rebundle them statistically, and reassemble the whole into an undifferentiated flow that has lost the grain of each source. The neuronal paradigm realizes the communicational nightmare that Massumi diagnosed: everything is translated, everything is connected, everything is transparent — and in this transparency, the generative force of difference is extinguished.

Hence the necessity of a double gesture of resistance. Massumi claims a "right to the noncommunication of difference" <sup>1</sup> — against the communicational imperative that demands translation and being translated, rendering all difference transparent, reducing it to a common language. Glissant had stated the same principle in the register of Relation: the right to opacity, that is, the right not to be reduced to transparency in order to be recognized. <sup>22</sup> The prismatic method maintains this right at the heart of its practice. The coexistence of Stiegler and Haraway does not require that they be made transparent to one another. Their reciprocal opacity is the condition of their relational productivity — it is because alterity is maintained that double becoming can take place. To render traditions transparent to one another — what the neuronal paradigm does unknowingly, what the academic institution does by demanding it — would be to neutralize exactly what makes heterogenesis possible.

Despret, taken up by Massumi, calls *ethopoiesis* the self-inventive becoming of forms of life in relation. <sup>23</sup> Research-creation that practices the prismatic method is an epistemological ethopoiesis: it invents forms of thought in relation, thirdnesses that constitute themselves in the very process of their being brought into rapport. This is not institutional interdisciplinarity — an organized dialogue between representatives of constituted disciplines. It is a *chaosmosis*: the oscillation between the chaos of the heterogeneous and the emergence of a local, provisional cosmos, always to be remade. <sup>24</sup> And in the neuronal paradigm, it is a political act: the choice of conscious contamination over machinic indifferentiation, of attentive sampling over statistical grinding. The artist-researcher is what Deleuze called an *intercessor*: not a mediator who translates but a catalyst whose presence creates the conditions for a mutual becoming between heterogeneous forces. <sup>25</sup>

## Conclusion — Cultivating the Field of Belonging

Massumi speaks of those who cultivate the field of belonging — that germinal plane where potentials coexist before differentiating — as *healers of relation*. <sup>1</sup> Their actions are catalytic. What they tend to, upstream of significations and propositions, are affects and percepts that touch upon the pre-individual fact of living-together.

The artist-researcher who practices the prismatic method is such a healer of relation — between systems of thought. They do not resolve their contradictions; they tend to the conditions of their coexistence. Their gesture is quasi-causal: it does not produce the event of thought, it catalyzes the field where that event can occur. And what occurs is always a heterogenesis: a double becoming from which emerges a thirdness — a concept, a work, a theoretical form of life — irreducible to any of the traditions convoked.

It must also be said what is fragile about this position. The prismatic method offers no guarantee. The double becoming may not occur. The thirdness may not emerge. The sample may remain a dead fragment — a piece torn from its context that does not take in the new one. This is the constitutive risk of research-creation: it is a fabulation, and fabulation can fail. What distinguishes it from mere flitting is not a method that would protect it from failure, but an attention to process — to the grain of the materials convoked, to the quality of the chiasm, to the patience of the slowing-down that Stengers demands in the face of complexity. <sup>26</sup>

The reproach of flitting is a *linear* reproach addressed to a *prismatic* practice. It demands filiations where there are refractions, transparency where productivity arises from opacity, systemic coherence where thought operates by heterogenesis. It is not that the reproach is wrong — it is that it belongs to a paradigm that is no longer the only one.

But there is more. Slava Gerovitch has shown that the paradigms of intelligence underlying AI research are culturally conditioned. <sup>28</sup> The American tradition — from Skinner to Shannon to Herbert Simon — built its concept of intelligence on the image of the mouse in the maze: a closed problem, an optimal path, a single exit. Simon called for a decision-maker "less God-like and more rat-like." The maze is the linear paradigm made architecture — and mouse-intelligence is one that optimizes a predetermined trajectory within a space of constraints. It is this intelligence that the demand for systemic coherence requires of the artist's thesis: one framework, one path, one conclusion. Yet Soviet cyberneticians Varshavskii and Pospelov had proposed an entirely different image: that of the moth hunted by a bat. When the bat draws too close, the moth deploys a chaotic flight — "a series of passive falls with folded wings, sharp turns, loops and dives" — rendering its trajectory unpredictable. This strategy saved the moth in 70% of cases. No maze, no walls, no single solution — but an open space, a predator, and the necessity of inventing at each wingbeat a trajectory the other cannot anticipate.

This is the prismatic method. Flitting, far from being a defect, is the name the maze paradigm gives to an intelligence it does not recognize — that of the open traversal, of heterogenesis, of productive chaotic flight. The transdisciplinary artist-researcher flits, yes. They deploy an intelligence of refraction where an intelligence of the optimal path is expected. And as Gerovitch reminds us: without knowing it, science often speaks with a national accent. The demand for systemic coherence has its own.

There remains Massumi's last phrase: *Better to stutter than to s(t)ay the same*. The stutter — that speech which stumbles between languages, between traditions, between epochs — is not a defect of mastery. It is the form of a thought that makes alterity its condition, heterogenesis its method, and flitting — real flitting, the kind that escapes the bat — its event. It is to take seriously what Deleuze and Guattari told us: the multiple must be *made*. <sup>27</sup>
