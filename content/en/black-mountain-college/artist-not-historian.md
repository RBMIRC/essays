# The Artist Is Not a Historian: Toward a Post-Documentary Methodology

## On the Specificity of Artistic Knowledge Production

The artist who enters the archive does not become a historian. This seemingly obvious statement requires elaboration, for the institutional pressures of research-creation often push toward a methodological mimicry that obscures what is specific to artistic inquiry. When I began working with the Black Mountain College archives—correspondence, photographs, administrative documents, student work—I was not conducting historical research. I was preparing the conditions for something else: a practice I have come to call *post-documentary*, in which speculation becomes not the opposite of rigor but its extension into other registers.

The historian's task, as Michel de Certeau described it, is to produce a discourse about the past that maintains a scrupulous separation between the writing subject and the written object. The archive serves as guarantor of this separation: documents are cited, contextualized, verified against other documents. The historian's authority derives from this operational distance. But the artist operates under different constraints and toward different ends. The archive, for the artist, is not evidence but material—not proof but provocation.

That this distinction needs to be restated at all is itself symptomatic. Henk Slager and the European Artistic Research Network (EARN) have recently argued that artistic research has entered a "post-research condition," in which the initial struggle for academic legitimacy—won over the past decade—has paradoxically resulted in its subsumption under what Slager calls "the dominant trend of academisized research that has reigned in art now for at least ten years." The danger, as he articulates it in the editorial to *MaHKUscript* vol. 4 (2020), is explicit: "Today as well the danger lurks that 'artistic research' will be encapsulated by academic routines and institutional stereotypes which will seriously impede creativity and artistic reflection."[^1] The post-documentary methodology I am proposing should be understood, in part, as a response to this condition: not another bid for disciplinary recognition, but a deliberate insistence on the specificity of artistic operations within and upon the archive—operations that produce knowledge irreducible to historical, sociological, or computational paradigms, even as they draw on all three.

## The Subject Is Not the Subject

Let me be precise, even provocative: Black Mountain College is not the subject of the work *Retcon Black Mountain*. The college—its history, its figures, its documented achievements—functions rather as a pretext, a gravitational field around which something else can form. What interests me is the derivative: the parallel artefacts, the entropic dispersal, the question of how we might collectively represent a partial heritage that institutions have already framed as "utopia."

I conducted interviews with BMC scholars, experts, and the last surviving members—Basil and Martha King, in their New York apartment—treating these encounters with all the seriousness the protocol demands: prepared questions, recording equipment, the full apparatus of oral history. But the approach was aesthetic, not historical. I was not gathering testimony to reconstruct what happened. I was generating material for operations the historical record cannot authorize.

The distinction matters. When the historian interviews a witness, the goal is extraction: to retrieve information that can be verified, contextualized, added to the archive. When the artist interviews, the encounter itself becomes artefact. The grain of the voice, the hesitations, the apartment's afternoon light, the distance between what is remembered and what is sayable—these are not noise to be filtered but signal to be composed. The interview does not document Black Mountain College; it produces a new object that relates to Black Mountain College through derivation rather than representation.

Recent research at Tate, undertaken within the collaborative project *Reshaping the Collectible: When Artworks Live in the Museum* (2023), provides a theoretical framework for precisely this operation. The project defines a category of "living archives"—artworks that, "on the instruction of the artist, or by processes that are inherent to the way the artwork exists, generate additional—usually documentary—material at each activation or display." The artwork creates documents intentionally, leaving traces or remains; the generative archival material "contextualises the artwork and roots it in the history of its production, exhibition, display and—in some cases—audience interactions." Crucially, this material "is typically understood to be a component of the artwork; an output of the way it works, the way it functions, and should not be used in place of the artwork for exhibition."[^2] The interview with Basil and Martha King, in this light, is not a document *about* Black Mountain College filed alongside others; it is a component of a new artwork that generates its own archival trace—one that will, in turn, become material for subsequent operations. The archive does not precede the work; the work produces its archive, which produces further work. What Aga Wielocha, building on Siegfried Zielinski's concept of "AnArchives," has theorized as the "(An)archive"—"an open-ended set with a rhizomatic structure" in which "the identity of a contemporary artwork is distributed between physical objects and processes, and concepts and contexts, which shape an artwork throughout its career"[^3]—describes with precision the condition under which *Retcon Black Mountain* operates. The boundary between artwork and documentation is not blurred by negligence but dissolved by method.

But this formulation, however theoretically elegant, does not fully resolve the ethical tension it contains. To say that the Kings' interview is a "component of a new artwork" rather than "testimony" is to perform a reclassification that the Kings themselves did not authorize. They agreed to be interviewed as witnesses to a historical experiment; they did not agree to become material in a speculative one. The (An)archive framework argues that the artist's intervention expands the ontological life of the subject rather than extracting from it—but this is the artist's framing, not the subject's. There is a structural asymmetry here that no amount of theoretical sophistication can dissolve: the artist composes; the witness is composed. Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley's insistence on positional accountability—forcing the audience to declare who they are before accessing the archive—exposes exactly this problem. And Stephanie Syjuco's "rogue archivist" practice, for all its critical force, operates within the same tension: to "talk back" to the colonial archive is also to talk *over* the subjects it contains. The post-documentary practitioner can make this tension visible—can ensure that the operations performed on archival material remain legible, that the sources are traceable, that the derivation is transparent. But transparency is not consent, and legibility is not accountability. This is a shadow the methodology carries, not a problem it has solved.

This is why the institutional framing of BMC as "utopia" must be contested—not because it is false, but because it forecloses. Utopia is nowhere, by definition. It cannot be continued, only commemorated. But what if we approached BMC not as a lost ideal but as an unfinished transmission, a signal still propagating, degraded by entropy but not yet extinguished? The work then becomes: how do we receive what was never fully sent? How do we represent collectively—which is to say, how do we make present again, together—a heritage that exists only in partial, scattered, institutionally overdetermined fragments?

## Affect as Hypothesis

What distinguishes artistic methodology from historical method is not a lack of rigor but a different orientation toward what counts as knowledge. The historian asks: *what happened?* The artist asks: *what does this do to me, and what can I do with that?* This is not solipsism. It is the recognition that affect—the pre-personal intensities that move through bodies and situations—constitutes a legitimate domain of inquiry, one that requires its own forms of precision.

When I encounter Josef Albers' pedagogical notes, or Anni Albers' weaving diagrams, or the fragmentary traces of communal meals and chance encounters, I am not primarily interested in reconstructing what occurred at Black Mountain College between 1933 and 1957. I am interested in what these traces activate: the sense of possibility they carry, the pedagogical imaginaries they suggest, the futures they still contain. This activation is not arbitrary. It can be described, analyzed, compared with other activations. It produces knowledge—but knowledge of a different order than historical knowledge.

The hypothesis of artistic research is always, in some sense, affective: it proposes that certain configurations of material, certain arrangements of attention, will produce particular effects. These effects are not merely subjective. They are intersubjective, transmissible, revisable through practice. When I claim that Black Mountain College's experimental pedagogy resonates with contemporary questions about AI-assisted creation, I am making a hypothesis that can be tested—not through archival verification but through the production of situations in which that resonance becomes palpable or fails to.

I should be candid about what "fails to" means here, because this is where the methodology is most vulnerable. Historical knowledge is evaluated through peer review, verification, and the weight of convergent evidence. Affective knowledge, as I am describing it, is evaluated through resonance—through the capacity of a produced situation (a workshop, a collaborative comic, an installation) to generate in its participants a recognition that something has been transmitted, activated, made present. But resonance is not verification. A workshop can produce intense collective affect and still be producing nothing more than group enthusiasm. A collaborative comic can feel like a reactivation of Black Mountain's pedagogical spirit and still be merely a pleasant exercise in nostalgia. The intersubjective is not the objective, and the transmissible is not the true.

The concept of "continuous prototype" (*jatkuva prototyyppi*), developed by Tero Heikkinen, Petri Kaverma, and Denise Ziegler within EARN's methodology working group, offers not a solution but a reframing: defined as "a conceptual method that enables to formulate the way artistic research practices work in the fields of fine art and critical design,"[^10] the continuous prototype does not prescribe how to do artistic research but points to possible conditions to fertilize it. If the artistic output is conceived as an ongoing experiment rather than a final truth—a working model of a sensation rather than a statement of fact—then the evaluative criterion shifts from "is this knowledge valid?" to "does this prototype generate further productive operations?" A prototype that produces nothing—no further derivations, no new configurations, no continuation by others—has failed, not because it was "wrong" but because it was inert. A prototype that generates unexpected continuations—that catalyzes work the original researcher did not anticipate—has succeeded, not because it was "right" but because it was generative. This is a real evaluative criterion, and it can be applied with rigor. But I will not pretend it is equivalent to historical verification. It operates in a different register, and anyone who presents a doctoral thesis grounded in affective hypotheses will, at some point during the defense, face the question: *but how do you know?* The honest answer is: I don't know in the way you mean. I know in a way that the methodology I am proposing is still learning to articulate—a way that involves the body, the collective, the situated encounter, and that resists translation into the propositional form that academic evaluation demands. This resistance is not a deficiency of the methodology—it is its condition. It is also, inseparably, its vulnerability.

But how can affect constitute knowledge? The objection is predictable, and it has recently found a productive response in what might be called absential aesthetics—a theoretical current emerging from work on art produced in the aftermath of historical erasure. Soko Phay, professor of contemporary art theory at the Université Paris 8 and director of the laboratory "Arts des images et art contemporain," has developed through her ongoing research on "l'art devant l'extrême"—from *Cambodge, l'atelier de la mémoire* (2010) through *Archives au présent* (2017) to the forthcoming *Cambodge, l'art devant l'extrême* (2025)—a sustained argument for the aesthetic mediation of historical absence. When history is erased, as under the Khmer Rouge regime that destroyed nearly all of Cambodia's archival and cultural infrastructure, the artist's role is not to reconstruct what was lost but to document the shape of what is missing—to give form to absence as a positive aesthetic substance. The "apartment's afternoon light" in my interview with the Kings, the grain of their voices, the distance between what they remember and what they can say—these are not merely atmospheric details to be composed but instances of what Phay's framework would identify as *active absence*: knowledge derived precisely from the formal archive's failure to capture the whole truth. The affect is not subjective noise; it is the signal of what the archive could not retain, and its documentation through artistic means constitutes a form of knowledge production that no other methodology can authorize.

This affective orientation toward the archive—where traces activate rather than merely attest—finds a compelling parallel in the work of Thao Nguyen Phan (b. 1987, Ho Chi Minh City), whose first monographic exhibition in France, "Le soleil tombe sans un bruit," was presented at the Palais de Tokyo (June 12–September 7, 2025). Phan's practice operates precisely at the threshold between what happened and what the traces of what happened can still generate. In *Reincarnations of Shadows (moving-image-poem)* (2023, Pirelli HangarBicocca, Milan; 2024, Kunsthal Charlottenborg, Copenhagen; 2025, Palais de Tokyo, Paris), she approaches the figure of Vietnamese modernist sculptor Diem Phung Thi not through biographical reconstruction but through what she calls a "multilingual tale"—a split-screen poem in which archival fragments, personal memory, and speculative narration become indistinguishable. The work is, in the terms developed here, an affective hypothesis: it proposes that certain configurations of the Diem Phung Thi archive—her sculptures, fragments of interviews, the visual textures of postcolonial Vietnam—will produce a particular kind of knowledge about intergenerational transmission between women artists. Phan does not reconstruct Diem Phung Thi's life; she tests what that life can still activate. The resulting film is "more poem than biopic," treating moving images as what Phan describes as "a cascade of reincarnations"—each frame a derivative of the previous, each screening a new activation. Her broader practice, from the watercolors painted over pages of the Jesuit Alexandre de Rhodes's *Voyages de Rhodes* (1653) to the jute installation *No Jute Cloth for the Bones* (2019–2023) recalling the great famine under Japanese occupation, consistently refuses what she has called the "collective amnesia" imposed by official history. Instead, she combines "memory, folklore, and myth to create collective narratives as an alternative to the official historical accounts imposed by dominant political forces." The archive, for Phan, is not a repository of facts but a site where hidden symbols and rituals—those that have "remained in the shadows, passed down through whispered oral narratives"—await reincarnation through aesthetic practice.

## Entropy and Collective Representation

Every archive undergoes entropy. Documents decay, memories fade, contexts become illegible. The historian fights this entropy through conservation, contextualization, and institutional maintenance—slowing the degradation, preserving the signal. The artist takes a different position: entropy is not only loss but transformation. What degrades also mutates. The partial, the fragmentary, the incorrectly transmitted—these are not failures of the archive but its derivatives, carrying information about the processes of transmission themselves.

Black Mountain College closed in 1957. Its physical campus was sold, subdivided, left to weather. Its participants dispersed, aged, died. What remains? Boxes in libraries, oral histories recorded decades after the fact, photographs of people no longer alive in buildings no longer standing, and—crucially—an institutionalized narrative that frames the whole as "utopia," as unrepeatable experiment, as historical parenthesis safely closed.

But entropy also produces artefacts. The degraded signal is still a signal. The misremembered anecdote carries information about what was memorable enough to misremember. The institutional framing of BMC as utopia is itself a derivative, telling us something about what contemporary institutions need BMC to have been. To work with these derivatives is not to abandon historical accuracy but to shift the object of inquiry: from *what was Black Mountain College?* to *what has Black Mountain College become, in its dispersal, and what can it still become?*

The emerging field of Computational Archival Science (CAS) offers an unexpected but productive point of articulation here. Defined since its formalization in 2016 as "a transdisciplinary field grounded in archival, information, and computational science" concerned with "the application of computational methods and resources" to "large-scale records/archives processing, analysis, storage, long-term preservation, and access problems,"[^4] CAS applies text mining, network analysis, and machine learning to large-scale archival datasets, uncovering patterns and connections that traditional methods cannot detect. Through ten successive workshops at the IEEE Big Data Conference (2016–2025), CAS researchers have developed increasingly sophisticated tools for what they describe as the extraction of meaning and knowledge from quantity—the transformation of archival mass into legible structure. For the historian, these tools serve verification and contextualization: they accelerate the identification of patterns within known categories. But for the artist working with entropic archives, computational methods open a different possibility: what we might call *void detection*—the systematic identification of archival silences, phantom presences, structural absences that the archive's own organizational logic renders invisible. When I speculate about a "Black Mountain College for the age of artificial intelligence," the computational dimension is not merely metaphorical. The 5,500-document research corpus assembled for this project—letters, photographs, administrative records, secondary literature—constitutes a dataset whose patterns and gaps can be mapped computationally, revealing not only what the archive contains but what it systematically excludes: the unrecorded meals, the undocumented conversations, the pedagogical experiments that left no paper trail. The "degraded signal" and the "unfinished transmission" become, in computational terms, statistically significant absences—voids whose shape can be described even when their content cannot be recovered.

This is why the work must be collective. A single researcher can study BMC; only a collective can represent it—in the sense of making present again. Representation here is not mimesis but reactivation: bringing into presence something that exists only as trace, potential, entropic remainder. The interviews, the workshops, the collaborative comics, the technical infrastructures—these are not documentation of research but the research itself, attempts to discover what forms of collectivity the BMC transmission can still catalyze.

## Precedents: Document as Speculative Instrument

This methodology has precedents, though not a school. Walid Raad's Atlas Group works provide perhaps the clearest articulation: an archive of the Lebanese civil wars composed of documents that are "hysterical"—factual and fabricated, found and produced, their authenticity beside the point. Raad does not falsify history; he demonstrates that the documentary apparatus itself produces fictions, that the question is not whether documents lie but what truths their lying reveals. The Atlas Group interrogates not only Lebanon but the conditions under which Lebanon becomes legible to international audiences—the political economy of testimony, the institutional hunger for authentic suffering, the complicity of the viewer who wants documents to be true.

The Otolith Group extends this into what Kodwo Eshun has called "speculative documentation." In works like *Otolith I* and *II*, archival footage of nonaligned movements, Soviet space programs, and Third World futurisms is recomposed into essays that treat the past as a repertoire of unrealized futures. The document here is not evidence of what happened but index of what did not—the futures that were imagined, organized toward, and then foreclosed. History becomes a site of counterfactual retrieval, and the audience is positioned not as witness to the past but as heir to its abandoned possibilities.

Hito Steyerl, working from a technofeminist position, has theorized and practiced what she calls the "poor image"—the degraded, compressed, circulated copy that loses resolution but gains velocity. The poor image is an entropic artefact, bearing the traces of its transmissions. Steyerl's films treat documentary materials as already compromised, always partial, constitutively unreliable—and therefore available for operations that pristine documents would resist. The methodology acknowledges that we never access the archive directly but only through layers of mediation, institutional framing, and technological degradation. To work with poor images is to work with what remains after extraction, after the high-resolution original has been locked away or lost.

What these practices share is a refusal to let the document dictate the terms of its interpretation. The document becomes instrument rather than evidence—a tool for speculating on conditions larger than what it depicts: the geopolitics of visibility, the epistemology of testimony, the audience's desire for unmediated access to the real. In each case, the work interrogates not only its ostensible subject but the apparatus through which subjects become available for interrogation. And in each case, the audience is implicated: positioned not as neutral receiver of information but as participant in the political economy of documentation itself.

If these established practitioners articulate the post-documentary methodology through completed bodies of work, a younger generation has extended these operations into computational and participatory registers, often working from within the very colonial archives that structured the conditions of their own visibility. Rose Salane (b. 1992), working with objects acquired from MTA auctions—lost rings, counterfeit tokens, liquidated inventory—submits bureaucratic detritus to multiple, incompatible systems of evaluation: pawnshop appraisals, mitochondrial DNA analysis, psychic readings. The archive here is not institutional but accidental, produced by the aggregation of anonymous losses. Salane's method demonstrates that documents need not preexist: they can be assembled from the entropy of circulation, then subjected to speculative operations that reveal the systems—economic, biological, spiritual—through which value is assigned and contested.

Danielle Brathwaite-Shirley (b. 1995, London; lives and works between Berlin and London) builds interactive video game environments that function as archives for Black trans lives—"those living, those who have passed, and those that have been forgotten." Their practice emerges from a founding gesture: the discovery of an 1836 poster depicting Mary Jones, a Black trans woman reduced to a cruel caricature labeled "Man Monster." This erasure catalyzed the creation of the *Black Trans Archive* (2020, accessible at blacktransarchive.com)—not a conventional database but a first-person video game, built with Black trans developers and coders, where the archived material is inseparable from the interface that delivers it. The aesthetic, deliberately *low-fi*—inspired by 1990s PSX graphics—refuses immersive seduction in favor of what Brathwaite-Shirley calls "capturing a feeling rather than a smooth, polished piece of art." But the archive is not neutral: upon entry, players must identify themselves as Black and Trans, Trans, or Cis, and this identification determines what they can access and what labor they are asked to perform—the cisgender player is confronted with their own privilege and historical responsibility rather than with Black trans trauma. In *SHE KEEPS ME DAMN ALIVE* (2021), a lightgun shooter in which moral clarity is deliberately destabilized; in *GET HOME SAFE* (2022), an interactive diary of harassment experienced walking home at night in Berlin; in *THE SOUL STATION* (2024), presented at Berlin's Berghain, a post-revolutionary multiplayer world where progress depends on collective strategy and trust; and in *The Delusion* (2025), a solo exhibition at Serpentine North in London (through January 2026)—at each iteration, the audience is not witness but complicit, the archive produces not knowledge but accountability. The work *I Can't Remember A Time I Didn't Need You* (2020) was acquired by the V&A in 2022, and the *Trans & Conditions* commission for CIRCA (2025) extended the practice into public space—Piccadilly Lights in London, open letters sent directly to MPs, the archive delivered to Downing Street.

If Brathwaite-Shirley turns the video game against archival erasure, Mélanie Courtinat (b. 1993), working from Paris, turns it against its own ideological conventions. She takes the formal structures of game design—tutorials, side quests, NPCs, save points—as raw material for speculative subversion. Where Brathwaite-Shirley imposes an ethical position on the player from the title screen, Courtinat draws them into an apparently familiar ludic contract only to reveal its coercive premises. In works like *The Siren* (2024) and *Indulto* (2025)—shown together in the solo exhibition "Faites vos jeux, rien ne va plus" at Stereolux, Nantes, and the same year at the Frac Île-de-France, the Louvre-Lens, and the Kunstmuseum Bern—players navigate hyperrealistic environments only to discover that the rules governing their actions are arbitrary or falsely premised. The tutorial, that pedagogical apparatus meant to transmit the game's logic, becomes a site of ideological exposure; the boss fight reveals itself as "voluntary servitude." The two practices share a conviction: the video game is not a neutral medium but a document—in Brathwaite-Shirley's case, a document of the erasure of Black trans lives; in Courtinat's, a document of the conventions through which contemporary subjects are trained to accept authority, repetition, and the fiction of meaningful choice.

This question of accountability—of who gets to enter the archive and under what conditions—takes a different form in the work of Stephanie Syjuco (b. 1974, Manila). Where Brathwaite-Shirley constructs an archive that enforces positional accountability upon entry, Syjuco enters existing institutional archives as what the Smithsonian has called a "rogue archivist," mining colonial-era collections to reveal the structures of visibility and invisibility they enforce. Her ongoing project, the *Rogue Finding Aid*, constitutes what she describes as "a parasitic image collection that mines and rephotographs institutional archives to center the visual representation of Filipinx Americans for collective research"[^5]—a parallel, institutionally unfaithful database that cuts across multiple archives, reframing their contents through the critical lens of the colonized subject. In the series *Block Out the Sun* (2019), Syjuco rephotographs images of Filipinos taken at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, interposing her own hands over the faces of the exhibited subjects—not to destroy the photograph but, as she puts it, "to question its ability to be seen as a neutral image of foreign peoples." The gesture is simultaneously protective and speculative: it withdraws the colonized body from the extractive gaze while producing a new image that indexes the relationship between colonial photographer and contemporary artist-descendent. Syjuco's broader practice—from the self-portraits of *Cargo Cults* (2016), in which she poses in mass-produced "ethnic" fabrics with dangling price tags, to the installation *Dodge and Burn (Visible Storage)*, crowded with printed-at-actual-size archival images mounted on laser-cut wood—treats the colonial photographic archive not as evidence to be cited but as material to be "talked back to," subjected to operations of détournement, occlusion, and speculative recomposition that expose the imperial apparatus embedded in the documentary form itself.

The institutional archive and its technologies of classification are also the site of Gala Porras-Kim's (b. 1984, Bogotá; 2025 MacArthur Fellow) sustained inquiry, though her operations move in yet another direction—not the détournement of colonial images, as with Syjuco, nor the subversion of interactive systems, as with Brathwaite-Shirley and Courtinat, but what might be called speculative conservation: the disciplined reimagining of what objects in museum collections were meant to become. Porras-Kim's research-intensive practice—informed by, among others, a fellowship at the Museo delle Civiltà in Rome in collaboration with the MAO in Turin (2023–25)—focuses on artifacts separated from their original contexts—Mayan ritual offerings dredged from the Sacred Cenote at Chichén Itzá and held at Harvard's Peabody Museum, ancient vessels and ceremonial instruments locked in museum storage, fragmented objects whose provenance and function remain contested. Her method is epistolary as well as sculptural: she writes letters to museum directors proposing interventions that the institutions' own conservation protocols cannot authorize. For the project *Precipitation for an Arid Landscape* (2021–23), she rehydrated a slab of copal from the Peabody archives with a drip of water every thirty seconds—symbolically returning the material to its ceremonial aquatic context. The gesture is post-documentary in the precise sense outlined here: it does not document what the artifact was but speculates on what it was meant to continue doing, treating the museum's conservation regime as itself a form of entropy that the artwork can work with rather than against. Her recent exhibition *The categorical bind* (Sprüth Magers, London, 2025) and her participation in the Liverpool Biennial (2023) and the Centro Andaluz de Arte Contemporáneo in Seville (*Vistas más allá de la tumba*, 2023) confirm the European grounding of a practice that now circulates between Los Angeles, London, and the continental museum network.

The inversion from archive-as-source to archive-as-product finds a particularly charged articulation in the digital space through the work of Seumboy Vrainom :€, a figure who operates at the intersection of artistic practice, militant pedagogy, and computational dissemination. Self-described as a "militant Hors-Sol" (above-ground militant) and "apprenti chamane numérique" (apprentice digital shaman), Seumboy—trained at the École des Beaux-Arts d'Angoulême and Sciences Po's SPEAP program—launched *Histoires Crépues* in 2020, a platform that approaches French colonial history through what he calls "period sources, commented and augmented." The operation is explicitly post-documentary: Seumboy does not present himself as a historian ("dans ma première vidéo j'ai précisé que je ne l'étais pas et je le dis toujours quand on me le demande"[^6]) but rather positions himself in "la présentation des sources, pour qu'on voie que je ne sais pas exactement quoi en tirer mais que j'y donne accès et que j'en propose une certaine lecture."[^7] This methodological transparency—showing the sources while admitting the uncertainty of their interpretation—is precisely the protocol of sourced speculation described in this text. But Seumboy's practice extends beyond commentary into the production of what he calls "les images qui nous manquent": using AI-generated imagery and mixed-media techniques to create "des images aux dimensions historiques, qui ressemblent à de l'archive mais qui sont une archive qu'on n'a pas, que je n'ai jamais eu l'occasion de voir, des scènes qui n'ont pas été captées, notamment des scènes de résistance à la colonisation."[^8] The work asks a question that resonates directly with the post-documentary condition: "Est-ce que je veux perpétrer des représentations auxquelles j'ai accès, via ces archives occidentales ? Ou alors est-ce que je veux tenter de créer de nouvelles textures d'images, de nouveaux styles graphiques innovants qui nous permettent d'imaginer des archives qui n'auraient pas été des archives coloniales ?"[^9] Here the derivative operation is radicalized: speculation is not merely *sourced from* the archive but directed toward the production of an alternative archive, one that indexes the absences and foreclosures of the colonial documentary apparatus itself.

## Primary Sources and Secondary Operations

Working with primary sources transforms their status. The historian treats the document as testimony, to be interrogated for what it reveals about its moment of production. The artist treats the document as score, to be performed in the present. This is not a metaphor. The archival document, in artistic research, functions less like evidence than like a musical notation: it prescribes certain operations while leaving others open to interpretation.

Let us return to Black Mountain College, and consider the difference between citing a letter from Charles Olson—poet, essayist, and last rector of the college from 1951 until its closure in 1956—to Robert Creeley, and reactivating that letter within a contemporary practice. The citation fixes the document in its historical moment, draws meaning from context, establishes authorial intention. The reactivation extracts the document from its moment, tests its propositions against present conditions, allows it to mean otherwise than its authors intended. Both operations are legitimate. Neither is superior. But they produce different kinds of knowledge and require different criteria of evaluation.

This is where the notion of *sourced speculation* becomes operative. Speculation, in artistic research, is not fantasy. It is the disciplined extension of archival material into configurations that the archive does not directly authorize but that remain tethered to what the archive contains. The sources provide not proof but constraint: they limit the field of speculation, give it texture, prevent it from dissolving into pure projection. When I speculate about what a "Black Mountain College for the age of artificial intelligence" might look like, I am not inventing freely. I am deriving—in the mathematical sense—propositions that follow from the archived material without being contained in it.

But the mathematical metaphor, precise as it sounds, conceals a difficulty. If the derivative describes the rate and direction of change, it requires a function to differentiate—a continuous, describable relationship between variables. Archival material does not behave like a function. It is discontinuous, full of gaps, shaped by accidents of preservation and destruction. The "tether" between source and speculation is not a mathematical relationship but something more precarious: part material (the physical properties of the document—its texture, its damage, its institutional provenance), part structural (the logic of the archive's organization, what it places next to what), part affective (what the encounter with the document activates in the researcher's body and practice). These three registers do not always pull in the same direction. A material tether might demand fidelity to the object's degradation; a structural tether might demand fidelity to the archive's classificatory logic; an affective tether might demand fidelity to a resonance that neither materiality nor structure can account for. When Walid Raad produces a "hysterical document," the source becomes a pretext whose material and structural specificity matters less than the operations it authorizes.

I cannot pretend that "sourced speculation" names a unified operation. It names a family of operations whose common feature is the refusal to cut the tether entirely—to maintain some traceable relationship between what the archive contains and what the work produces—while acknowledging that the nature of that relationship varies with each practice, each archive, each encounter. The risk of this honesty is that "sourced speculation" begins to look less like a methodology and more like a disposition: a commitment to working *with* rather than *from* or *about* archival material, without a stable protocol for determining when the working-with has drifted into working-despite. This is not a problem I have solved. It is a problem the work inhabits.

## The Post-Documentary Condition

Documentary practice, in its classical form, claims to show what is—though Olivier Lugon reminds us, through his analysis of Walker Evans, that the "documentary style" is as much a matter of reception as of capture: "Art is never a document, but it can adopt that style," wrote Evans, and it is precisely this stylistic adoption, this construction through the gaze and the institution, that Lugon restores in all its historical complexity.[^11] The document documents: it attests, certifies, proves. Post-documentary practice begins from the recognition that this attestation is itself a construction, that documents are made to document, and that the making can be extended, continued, diverted. The "post" here is not temporal (after documentary) but structural (beyond the constraints of documentary's epistemological claims).

In post-documentary methodology, the archive becomes generative rather than merely preservative. Primary sources serve as seeds for derivative works—not in the financial sense of derivation, where value is extracted from an underlying asset, but in the sense of calculus, where the derivative describes the rate and direction of change. What is changing, when we derive from archives? The relationship between past and present, between what was recorded and what becomes possible, between the dead letter and the living practice.

This derivation requires its own forms of citation, its own protocols of accountability. When speculation is sourced, the sources must be identifiable even when they are transformed. The reader or viewer of post-documentary work should be able to trace the lines of derivation back to their archival origins, not to verify historical claims but to understand the operations that produced the work. Transparency here serves not truth but legibility: it makes the method visible, available for critique and continuation.

Isabelle Barbéris's recent work on what she terms "silent censorships" (*Censures silencieuses. L'autocensure culturelle : pourquoi et comment*, PUF, 2024) provides a useful counter-pressure to this argument. Barbéris, writing from within the French institutional landscape, has analyzed how new forms of normative control operate in cultural production—not through overt prohibition but through preemptive self-censorship, where "the conditions of possibility of certain cultural creations" are foreclosed before they can emerge. Her broader critique, developed across *L'Art du politiquement correct* (2019) and *Panique identitaire* (2022), warns that when artistic practice is subsumed under social or political mandates—whether identitarian, managerial, or technocratic—it loses the capacity for what she calls aesthetic resistance: the power of art to think otherwise than the categories available to it. The warning is pertinent to artistic research more broadly: the post-documentary methodology risks becoming another orthodoxy if it collapses into prescriptive formula rather than remaining a generative protocol. More specifically, "post-documentary" risks becoming a brand—a way of signaling institutional belonging rather than performing actual operations on actual archives. The distinction between the post-documentary and documentary is not a hierarchy; it is a description of different operations, each with its own rigors and its own risks. The post-documentary practitioner who forgets this collapses into the very mimicry—now mimicry of the avant-garde rather than of the historian—that the methodology was designed to resist.

## Transdisciplinary Without Transcendence

Artistic research methodology is often described as transdisciplinary, and this is accurate but requires qualification. Transdisciplinarity does not mean floating above disciplines, borrowing freely from each without accountability to any. It means working *through* disciplines, subjecting oneself to their constraints in order to discover what exceeds them.

My engagement with Black Mountain College necessarily involves historical research: reading secondary literature, visiting archives, learning the institutional and biographical contexts. But this historical work is instrumental rather than terminal. It prepares the ground for operations that history alone cannot authorize. Similarly, the project involves engagement with aesthetic theory, critical pedagogy, media archaeology, and computational systems—not as a dilettante sampling surfaces but as a practitioner accepting the demands each field makes.

What makes this transdisciplinary rather than merely multidisciplinary is the production of something that none of the constituent disciplines could produce alone: a methodology that treats affect as hypothesis, archives as scores, speculation as sourced derivation. This methodology does not transcend its component disciplines but emerges from their interference patterns, from the places where their protocols contradict and their objects refuse to align. The concept of "continuous prototype" developed by Heikkinen, Kaverma, and Ziegler[^10]—artistic outputs conceived as ongoing experiments rather than final truths—captures this condition precisely: the post-documentary work is never finished because its object (the BMC transmission, the degraded signal, the entropic remainder) is itself in continuous transformation. Each workshop, each collaborative comic, each computational operation on the archive produces not a result but a new configuration from which further derivations become possible.

## The Stakes of Distinction

Why insist on the distinction between artist and historian? Because the confusion produces bad history and bad art. When artists pretend to historical authority they do not possess, they make claims that cannot withstand disciplinary scrutiny and lose the freedom that artistic practice provides. When artists abandon the archive entirely in favor of pure invention, they lose the productive constraint that sourced speculation requires. The post-documentary methodology I am describing depends on maintaining the distinction clearly enough that both historical rigor and speculative freedom can operate without mutual contamination.

This is not a call for artists to leave archives to historians. On the contrary: artistic engagement with archives opens possibilities that historical methodology forecloses. But these possibilities become available only when we stop pretending that artistic research produces historical knowledge and begin articulating what it does produce: affective hypotheses, derivative operations, scored reactivations, sourced speculations. A science, if we want to call it that—but a science of what is not yet rather than what has been.
