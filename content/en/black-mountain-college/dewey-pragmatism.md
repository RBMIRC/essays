## Black Mountain College

The narrative that follows is not a history of Black Mountain College. Histories exist—Duberman's (1972), a foundational oral inquiry; Harris's (1987), a systematic inventory of artistic practices; Díaz's (2015), a reassessment of pedagogical experimentalism; Cometti and Giraud's (2014), the first French-language synthesis; Silver's (2024), a reconstruction of the college's agrarian economy; *Black Mountain College as Multiverse* (2022), a cartography of BMC's pedagogical legacy in contemporary art schools—not to mention accounts by those who lived it from the inside: M.C. Richards, Fielding Dawson, Francine du Plessix Gray, Jonathan Williams, Michael Rumaker, among others, whose testimonies constitute an archive in their own right. These works form the factual foundation of the present text. What follows draws on them but does not extend them. This is neither a historiographical synthesis nor an institutional monograph: it is a *retcon*.

The term requires immediate explanation. "Retcon" is a portmanteau of *retroactive continuity*, coined by Roy Thomas in 1983 within the editorial apparatus of Marvel and DC Comics [^1]. It designates a technique for managing contradictions accumulated in serialized narrative universes: when decades of stories by multiple authors produce irreconcilable chronologies, the retcon reconfigures—it rearranges established facts into a new constellation that accommodates both the established past and the demands of the present narrative. The provenance is no accident: as Joshua Clover has shown, the retcon names a temporal logic in which subsequent events determine the meaning of prior ones [^2]. The concept is developed from other angles—epistemological, legal, computational—in several satellite texts of this dissertation, to which readers are referred.

Applied here, retcon is the method I propose for rereading BMC from Eden—from a post-industrial territory, a \$1,000 house, and the tools of contemporary research-creation (*recherche-création*—a methodology, primarily developed in Francophone contexts, that treats artistic practice as a mode of knowledge production), including artificial intelligence. This rereading does not claim to correct existing historiography. It claims to *fork*: to create a divergent branch that follows trajectories the dominant mythology has occluded—Goldowski's cybernetics rather than Cage's happening, Molly Gregory's structural labor rather than Rauschenberg's *Combines*, the desegregation debate rather than Fuller's geodesic dome. The fork does not supplant; it proliferates.

Readers are therefore forewarned: the historical narrative in Section I is a retcon. It does not lie—the facts are documented, the sources cited—but it selects and arranges according to a retroactive logic. It is because I initiated this long-term project in Eden with the inaugural conceptual act of purchasing a nineteenth-century workers' house, because I have conducted conditions of co-creation and *Summer Institutes* there for ten years, because I read the BMC archive through a corpus of over 10,000 documents processed by computational tools, that certain facts appear in this narrative and not others. The retcon does not claim objectivity; it makes explicit what every historical reconstruction does implicitly—choosing from the present what the past means.

Four operations structure this method. The *fork* preserves alternative trajectories against their absorption into dominant narratives. *Anchor reinforcement* returns to the specific, the dated, the contradictory—against cultural memory's tendency to smooth particularity into type. *Counter-convergence* attends to what canonization excludes. The *halting condition* decides how far reactivation can extend without becoming pure invention—it is the archive itself, and the computational work on the corpus, that provide the constraint. The historical narrative that follows puts these operations to the test.

That this method borrows its operations from serial fiction—a product of the industrial management of intellectual property—in order to work on a territory undone by capitalist restructuring is not a paradox to be neutralized but a constitutive condition of the practice. The retcon does not claim to operate outside the systems it critiques. It raises questions of private property and collective appropriation in a processual movement that navigates between the two. As Josef Albers told Martin Duberman during an interview for what would become the first BMC exegesis: "Don't become a machine for adding up dates and factual facts... produce *actual* facts. That's my terminology. It means giving statements and formulations that lead further. 'Actual': it is still 'in act'" [^3].

### Foundation (1933)

Black Mountain College was born of a schism. In the spring of 1933, John Andrew Rice—a classics professor who had been expelled from Rollins College, Florida, for persistent insubordination and pedagogical nonconformity—decided that expulsion was itself the lesson: if the existing university could not tolerate genuinely experimental teaching, a new institution had to be created from scratch. Rice was joined by several Rollins colleagues who had resigned in solidarity and by Theodore Dreier, a physicist who would serve as treasurer, fundraiser, and institutional memory for the next twenty years.

Over the course of a single summer, a charter was obtained from the state of North Carolina, \$14,500 raised, and a campus identified in the vacant buildings of the YMCA Blue Ridge Assembly near Asheville. The college opened in September 1933 with some twenty students—at least sixteen of them recruited by Nan Chapin, the first to enroll—and a dozen faculty who had agreed to work for room and board (by 1935–36, the average salary would be \$819 [^4]). The community gathered around Robert E. Lee Hall, the vast central building of the Blue Ridge campus that housed classes, meals, and meetings. The guiding principle was, in Duberman's phrase, "a modified Marx: from each according to his ability, to each according to his (elementary) needs" [^5].

The institution challenged the foundations of the American university: professional administration, fraternities, grading systems, degree conferral. A *Board of Fellows* [^6], composed of six elected faculty members and one student representative, constituted the governing body; decisions were made by consensus rather than vote. An advisory board of eminent figures—including, over the years, Walter Gropius, John Dewey, and Franz Kline—possessed only the power of persuasion. There was neither a board of trustees nor an endowment. The arts occupied the center of the curriculum, in keeping with the first catalog: "dramatic arts, music and the fine arts, which often exist precariously on the fringes of the program, are regarded as an integral part of the life of the college and of equal importance with the subjects that usually occupy the center of the college program" [^7]. Education was conceived as a total environment, the boundaries between formal instruction and informal exchange deliberately blurred.

Daily life translated these principles. Classes were held in the morning (eight-thirty to twelve-thirty), always in small groups of seven or eight. Afternoons were left free for manual labor, sports, and hiking. Classes resumed from four to six. Evening seminars bringing together several faculty members were held at eight "in order to have all the time to follow an idea" [^8]. A practice specific to BMC, the "interlude," consisted of an unannounced suspension of all classes for a week. One of the longest discussions of the first year concerned eliminating Sunday lunch to give the kitchen staff a rest; the entire college split into "two-meal" and "three-meal" factions, the dispute lasting hours [^9]. The episode revealed a structural truth: the pursuit of consensus demanded an investment of time and energy disproportionate to the issue at hand. It was these ordinary frictions of direct governance, far more than moments of genius, that constituted the real texture of life at BMC. Francine du Plessix Gray, who studied at the college during the summer sessions of 1951 and 1952, summed up the experience in a formula: she worked in the fields and washed dishes with the faculty, and they taught her rebellion—"a tension, a dialectic between rebellion and community" that structured her entire writing life [^10].

### Periodization: Five Phases, Two Sites

The history of BMC can be divided into five distinct periods, marked by two sites and several modes of governance. This periodization is a conventional decision and one choice among others: one could just as well divide the college's history by the role of women in the institution (from Nan Chapin to Molly Gregory, from Anni Albers to M.C. Richards), by the evolution of the place of nature and agricultural labor (from the inaugural question "*Why not raise chickens?*" to the farm's collapse), or by the production of artifacts and documents (from the first catalog of 1933 to Creeley's *Black Mountain Review*). Each of these divisions—many others would be possible—would make visible articulations that conventional chronological segmentation flattens. It is, moreover, one of the narratological methods of *Retcon Black Mountain* to explore these different situations vis-à-vis the usual canonical narrative.

First period (1933–1938): Blue Ridge, foundation. The college occupied the leased buildings of the Blue Ridge Assembly, organized around the vast Robert E. Lee Hall where classes, meals, and meetings converged. Rice was rector *de facto*. Enrollment: twenty to sixty students. The Alberses arrived in November 1933; Dewey visited in 1934–35. This was the period of pioneerism and shared poverty.

Second period (1938–1941): schism and relocation. Tensions between Rice and the rest of the faculty led to his sabbatical (1939) and then his definitive resignation (1940). Meanwhile, the college purchased the Lake Eden property in 1937—667 acres, an artificial lake, fourteen buildings including two large lodges and cottages—for \$35,000, thanks largely to a \$12,500 donation from former student Stephen Forbes. Gropius and Breuer's plans, which envisioned an integrated architectural ensemble, were abandoned for lack of funds (Dreier estimated the cost at \$500,000); the community decided to build the campus itself according to the more modest plans of A. Lawrence Kocher. The move (1941) was also a change in social structure: at Blue Ridge, communal life converged on Lee Hall; at Lake Eden, the dispersed buildings favored specialization and reduced informal interactions.

Third period (1941–1949): the Albers era at Lake Eden. Albers dominated; he assumed the rectorship in 1948. Dreier managed finances. The community organized around the opposition between a "European" bloc (Albers, Goldowski, Dehn, Guermonprez, Jalowetz, Schlesinger) and an "American" bloc (Levi, Richards, Wallen). The summer institutes began in 1944. Wallen attempted to reformulate governance through group dynamics (1945–1947). Goldowski introduced cybernetics (1948). The schism of 1949—departure of the Alberses, Dreier, Levi, Richards—was the most serious crisis in the college's history.

Fourth period (1949–1953): transition and refoundation. Goldowski, Trayer, and Pittenger ensured immediate survival. This was the period of the most celebrated summer institutes: Fuller (geodesic dome), Cage (*Theater Piece No. 1*, 1952), Cunningham, de Kooning, Rauschenberg. Permanent enrollment fell to about twenty students. Olson, who had arrived in 1948, assumed the rectorship in 1953.

Fifth period (1953–1957): Olson's rectorship and dissolution. The college recentered on writing and poetry. Enrollment fell to nine students by the end of 1954. The *Black Mountain Review* (1954–1957, ed. Creeley) became the principal vehicle of literary influence. Material conditions were extreme. In 1957, the college closed without ceremony.

Collective physical labor constituted a thread running through all five periods. In October 1933, Dave Bailey posed the inaugural question at lunch: "*Why not raise chickens?*" [^11] Within a week, a chicken coop was built—without prior consultation—and within three weeks, a twenty-five-acre farm had been acquired [^12]. From this farm grew the *Build Program* and the *Work Program*—two to three hours of manual labor per day, deducted from tuition, materially linking the pedagogical economy to the college's productive economy [^13]. Under Molly Gregory's direction, the college reached its highest degree of food self-sufficiency during the war. The farm's collapse after 1949 was inseparable from that of the institution: by 1954, the community was in food crisis. The arc of the farm—from the self-managing enthusiasm of 1933 to the collapse of 1954—constitutes the best material indicator of BMC's trajectory.

### The Deweyan Framework

John Dewey's influence on Black Mountain College was direct and personal. John Rice, the college's initiator and founder, had long admired Dewey's work and described him as the only man "perfectly able and willing to live in a democracy" [^14]. Dewey visited the college twice during the 1934–1935 academic year, bringing his friend Albert Barnes on one occasion. His posture was that of the observer: "he did not visibly enthuse," a student noted, "gently discouraging efforts to treat him as Grand Philosopher" [^15]. It was only in a letter to Theodore Dreier dated July 18, 1940, as the college was settling on the Lake Eden campus under precarious financial conditions, that Dewey offered his most frequently cited assessment: "The work and life of the College (and it is impossible in its case to separate the two) is a living example of democracy in action. [...] The College exists at the very 'grass roots' of a democratic way of life" [^16].

For Rice, Dewey's teaching was above all a lesson in method: "arriving at a conclusion, it was not arriving at a conclusion, it was arriving at a pause. And one looked at the pause, one looked at the plateau, and then one saw another thing to climb" [^17]. The pedagogical philosophy rested on Dewey's foundational insight: to know is to do. Without the dimension of doing, knowledge remains empty—it fails to attain its true power as a means of inquiry. BMC's pedagogy refused the transmission of "cartloads of badly understood knowledge, because it is remote" in favor of experimental engagement with materials and problems. As Charles Olson wrote in a 1951 letter, BMC chose "that education as knowing (as history) be interconnected with the arts and sciences, and that it be in connection with the active work undertaken by active women and men."

Governance was democratic in the strong sense: faculty and students participated together in decisions, consensus was preferred to voting, and students learned democracy by practicing it—what Jérôme Duwa calls a "hyper-democracy" [^18] that was not without its conflicts and exhaustions. Art occupied the center of the curriculum, not as ornament but as the site where experimental intelligence could be most intensely cultivated. The integration of intellectual and manual labor—the farm, building maintenance, cooking—was not merely an economic necessity (though the college was always poor) but an educational philosophy. As Rice specified: "Between the vanquished who have surrendered to the public world and the invincible who will never surrender, there are those who are beginning to wonder why they surrendered."

Yet the Deweyan framework carried limits that BMC could not perceive. Dewey's "associated living" presupposes a functional community capable of democratic deliberation. But what happens when the economic basis of association is systematically dismantled? This question—that of *dis-association*—will be the subject of Part II, which submits Deweyan pragmatism to a dialectical examination: what Dewey provides, what he cannot provide, and what survives this critique to enter the project's methodology.

### The Albers Pedagogy

In November 1933, Josef Albers arrived with his wife Anni—both former Bauhaus teachers, refugees from Nazism, recommended by Philip Johnson, then director of the architecture department at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. Albers spoke no English; the college could not guarantee its own existence beyond a year. Anni was intrigued to see notices nailed to the columns of Lee Hall, which appeared to be made of stone; they were wood. First lesson: American things were not always what they seemed.

The Alberses developed a method they called "starting at zero" [^19]. Anni: "We always start from what exists today and try to explain it. Whereas I tried to pose a problem, to put the students at absolute zero, in the desert, in Peru. There is nothing. What is the first thing one must think about?" [^20] Josef brought the Bauhaus *Werklehre*: direct engagement with materials—paper, wood, metal—favoring inexpensive materials so that preciousness would not inhibit experimentation. The question was not "what can I make with this?" but "what can this become?" Courses were built around juxtaposition: "making a raw and dismal burnt sienna as alive and bright as gold by 'working on its neighbors'" [^21]. Albers drew political lessons from this: "a symbol of the community spirit, of 'live and let live,' of 'equal rights for all'" [^21].

The rigor was not merely technical. The privilege granted to spontaneity and self-expression—consecrated in progressive education—was Albers's bête noire. True expression was transcendent, acquired over a lifetime. "A painter should have a clear mind and a right eye." "The tradition in art is to create, not to revive; if we were to revive, we would still be in caves" [^22].

But the figure that mythology has transmitted smooths over contradictions that the archives restore. Rice distinguished "three Alberses—the teacher, the social being, and the Prussian" and claimed he had "never been able to discover the slightest connection between them" [^23]. Dan Rice dropped the course: "that heavy Germanic pedantry. He seemed so—stiff" [^24]. These contradictions matter: what is mythologized is a smoothed Albers, but the archive contains an Albers who provoked real resistance, and it is this resistance that makes the archive a material for reactivation rather than a model for imitation.

Mexico played a decisive role. It was only during their first trip in 1935 that the Alberses felt at home in their new world. Josef discouraged pilgrimage to Europe and encouraged travel to Mexico—a return to the fundamental elements of design and the most basic human needs.

### Erased Figures, Neutralized Virtualities

BMC's mythology has retained a handful of names—Cage, Cunningham, Fuller, Rauschenberg, de Kooning, Olson—that function as indexes of American experimentalism. But this canonization erases figures whose role was structurally just as important to the college's intellectual life. Max Dehn, a mathematician of world renown, a refugee from Nazism, taught mathematics, Plato, and ethics; his grave lies on the campus grounds. Natasha Goldowski, a physicist trained at the Sorbonne, had worked on the Manhattan Project; it was she who, in 1948, led a seminar using the proofs of Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics*. John Wallen, a psychologist trained at Harvard, undertook to reformulate governance in terms of group dynamics—a project that Duberman considers one of the most revealing episodes in BMC's history [^25]. Paul Goodman, whose writings would influence the New Left, provoked a communal crisis that laid bare the limits of the college's professed tolerance. Among women, Anni Albers, Ruth Asawa, and Dorothea Rockburne are the only ones to have achieved institutional recognition—Asawa and Rockburne belatedly, and always at the margins of the male canon. M.C. Richards was dismissed in the 1949 schism as "packaged" with her husband. Trude Guermonprez took over weaving instruction. Johanna Jalowetz taught voice training and bookbinding—"some students took her class simply because they loved her and wanted her to feel useful" [^26]. Gregory, Chapin, the farmers Weston and French: BMC's pedagogical, productive, and affective infrastructure rested on figures the canon has rendered invisible. The violence of this invisibilization was not merely retrospective. Francine du Plessix Gray, who had studied with Olson during the summers of 1951–1952, recalled him pressing his fingers into her skull until it hurt, to "get the snob Yurrup out of her head"; he called her writing "pure shit" and ordered her to publish nothing for ten years—advice she followed [^27]. The poet Hilda Morley, who taught from 1952 to 1956, described the discomfort she felt passing "Olson's boys" in the dining hall, whose glances signaled that she was a "dubious character" [^28]. BMC's pedagogical freedom coexisted with gendered power dynamics that the mythology of experimentation has covered over.

Three structural factors confirm this invisibilization. The first is gender. As the preceding examples show, BMC's pedagogical, productive, and affective infrastructure rested on women the canon has rendered invisible (the exhibition *Question Everything! The Women of Black Mountain College*, BMCM+AC, 2020, brought this role to light by showing that during World War II, numerically overrepresented women effectively ran the college)—and this invisibilization was not merely retrospective but active, inscribed in the college's daily power relations. The second is race. BMC was situated in the segregationist South—Theodore Dreier wrote to the college's attorney that they were a "Yankee island in a Southern sea"; a neighbor had told him he was twenty years old before "learning that *damn* and *Yankee* were two separate words." The institution had a local reputation as a refuge for communists, nudists, and advocates of racial integration. Fires were set in the campus woods by locals who disapproved of its policies. The debate over admitting Black students lasted from 1933 to 1945: no North Carolina law forbade desegregation in higher education, but local pressures were real. The question returned with greater force in 1943, when Clark Foreman, committed to integrated education, made the admission of Black students and faculty a central axis of his mandate at the college. The board continued to consider itself favorably disposed in principle but opted in practice for caution—the perceived danger to the institution's survival outweighing any real effort not only to welcome Black individuals but to transform the college's ethos so they would feel welcome [^29]. The 1944 compromise—admitting a single Black student, Alma Stone Williams, qualified and older, not as a "student" but as a "member of the summer Institute"—was described by Eric Bentley as a *Hofjude* (the "court Jew"), a formula all the more stinging in that it targeted faculty who were themselves refugees from Nazism [^30]. The first full-time Black student, Sylvesta Martin, was admitted in the fall of 1945; by 1947, five Black students were enrolled and the college publicly declared that admission would be "open to all students of all races." But Percy Baker, a Black biologist who taught at BMC, testified that the college was "one of only two places in my life where I was unconscious of race," while other testimonies show that integration remained superficial and that recruitment persisted as a failure [^31]. As Dorothee Richter summarizes, BMC's notion of equality "positioned itself as the equality of white men" [^32].

The third factor is the *GI Bill* (Servicemen's Readjustment Act, 1944). After the war, veterans flooded into higher education thanks to federal funding. BMC benefited directly: enrollment increased, new buildings were installed using military surplus, the government provided a printing press, furniture, and typewriters, and the college acquired its first properly lit library. This funding temporarily made viable an institution that refused accreditation—but created a fatal dependency. When Veterans Administration inspectors demanded evidence of normal operations—regular classes, degrees, stable enrollment—BMC could not provide them. Olson, summoned repeatedly to Raleigh to justify classes that ran from midnight to dawn and sessions that ended "when he felt the class ought to end," miraculously persuaded the inspectors each time. Approval was first delayed, briefly granted, then definitively withdrawn.

These erasures are not accidental. Goldowski's cybernetics, Wallen's governance, women's structural labor, the desegregation debate, the paradox of federal funding—these are precisely the virtualities that the "genius"-centered mythology has neutralized. The retcon method I propose aims to put these virtualities back into circulation—not to correct the canon but to fork: to open narrative branches proper to the BMC narratives and their echoes in contemporary imaginaries of experimental pedagogy, utopian community, and the laboratory of the real.

### The *Summer Institutes* (1944–1952)

The *Summer Institutes*, which began in 1944, constituted BMC's principal vehicle of outreach. During these intensive six-to-eight-week sessions, the permanent community—which rarely numbered more than seventy or eighty students during the academic year, and sometimes far fewer—expanded with the arrival of visiting artists, thinkers, and faculty; perspectives collided, disciplines intersected. It was in this framework that what would later be called the American neo-avant-garde took shape.

The list of participants constitutes an index of mid-century American experimentalism: John Cage, whose *Theater Piece No. 1* (1952) established the coordinates of performance art; Merce Cunningham, who developed there the choreographic methods foundational to postmodern dance; Buckminster Fuller, who attempted his first large-scale geodesic dome; Willem de Kooning, Jacob Lawrence, Robert Motherwell, Beaumont and Nancy Newhall, and Harry Callahan among the faculty; Robert Rauschenberg, Cy Twombly, Kenneth Noland, and Ruth Asawa among the students.

The photography program illustrated BMC's distinctive approach to artistic training. Unlike the Institute of Design in Chicago or the California School of Fine Arts, which trained generations of professional photographers through rigorous technical programs, photography instruction at BMC "radicalized the conditions of teaching photography by replacing the prescriptive set of technical exercises offered in eminent photography departments with a program based on freedom and collaboration" [^33]. As Harry Callahan, who taught during the 1951 summer session, reflected: "At the Institute of Design, well, I thought it was the only way (to teach, Ed.). That's all I knew. And then when I went there, I saw this other way." Asked about the possibility of teaching photography, Callahan proposed: "I don't think you can teach someone to be creative, but I think you can put them in an environment that will allow it" [^34].

Hazel Larsen Archer, the only dedicated photography instructor at BMC from 1949 to 1953, articulated the program's philosophy: "We make many mistakes, many, but they serve to remind us that we constantly aspire to something, and we are even more amazed by this wonderful and mysterious process of making an image" [^35]. The program functioned less as professional training than as an extended workshop that broadened the photographer's vision through close collaboration and experimentation with visual and performing artists. Several BMC photography students—Stan VanDerBeek, Robert Rauschenberg—became acclaimed artists whose work situates photography in a relational field that blurs the boundaries between media.

The summer of 1952 merits particular attention. The *Untitled Event* staged by Cage combined piano music, phonograph records, film projection, dancing by Cunningham, Rauschenberg's *White Paintings*, and poetry read by Olson and M.C. Richards. Testimonies diverge on numerous details—Cage may have read a lecture on Zen Buddhism or on Meister Eckhart, and perhaps also the Bill of Rights or the Declaration of Independence. This documentary imprecision, regrettable from a strictly historiographical standpoint, preserves the event from retrospective reductions and inscribes it in BMC's fundamental disposition: organizing the conditions of the unforeseeable.

While the literature emphasizes Cage's central role, he himself acknowledged that theatrical experimentation "was in the air." What emerged from those summers was neither a style nor a school but an epistemic disposition: treating artistic conventions as hypotheses rather than laws, working across disciplinary boundaries, conceiving doing as a form of thought. Steven Henry Madoff describes this disposition as a "pedagogy of the hinge": a displacement of the articulations of the conventional educational order in the service of a porous and convivial interdisciplinarity, "a dynamic of horizontalizing practices" [^36].

It would be wrong, however, to make BMC a place devoted solely to the arts. Numerous disciplines, including the sciences, were taught there. The fundamental experience, for students and faculty alike, was above all political. Taking BMC into account as an American experience that displaced issues that had first been, *mutatis mutandis*, those of the Bauhaus, also enables a rereading of American art history by questioning the dominant narratives founded on the late emergence of an original painting in the post-World War II period, with Abstract Expressionism and the heroicized figure of Jackson Pollock. The occlusion of Black Mountain College would explain why French readers waited so long for a first synthesis on this total experience.

### Dissolution (1953–1957)

The college closed in 1957. The immediate causes were financial: falling enrollment, accumulating debts, the cost of maintaining the property. The deeper causes were structural: democratic governance generated paralysis as the community fractured into factions. Charles Olson, who led the college in its final years, attracted some and repelled others with the radicalism of his vision. The remaining faculty were committed but exhausted.

What endured after closure: an archive distributed across multiple institutions (Western Regional Archives in Asheville, Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center, Stanford, Yale, MoMA, Getty Research Institute, Smithsonian, Albers and Rauschenberg foundations, private collections), a mythology that amplified as former students achieved renown, and an open question about the meaning of the experience.

Black Mountain College succeeded and failed simultaneously, daily, in realizing its ideals. Dewey's *learning by doing* was not a pedagogical slogan there but a material necessity: students and faculty themselves built the Lake Eden campus buildings from 1941 onward, fabricated some of the furniture, maintained the farm that supplied part of the food, and repaired the roads. Education through doing was inseparable from the institution's poverty—one learned by building because there was no money to hire builders. This indistinction between pedagogy and material survival was both the college's strength and its fragility: the vitality of the learning experience came at the cost of a chronic inability to resolve ideological disagreements and personality conflicts among faculty. The frequent conflicts and schisms and the departures that resulted were losses the college could ill afford. Attempts to hire a professional administrator invariably led to discussions about a marketable and predictable program, and the faculty was unwilling to sacrifice the community's vitality for the possibility of longevity. As Mary Emma Harris writes: BMC was not a perfect form but an idealistic and fragile adventure, "more an organism than an institution, changing, volatile, evolving, intense" [^37].

Duberman, whose 1972 work constitutes the founding narrative, offered a sharper judgment. In its best moments, BMC showed what a disparate group of individuals committed to a common enterprise could accomplish; in its worst moments, the community amounted to little more than a group of "squabbling prima donnas" [^38]. For some, the experience was bitter, confirmation of Emerson's dictum that "we descend to meet"—close human association aggravating rather than erasing the will to power and cruelty [^39]. Fielding Dawson, a student from 1949 to 1953, condensed the ambivalence in a formula that resists all nostalgic recuperation: BMC was a "wide open heavenly nightmare" where he was free, "but. BUT. Being free, like immature persons in prison, I didn't want to learn" [^40]. This frankness matters: it refuses the retrospective consolation that treats institutional failure as the necessary price of genius.

### The Problem of Retrospective Construction

What precedes—the historical narrative in its details, contradictions, and erased figures—is the material. But inheriting this material requires understanding what has happened to it between the college's closure and the present moment. The retrospective construction of BMC is not a bias to be corrected: it is the problem to which the retcon responds.

BMC's utopia, with its flaws and lacunae, makes it possible to interrogate pedagogy in general and art pedagogy in particular. But the interrogation must extend to the very image of BMC that has been transmitted to us—an image constructed as much by academic discourse and nostalgic desire as by historical evidence. The college that appears in retrospective accounts is not identical to the one that existed; between the two lies the work of mythologization, canonization, and selective memory.

Black Mountain College entered cultural memory through a specific process. Martin Duberman's *Black Mountain: An Exploration in Community* (1972), already mentioned here, established the dominant narrative: a utopian experiment destroyed by its own contradictions but redeemed by its artistic progeny. But Duberman himself was aware of the constructed character of his enterprise: he considered his book "still one individual's response" to BMC, not the last word but "*his* word" [^41]. And more radically: the college's full history is more complex than a recitation of famous names—it is the story of a small group of unknowns who tried to find consonance between their ideas and their lives [^42]. The book appeared at a moment when the counterculture was seeking historical antecedents and art-world institutions were seeking legitimating genealogies for post-minimalism and conceptualism. BMC answered both needs perfectly. It offered an American narrative of principled dissent, experimental community, and artistic innovation—a usable past for the early 1970s. Significantly, Duberman himself at times departs from academic methodology and constitutes himself a fictive member of BMC, participating in reinvented meetings—as though bare fact could not render the truth of the moment, as though historiography itself had to resort to a form of retcon to reach what the archive alone cannot restore.

By 1972, many former BMC students had achieved fame: Rauschenberg and Twombly in painting, Cunningham in dance, Cage in music, the Black Mountain poets in literature. Their success retroactively consecrated the college that had nurtured them. The institution that had closed in obscurity and debt was reconstructed as a hothouse of genius. This reconstruction served multiple purposes: it provided artists with a pedagogical genealogy superior to mere academic training; it provided institutions with proof that unconventional education could produce conventional success; it provided historians with a manageable case study for theorizing the relationship between art and education.

But the reconstruction was precisely that—a reconstruction. The BMC that emerges from post-1970s scholarship is shaped by what its famous former students became, not by what the college was when they attended it. The narrative runs backward: knowing that Rauschenberg will become Rauschenberg, we read his student work as anticipation rather than experience. Knowing that Cage's *Theater Piece No. 1* will be retroactively designated "the first happening," we treat the summer of 1952 as origin rather than incident. The college becomes a theater of emergence, its daily life significant only insofar as it prefigures later developments.

This retrospective construction has consequences for any attempt to inherit from BMC. What is inherited is not the college itself but the college as it has been processed by academic discourse, museum exhibitions, anniversary celebrations, and the testimonies of participants who have themselves been shaped by the mythology. The BMC that appears in catalogs such as *Starting at Zero* (2005) or *Black Mountain College: Experiment in Art* (2002) is an organized, selected, and arranged object, governed by principles that were not operative in 1933 or 1945 or 1952.

The nostalgia that structures much of BMC's reception merits particular examination. Nostalgia presupposes loss: something precious existed and is no more. The nostalgic relation to BMC treats the college as a lost totality—a moment when art and life, education and democracy, experience and community were unified in a way that is no longer ours. This treatment occludes several facts. First, the college never achieved the totality ascribed to it; conflicts, failures, and compromises were constant. Second, the "loss" of BMC is not simple absence but transformation—the college's dissolution dispersed its energies into other institutions, practices, and discourses. Third, nostalgia for BMC often serves to critique present conditions (the entrepreneurial university, the professionalized art world) by contrasting them with an idealized past—a rhetorical operation that uses history as a weapon rather than approaching it as a problem.

BMC's academic construction also raises the question of who benefits from the mythology. Art schools invoke BMC to legitimize experimental pedagogy while operating under conditions (accreditation, tuition revenue, measurable outcomes) fundamentally incompatible with what BMC actually practiced. Museums organize exhibitions on BMC that treat ephemeral experiences as collectible objects, transforming the anti-institutional into the institutionalized. Scholars produce dissertations and monographs that convert living practice into academic knowledge, generating careers from the analysis of an experience that refused careerism. None of this is illegitimate, but it is worth noting: the BMC that circulates in contemporary discourse is a BMC made usable for institutions that the historical college would not have recognized. M.C. Richards, who taught at BMC from 1945 to 1951 and chaired the faculty, was the first to identify this danger. When Harper and Row proposed she write a book about the college, she suddenly became aware that "Black Mountain College (whatever it was) risked being formulated as an institution and sold to the public—a fate it had scrupulously avoided during its lifetime" [^43]. She was also largely discouraged by Josef Albers and Theodore Dreier, as evidenced by their correspondence preserved at the Getty Research Institute in Los Angeles. Richards abandoned the project and passed her research notes to Duberman, who used them for his own book (Taylor, 2023) [^43]. Her refusal is instructive: it signals that transforming BMC into an object of discourse is itself an operation of power, and that the inner voices—those of Dawson, of du Plessix Gray, of Richards herself—resist this operation precisely because they carry the experience of what cannot be formulated.

Any attempt to "return" to BMC—to replay it, to revive it, to draw lessons from it—must reckon with this constructed character. Dorothee Richter observes that "the attitude of disobedience is inscribed in the myths and ideological frameworks of Black Mountain College" [^44]—which means that transgression itself has become an institutionalizable legacy, a symbolic capital mobilizable by institutions that have nothing transgressive about them. What presents itself as historical recovery is always also projection: we find in BMC what we bring to it, what our present needs demand that we find there. The risk is not that we will fail to understand BMC but that we will understand it too well—that we will produce a BMC perfectly adapted to our ends, a mirror rather than an archive. Against this risk, critical historiography offers partial protection. By attending to the gaps, contradictions, and failures that mythology smooths over, we can at least complicate the utopian image and resist its most interested uses.

The question is not whether BMC was a utopia—a question that can only be answered "no" if by utopia we mean perfection, "yes" if we mean a place organized around principles its members believed worth pursuing. The question is what work the designation "utopia" performs in contemporary discourse. Calling BMC utopian often amounts to placing it beyond critique (utopias fail; that is what utopias do) while simultaneously extracting lessons from it (utopias teach; that is what utopias are for). This double operation—exemption from critique coupled with availability for appropriation—characterizes much of BMC's reception. It allows us to admire the college without examining too closely what it actually did, to inherit from it without asking what inheritance means. Madoff formulates this tension precisely: BMC offers "a retrospective horizon of nostalgic potentiality and an inquiry toward prospective rehabilitation" [^45]. It is precisely in this gap between retrospection and prospection that the retcon method operates.

### The Archive as Constraint

The first three operations of the retcon—*fork*, *anchor reinforcement*, *counter-convergence*—are opening operations: they multiply trajectories, details, figures. Left to themselves, they would produce unbridled proliferation—the retcon would become pure invention, indistinguishable from fiction. The fourth operation, the *halting condition*, introduces the inverse constraint: the retcon reconfigures given facts; it does not fabricate them. The halting condition is the resistance of the archival material to reconfiguration, the moment when the archive says no.

This constraint is not abstract; it has an infrastructure. The project rests on a computational ecosystem built between 2020 and 2025, each component of which plays a specific role in the retcon's discipline.

The starting point is the visual archive—what the project calls *The Dining Hall*. Named after the BMC refectory, the site of non-hierarchical communal life, it gathers over 10,000 documents from research in BMC archival collections: photographs, exhibition catalogs, reproductions of works, architectural plans, illustrated press clippings, digitized documents. The sources are distributed across multiple institutions—Western Regional Archives in Asheville, Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center, collections at Stanford, Yale, the Getty Research Institute, the Smithsonian, the Albers and Rauschenberg foundations, and private collections. The *Dining Hall* is primarily visual: it is the project's image collection, processed by keyword extraction, thematic classification, and modeling tools, producing a semantic index that enables cross-cutting queries impossible in archival classifications organized by collection, date, or author. The technical architecture is political. The platform operates on blockchain, peer-to-peer (OrbitDB protocol on IPFS): each document can be *forked*—duplicated, annotated, reconfigured—while preserving proof of authenticity from the initial deposit. Files are not hosted on a central server but in the users' own browsers. The centralized archive—the collection deposited in an institution—reproduces the canon's hierarchy: what is cataloged is visible, what is not disappears. The *Dining Hall* distributes authority: no single institution is the sole guardian, adding documents does not require centralized validation, and the fork guarantees that recombining archives is possible without destroying the original. It is an archival management system that is itself a political proposition—the same proposition that the BMC refectory embodied spatially: a place where no one presides and where sedimentation is collective.

The second element is *The Studies*—the set of textual documents related exclusively to BMC: correspondence, *Board of Fellows* minutes, unpublished memoirs, administrative reports, course programs, as well as the published works devoted to the college—Duberman, Harris, Díaz, Cometti and Giraud, Silver, the published testimonies of Richards, Dawson, du Plessix Gray, and Rumaker. No images and no sources external to BMC: *The Studies* is the strictly textual and strictly BMC-related corpus from which Theodore's "voice" is constructed. Where *The Dining Hall* uses the fork as its operation (duplicating, reconfiguring while preserving the original), *The Studies* uses *commentary*: participants inscribe in the dated documentation their annotations, questions, and associations. The archival document remains intact; what accumulates around it is a layer of collective, time-stamped reading that sediments around the original text without modifying it. It is this sedimentation—the grain of the BMC archives traversed by the voices of those who read them today—that produces Theodore's training corpus.

The third element is *The Library*—the annotated bibliography of the research at large. Unlike *The Studies*, which contain only documents about BMC, *The Library* organizes all the project's secondary and tertiary sources—critical theory, philosophy, cultural studies, urbanism, contemporary art—into a structured, annotated ensemble that constitutes the dissertation's critical apparatus.

The fourth element is *Theodore*—a computational entity enriched by *The Studies*. Theodore functions as a counter-index: where the archival catalog classifies by presumed importance, Theodore distributes by frequency and co-occurrence—which amounts to reading the archive without the canon's filter. Asking Theodore "who worked on the farm?" produces an answer that mixes Gregory, French, the Quakers, and names that canonical historiography has not retained—not because the system "knows" something Duberman did not, but because the corpus's statistical distribution does not reproduce the hierarchy of cultural memory. In this sense, Theodore *detokenizes*: it restores to archival fragments a continuity that archival tokenization (classification by collection, call number, author) had broken, producing unforeseen proximities between documents that institutions preserve separately. But Theodore is not merely a consultation tool. It is the scenario engine of *Retcon Black Mountain*—not because it writes the scenario in place of humans, but because it is capable of simultaneously managing different streams of co-modification of the BMC narrative. The scenario is created humanly, collectively, with the situated singularities of each participant—practitioners (*praticien·nes*), residents, researchers. Theodore is the infrastructure that allows these parallel streams to coexist without reducing one to another: it maintains the corpus's coherence while accommodating concurrent rewrites, contradictory narrative branches, simultaneous forks. The narratives it produces are neither faithful to the archive (it hallucinates, it associates, it drifts) nor independent of it (it can only produce what *The Studies* contain). It is this position—between knowledge and fabulation, between document and narrative—that makes Theodore an instrument of retcon rather than an instrument of research.

These four components—*Dining Hall*, *Studies*, *Library*, *Theodore*—play distinct roles in the halting condition. The *Dining Hall* provides visual memory—what the archive gives to *see*. *The Studies* provide textual memory—what the archive gives to *read*, and it is this corpus that constrains Theodore's voice. *The Library* provides the interpretive framework—what research has produced as knowledge beyond BMC. Theodore reveals in *The Studies* patterns that cultural sorting has rendered invisible—but it can only reveal what the corpus contains. Together, these tools provide the retcon with its discipline: reactivation is free in its reconfigurations but constrained by what the corpus attests. The retcon can fork, reinforce, counter-converge—but it cannot cross the line beyond which the archive ceases to support interpretation. It is this line that the halting condition traces.

## Eden and The Little Red Schoolhouse

### 1,000 Dollars

In 2015, I initiated the Eden project, whose inaugural act was the acquisition of an 1884 wooden house for \$1,000, in a town of 15,000 in the United States: Eden, North Carolina. The approach was programmatic: it was the local real estate price—the collapse of values in counties classified as *economically distressed*—that directed me toward this town. The figure indexes not the singularity of a good deal but the *ordinariness* of abandonment: the economic system produces \$1,000 houses by the hundreds across the American South. The amount resulted from a symbolically charged negotiation with the seller, anchoring the acquisition from the outset in a logic of co-sharing rather than speculation.

The house was a product of the 2008 financial crisis, at least by its collateral effects. It had not necessarily been directly foreclosed, but the wave of foreclosures following the subprime crisis depressed the entire real estate market in already weakened counties: cascading depreciation of neighboring properties, growing municipal disinterest in maintaining impacted neighborhoods, a *shrinking cities* logic where public infrastructure contracts as the tax base erodes. It had passed through the hands of several owners, each unable to maintain it. The "cheap" house was not cheap; it was a contract of continuous expenditure, a commitment of resources to a territory that offered no return on investment. Paradoxically, the acquisition triggered renewed interest from public authorities in bringing the property into compliance—municipal injunctions, rehabilitation deadlines, inspections—where the same building had been ignored during years of vacancy. This regulatory zeal was not neutral: it effectively directed renovation expenditures toward private companies linked to the city council, turning code compliance into a mechanism of local economic circulation. The house thus became, despite itself, a micro-dispositif of redistribution: money invested in its rehabilitation fed the residual economic fabric of a contracting town.

The structure itself carried a history older than its dilapidation suggested. Research revealed that the house, built entirely of wood, had been erected in 1884 and had served as the town's first public school—literally the PS1 of Eden, in immediate proximity to the now-abandoned factory. It had witnessed the humble period of industrialization, when the mill villages of Leaksville and Spray were forming around the first spinning mills. Governor Luther Hodges (1898–1974), son of a textile worker from Spray, who entered a factory as an office boy at the age of twelve before becoming Governor of North Carolina (1954–1961) and then United States Secretary of Commerce under Kennedy and Johnson, had likely attended school there [^46]. Hodges—who was also the architect of Research Triangle Park and is buried in Eden—embodies the trajectory that textiles made possible when the economy still functioned: from the workers' classroom to the ministerial cabinet, in a single generation.

The stakes were never the house itself but the territory that produced it—its geography, its textile economy, its deindustrialization, its persistence after capital's withdrawal. To approach America through private property and observe it on a time scale that neither the residency nor the documentary visit allows. The approach is post-documentary: watching the Medusa protected by the living room window. Calvino, in *Six Memos for the Next Millennium*:

> To cut off Medusa's head without being turned to stone, Perseus supports himself on the very lightest of things, the winds and the clouds, and fixes his gaze upon what can be revealed only by indirect vision, an image caught in a mirror. [...] Perseus's strength always lies in a refusal to look directly, but not in a refusal of the reality in which he is fated to live; he carries the reality with him and accepts it as his particular burden [^eden1].

The house is the bronze mirror; the territory is the Gorgon.

[^eden1]: Italo Calvino, *Six Memos for the Next Millennium*, trans. Geoffrey Brock, Boston, Mariner Books, 2016 [1988], p. 4–6.


### Geography

Eden, North Carolina, is situated in Rockingham County, in the northern Piedmont region, near the Virginia border. The town of approximately 15,000 was formed in 1967 through the merger of three older communities: Leaksville, Spray, and Draper. The name "Eden" was chosen by referendum during the consolidation. It does not directly refer to the biblical garden but to William Byrd II, a Virginia planter who surveyed the Virginia–North Carolina border in 1728 and named his 20,000 acres in the Dan River valley "The Land of Eden." Byrd dreamed of attracting Swiss Protestant settlers to found a self-sufficient colony—a dream that never materialized: at least one ship sank, and the rare survivors never reached their destination. The choice of name in 1967 thus unwittingly reactivates an aborted colonial utopia—a toponymic irony the project will make retroactively visible.

The town lies just under 300 kilometers from the Lake Eden campus of Black Mountain College. Let me be clear: the connection between the two is constructed. Eden and BMC share no common history; no textile supply chain passed through Asheville, no college student came from the Piedmont mills, no archival document mentions one in the context of the other. Mountain North Carolina (BMC) and Piedmont North Carolina (Eden) are culturally distinct regions. The link I propose is a retcon in the precise sense of the term: a retroactive continuity that exists only because a subsequent gesture—the purchase of the house in 2015 and the project that followed—brought it into being. It is a gesture that belongs as much to conceptual art as to historical research: positing that two places successively assembled and then abandoned by capital share a structural condition, and making this condition the terrain of a practice. The question is not whether the link is "real"—every retcon is real insofar as it produces effects—but what it allows us to think that the separation of the two narratives forbids.

### The Textile Economy

Eden's development followed the pattern of Southern textile industrialization, whose genealogy must be recalled. The Piedmont's textile industry did not arise from nothing: it directly inherited from the plantation economy built on slavery. The cotton that fed the first mills was cultivated by enslaved people; in 1860, nearly four million enslaved persons lived in the Southern states, and cotton cultivation—intensified by the invention of the cotton gin in 1793—had been the primary engine of slavery's expansion across the Piedmont from Georgia to Texas [^47]. After the Civil War, abolition did not end extraction: the sharecropping system and crop-lien debt kept Black families—and a growing share of poor white families—in structural dependence on cash crops, cotton and tobacco, that fed precisely the merchants who became investors in spinning mills [^48]. The transition from cotton field to textile factory was not a rupture: it was a transfer of the logic of extraction into a new dispositif—the mill town—that reproduced in wage form part of the plantation's control structures (company housing, company stores, fierce resistance to any labor organizing). As Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts summarized as early as the 1850s, the textile industry rested on an alliance between "the lords of the lash and the lords of the loom" [^49].

It was in this context that Northern capital established factories across the Carolina Piedmont from the late nineteenth century onward, attracted by cheap labor, weak unions, proximity to raw cotton, and abundant waterpower. The territory that would become Eden was a site of this convergence between textile industry and chemical innovation. On May 2, 1892, in the village of Spray, the Canadian inventor Thomas L. Willson accidentally discovered the commercial process for manufacturing calcium carbide in an electric arc furnace—a discovery the American Chemical Society would recognize as a *National Historic Chemical Landmark* [^50]. Calcium carbide, which produces acetylene on contact with water, became the basis of industrial lighting, oxyacetylene welding, and later an entire branch of synthetic organic chemistry. The first commercial carbide plant, built by local entrepreneur James Turner Morehead between 1894 and 1896, gave rise to a lineage of companies, the best known being Union Carbide Corporation. The Willson plant burned in 1896; on its exact site was built the Spray Cotton Mill—the waterwheel from the chemical plant powered the mill into the 1970s. Eden thus carries in its very geography the superposition of two extraction regimes: textiles and industrial chemistry, both made possible by the waterpower of the Smith and Dan Rivers.

The Smith and Morehead families—the same Moreheads involved in the carbide discovery—built the textile mills along these rivers, producing towels, sheets, and other household textiles. By the mid-twentieth century, the textile industry dominated the local economy. Fieldcrest Mills (later Fieldcrest Cannon, then Pillowtex) became the largest employer; the mill-town model—factories, company housing, company stores, company-sponsored institutions—organized social life around industrial production.

This economy produced a specific form of life. Workers lived in mill villages—modest houses within walking distance of the factories. Shifts structured time; the whistle marked the beginning and end of the workday. Churches, schools, and recreational facilities served populations defined by their relationship to the mills. Labor organizing, when it occurred, met fierce resistance; the paternalistic model of labor relations preferred grateful workers to organized ones.

### Deindustrialization

The textile economy that built Eden also abandoned it. To measure the scale of the collapse, one must recall the scale of what existed. In 1940, 40% of North Carolina's jobs were in textiles and apparel; the state was then the nation's largest textile employer. Employment reached its historical peak in 1973 with 293,600 workers in Carolina textile mills—more than a quarter of the national textile workforce [^51]. In the 1960s, American factories still produced 95% of the household linens and apparel consumed domestically. In Eden itself, the eight interconnected mills along the Smith and Dan Rivers—the Fieldcrest complex alone employed more than 3,000 people—made the town a link in the productive chain that structured the entire Piedmont [^52]. The process of decomposition was gradual—decades of closures, layoffs, mergers and acquisitions—but the direction was unambiguous. Production moved first to lower-wage sites within the United States, then abroad to Mexico, Central America, and Asia. NAFTA (1994), then China's accession to the WTO (2001) accelerated what automation and globalization had already begun. Between 1996 and 2006, North Carolina went from 233,715 to 80,232 textile jobs—a 65% drop in ten years [^53]. By the early 2000s, the mills that had defined Eden for a century were closing: Pluma in 1999, Spray Cotton Mills in 2001 after 105 years of operation, then Pillowtex in 2003.

The Pillowtex bankruptcy in 2003 was the turning point. The company, which had absorbed Fieldcrest Cannon through a series of mergers, closed its sixteen plants and laid off approximately 6,450 workers across the United States—the largest mass layoff in North Carolina history [^54]. In Eden, the last 450 textile workers lost their jobs overnight. The closure was not unique (the textile industry was collapsing across the South) but it was concentrated, sudden, devastating.

What followed was not a recovery but a persistence in diminished form. Some residents found other jobs, often at lower wages in the service or logistics sectors. Others left for places where jobs existed. Still others remained on disability, in retirement, or in the informal economy. The infrastructure built for textile production—the industrial buildings, the workers' housing, the downtown commercial district—persisted while the economic base that justified it dissolved.

### The Residual Condition

Eden's present condition is neither industrial nor post-industrial in the sense that would imply a transformation. The factories have not been converted into lofts and galleries; no creative class has colonized the vacant storefronts; no wave of gentrification has swept over the workers' housing. What persists is something more troubling: a territory in suspension, organized around an economy that no longer exists, awaiting a transformation that has not come.

Statistics index the condition. Poverty rate of 19.8%, nearly double the national average (11%); 17.2% of families live below the poverty line. Median household income of \$50,959—significantly below the state median (\$73,958) and the national median (\$83,730). Rockingham County is classified as *economically distressed* (Tier 1) by the North Carolina Department of Commerce [^55]. Population in slow decline as younger residents leave to seek opportunities elsewhere—15,353 inhabitants in 2023, down from 15,407 in the 2020 census, in a state with 3.95% population growth. Infrastructure exceeding its use—more square footage than commerce requires, more housing than the reduced population occupies.

The physical landscape bears witness. Several hundred thousand square feet of empty industrial space occupy former factory buildings—brick structures with large windows, built for natural light and ventilation, now housing nothing or converted to diminished uses. Downtown storefronts are vacant; the commercial strip along the national highway serves automotive consumption while the pedestrian downtown deteriorates. Banks have closed their branches: only machines remain, ATMs dispensing credit without a human interlocutor—*lift* made architectural. Between 2024 and 2025, Airbnbs have appeared in houses that, ten years earlier, were signs of vacancy, and Amazon distribution centers have moved into regional logistics—capital returns to Eden, but in a form that requires neither skilled workers nor local retail nor downtown life. Residential neighborhoods of modest single-family homes, built for textile workers and their families, continue to house a population whose economic base has eroded. Eden sits squarely in the *Bible Belt*: it has nearly 200 churches for 15,000 inhabitants, predominantly Baptist and evangelical Protestant, unevenly distributed—Black congregations and white congregations occupy separate buildings, a persistent reflection of residential segregation. The political landscape mirrors the religious one: Rockingham County, classified as "Solid Republican" since 2012, voted 67% for Trump in 2024. The vote is not an accident but a symptom: that of a population for whom the transgression of democratic norms is precisely what is demanded, a change in political method born of territorial disarray. Globally, this phenomenon is not isolated: from India to Brazil, from Hungary to Italy, it reflects the same disarray of territories that capital has abandoned [^eden_pol].

This is not the picturesque ruin that attracts photographers and tourists. It is the ordinary desolation of places that capital has discarded—neither dramatic enough to be aestheticized nor prosperous enough to be developed, persisting without trajectory, enduring without hope of the transformation that redevelopment would bring. The condition is not tragic—tragedy presupposes a reversal. It is melancholic in the structural sense: separation from a prior state that will not return and whose loss does not resolve. And yet this condition is not pure negativity. Eden functions as what I call a *reservative site*: a place where disinvestment has paradoxically preserved the potentiality that development consumes. A developed territory exhausts its potentialities—every building optimized for rental yield, every space monetized, every possibility actualized according to the logic of return on investment. Disinvestment, paradoxically, conserves a potentiality that development would have destroyed. Eden's vacant storefronts are not simply evidence of decline; they hold in suspension multiple possible uses—community center, artist studio, cooperative, or continued vacancy. The reservative is a plenitude disguised as lack.

The \$1,000 house is the product of this sequence—plantation, spinning mill, mill town, offshoring, vacancy. To name this process precisely, I borrow from Marek Poliks and Roberto Alonso Trillo (*Exocapitalism*, 2025) the concept of *lift*: the movement by which capital tears itself away from the sphere of human affairs to relocate toward more efficient substrates—the offshore factory first, then the logistics platform (Amazon rather than downtown retail), financialization (the yield on Pillowtex stock rather than sheet production in Eden), the recursive structures of the software economy where price definitively decouples from material production. Physical things are slow, predictable, expensive—they impose fixed costs that constrain capital's movement. Eden is what *lift* leaves behind: the residue. The textile mills closed not because demand for textiles ceased, but because capital found better opportunities at each stage of its tearing away. Faced with this withdrawal, residents practice what Poliks and Trillo call *drag*: the attempt to capture resources from structures that escape the human scale—disability payments, platform gig work via smartphone, the informal economy, family networks that redistribute what remains. The Eden house is the survey point of this sequence—it is the anchor of observation, the site of the practice through which the material of post-documentary art can emerge.

### The House as Infrastructure of Sociability

Before continuing, a loop is necessary: Eden is above all a laboratory of sociabilities. The project was never primarily a work on the house—it was a work on the relations the house makes possible. This sociability is sincere, human, altruistic. The Eden project is a laboratory of these sociabilities through mediations constrained by notions of property, the public domain, capital flows, and visible and invisible structures that organize an ecosystem of which the house could be the center of analysis.

### The House as Work

The house presented the characteristic pathologies of deferred maintenance in a humid climate. Roof damage had allowed water infiltration; the resulting moisture had propagated through structural elements, encouraging rot in the framing and flooring. Windows had failed; heating and cooling systems had ceased to function; electrical and plumbing systems required replacement. The house was not in ruins but was not habitable—suspended between two conditions, requiring substantial investment to become either one.

But the significance of these conditions is not descriptive; it is epistemic. Each pathology is a condensed record of decisions taken and not taken—by residents who could not afford repairs, by owners who left, by a market that assigned negative value to maintenance, by a regulatory apparatus that coded the structure as burden rather than shelter. The house is not simply *damaged*; it is the support for a program that is both conceptual and practical: each intervention necessitated by the structure's condition becomes an occasion to produce a work. Material constraint generates form; repair and creation are indistinguishable.

The front and back porches were collapsing. Rather than rebuilding them, the structures were extracted and recomposed as architectural sculpture and viewing apparatus. The ensemble was transported and exhibited in the heart of Chelsea, at the Aperture Foundation in New York (*Eden*, Fondation d'entreprise Hermès / Aperture Foundation, November 2016 – January 2017) [^56]. The porch of a wooden house in North Carolina became a multi-ton construction in a Manhattan art space: the displacement is not metaphorical but physical, logistical, economically absurd—and it is this very absurdity that makes visible the gap between the two value regimes. The apparatus, designed with architects Jérémie Dalin and Sylvia Bourgoin, obeys a structuring constraint: every material used for the New York exhibition can be reused in the context of restoring the house in Eden. The exhibition is both a reenactment and a reuse—the porch circulates between two functions without fixing itself in either.

The house's siding contains asbestos and must be removed. Before the intervention, all exterior and interior surfaces are digitized using a consumer-grade document scanner repurposed from its intended use—nearly 17,000 technical images of every surface, every joint, every point of failure, from which are extracted the 1,000 images composing the shell of the book *Eden* published in 2016, conceived in collaboration with artist and designer Fred Cave [^56]. This is not preservation but what might be called performative archaeology: the scanner reveals what the eye normalizes. The stratigraphy of paint layers records the social history of taste and poverty—the moment when maintenance became patching, when patching became neglect. Water damage patterns map not only structural failure but the specific trajectory of the roof's collapse, which is itself an index of the foreclosure chronology. The presence of certain materials—lead paint under layers of latex, asbestos tiles under vinyl—records the regulatory regimes under which the house was built, modified, and finally abandoned. Every surface compresses decades of habitation, disinvestment, and systemic neglect into a material register that no amount of reading economic theory can reproduce, because the house makes abstraction *physical*—load-bearing, olfactory, measurable.

Then the house is repainted a red I describe as "toxic," manufactured from the industrial pigments used to paint stop signs and fire extinguishers—a red of alarm, of emergency signage. The gesture operates on three registers. It is first a return to original function: the *Little Red Schoolhouse* is the archetype of the American school, the wooden building painted in red ochre (the cheapest paint available in the nineteenth century) that became a national symbol of popular education and civic values. The 1884 house literally recovers its color and its name. It is then a plastic operation: the industrial red saturates the architectural form and transforms it into a visual object, visible from hundreds of meters in Eden's residual landscape. It is finally a premeditated announcement: in 2015–2016, this alarm color on a ruined infrastructure in the American Deep South anticipated—without yet fully knowing it, but with the prescience that attention to abandoned territories provides—the election of Trump and the political regime that would follow. The *Little Red Schoolhouse*, symbol of American educational democracy, repainted in emergency red in an *economically distressed* county: the artistic gesture and the political diagnosis coincide.

The documentation produces knowledge about financialized capitalism that archival research cannot generate. The 2008 crisis, the wave of foreclosures, municipal disinvestment—all of this is legible in the structure itself, not as metaphor but as material consequence. The house has become a witness to processes operating well beyond its walls, and the digitization project constituted a counter-archive: not a record of what was *said* about the crisis but of what the crisis *produced* at the scale of a single dwelling in a single territory. A second editorial project, still in progress, extends this logic by inverting it: no longer the scanned surface—the house's "skin," its regime of visibility—but what stands behind the walls. Showing the load-bearing structures, the framework, the skeleton of wood and nails that supports the visible envelope. What is of interest here is not architecture in the technical sense but what the structure makes thinkable: behind the operculum of the politically visible—the façade, the storefront, the surface that presents itself—lie the invisible arrangements that make community, consensus, conflict, tension, power. The house, opened up, becomes a scale model of what the territory does not show: the power relations that hold together what, seen from outside, seems simply abandoned.

### Strategic Anticipation

The purchase was not rational in economic terms. The house would absorb resources without generating revenue; its location in a declining territory excluded any capital gain; the necessary rehabilitation would cost multiples of the purchase price. By the logic of investment, the purchase was absurd. That is a simple sign to indicate that the stakes lie elsewhere.

But the logic of investment was precisely what the purchase refused. Buying a house in Eden meant claiming a territorial position in advance of its justification—establishing a presence in a place whose significance would only become clear later. It was a prolepsis—a gesture that precedes its own justification, an answer formulated before the question.

When someone asks "what is your connection to Black Mountain College?", the answer is already architectural. The house lies just under 300 kilometers from the Lake Eden campus; it exists in the same territory, shaped by the same regional history, subject to the same processes of capitalist development and abandonment. And both places bear the same name: *Lake Eden* and *Eden*—as though the town were the lake's reflection in a water that distorts, reforms, and differentiates. BMC taught on the shores of a lake named Eden; the house stands in a town named Eden. The reflection is not a copy: it inverts, displaces, alters proportions. The pedagogical utopia on the lake becomes the residual condition in the town; artistic experimentation becomes material rehabilitation; the chosen community becomes the endured neighborhood. The link is not genealogical (I am not a descendant of BMC participants) nor scholarly (I am not a historian of the college) but territorial: I have committed resources to a place that shares the conditions of the place where BMC operated, and the very name of this place—by chance or by toponymic persistence—doubles the connection the project constructs.

This territorial claim does not resolve the ethical problems of an outsider engaging with a community in crisis. It does not make me a resident in the full sense; I do not depend on Eden for my subsistence, I do not send children to school there, I do not share the constraints that shape the lives of those who cannot leave. But it establishes stakes: a material investment, a continuous responsibility, a commitment that extends beyond the period of residency. The house is an infrastructure for a practice whose legitimacy depends on something more than visit-document-depart.

### Laboratories and Residency

The house also functions as a testing site for ideas that cannot be tested abstractly—but this function was not programmed in advance; it arose from an economic contingency. The Eden project originates in an institutional commission (the *Immersion* commission from the Fondation d'entreprise Hermès and the Aperture Foundation). The house, acquired for much less than the initial budget anticipated, freed a portion of the production grant. Rather than reinvesting the surplus in the comfort of an individual project, I subdivided the funds and invited artists—sculptors, videographers, writers—to join me in Eden. The gesture was simple but its consequences structuring: by redistributing resources, the project shifted from artist monograph to collective practice, from photographic commission to territorial laboratory. The question was no longer "what can I do with this house?" but "what can happen *in* this house when practitioners from radically different positions engage there together?"

The distinction from the artist residency must be specified, as the superficial resemblance masks a structural difference. The residency is a *hosted* experience: an institution provides the infrastructure—studio, housing, meals—and the artist arrives, produces, and departs. The host institution absorbs maintenance costs; the artist is protected from the site's material conditions. The Eden house inverts this relationship. There is no host institution; the house *is* the infrastructure, and its maintenance is not separated from research but constitutive of it. Every dollar spent on roof repair is a decision about what kind of knowledge production the structure can support the following summer. Every failure to repair is a constraint that shapes the possible work. The experience is not "being there"—it is *maintaining the conditions of collective practice in a territory that resists maintaining anything*. This is a fundamentally different epistemological position from the residency, because the infrastructure does not disappear behind the work but remains visible, demanding, incomplete.

The house thus functions as a *social sculpture*—but the term, borrowed from Beuys, must be displaced. For Beuys, social sculpture is a metaphor: human creativity extended to the organization of society, the idea that "every human being is an artist" in the sense that everyone participates in shaping the social body. In Eden, social sculpture is literal. The house is a physical object that collective practice transforms—and that, in turn, transforms the practice. The transformation is simultaneously aesthetic (the high-resolution scans of degraded surfaces, the photographs of light passing through disjointed partitions), social (cohabitation in an inadequate space, the daily negotiation of uses, the communal kitchen as decision-making site—a direct echo of the BMC refectory), and epistemic (what the house teaches about the material conditions of knowledge production when infrastructure is precarious). Where Beuys's social sculpture tends toward utopia—the transformation of society through creativity—Eden's remains indexed to the resistance of the real: termites, leaks, the cost of materials, municipal codes. The house is not a metaphor for community; it is the material condition under which the temporary community must operate, and this condition is inseparable from what it produces.

For ten years, artists have come to Eden in succession to imagine works—in complicity, in collaboration, in mutual aid. Some presences were made possible by institutional mechanisms: Sarah Feuillas, for example, was selected collegially by a jury at Jeune Création in 2018, receiving a research grant that I personally produce—a case where the project functions as production infrastructure for artists whose work engages the territory without depending on it. The house functioned as *workspace*—working space and site of experimentation, not residency in the domestic sense. Photographers, sculptors, videographers, writers, architects have worked there, sometimes a few days, sometimes several weeks, engaging the building, the territory, the neighborhood as material and interlocutor. In 2024 and 2025, I established what I call the Eden Summer Institutes—intensive month-long sessions bringing together practitioners from Europe and America [^esi_participants], living together, sharing meals, working on problems that cannot be resolved in the abbreviated temporality of the conference or workshop. The residential format echoes the BMC structure while acknowledging the impossibility of reproducing its conditions. Duration matters—not the three-day workshop or the one-week residency but sustained presence, weeks of cohabitation, the slow development of trust and understanding that only time can produce. The Institutes are also the project's final local events: Eden's end is itself programmatic, decided from the beginning. Ten years of existence, not one more. The project does not expire from exhaustion but from the completion of a cycle whose duration was fixed in advance—a closure programmed from the origin.

### Risks and Asymmetries

In total, 79 artists and theorists have been involved closely or distantly in the Eden project, and several hundred Eden residents—across all generations and social conditions—have taken part. From the first year, the house served as a pretext for encounter. On the side of public missions, exchanges with heritage preservation associations—the house was not listed but identified as dating from 1884 and recognized as the county's former first public school—opened dialogue with local institutions. On the side of the neighborhood, the arrival of French artists immediately attracted attention: Ugo Schiavi was the first to come, in the summer of 2015, and together we undertook the first rehabilitation work. The house also functioned as a site for performance and exhibition from that first year—*Battlefield* with Ugo Schiavi, *Dogwood* with Thomas Hauser, Fred Cave, and Jesse Hoyle—creating occasions of encounter between practitioners from outside and territory residents.

But these encounters are not symmetrical, and territorial engagement carries risks that must be named. The first is aestheticization: the conversion of economic distress into artistic material, the extraction of creative value from conditions that harm those who live under them. An outsider buying a \$1,000 house in a declining territory can easily become a figure of poverty tourism, treating dilapidation as scenery for projects whose primary audience is elsewhere—the gallery, the festival, the dissertation committee. The challenge is therefore to work with the expertise of local associations that have identified audiences, available infrastructures, and themes likely to resonate in the territory.

The second risk is what might be called utopian displacement: projecting onto Eden the fantasies of alternative community that the BMC mythology invites. If BMC's retrospective construction produces a usable past for contemporary institutions, engagement with Eden can produce a usable present—a territory imagined as blank page, available for experiments that more resistant places would refuse. The risk is real: without constant vigilance, collaboration becomes colonization and solidarity turns into extraction. This is why the engagement strategies at Eden have been co-imagined with members of the local community—not as a guarantee against asymmetry, but as a minimum condition for the project not to reproduce what it claims to analyze. It should be noted, moreover, that Black Mountain College's history is largely unknown to the inhabitants of the territory that housed it: the project produces, however modestly, an effect of reappropriation of a local history that international canonization had paradoxically rendered invisible to those closest to it.

The third risk concerns the relationship between presence and legitimacy. Owning a house in Eden, spending summers there, conducting workshops and producing publications—none of this makes someone a member of the community in any meaningful sense. The community is constituted by people who have no choice but to be there, who depend on local institutions, who will remain when the summer practitioners have departed. Confusing territorial claim with community belonging is confusing property with membership, investment with commitment, presence with participation. The example reported by Jean-Paul Fourmentraux in *L'Œuvre commune* is instructive here [^fourm]. The Nouveaux Commanditaires program—conceived by the artist and photographer François Hers, co-founder of the Viva agency (1972), director of the DATAR photographic mission (1983–1989)—and implemented by the Fondation de France since 1991—inverts the usual pattern of public commissions: it is citizens who commission a work from an artist, accompanied by a mediator. Yet the case of Alain Bernardini and the *Monument d'images* at the Paul-Brousse hospital in Villejuif (2007–2009) shows that even this dispositif does not immunize against conflict. Fourteen hospital workers commission a monument for the hospital entrance; two unions oppose the project; the physical work is abandoned and transforms into a book—compiling photographs, petitions, open letters, fictional texts that document the controversy itself. The *Monument d'images* became the narrative of its own impossibility. What Fourmentraux shows is that the "common work" is never "already there": it demands to be established through a series of associations, intrigues, and negotiations whose outcome is not guaranteed—and whose failure can be as productive as success.

The fourth risk is economic asymmetry, and it must be named precisely because it is structural rather than incidental. The \$1,000 house that is a burden for a local resident—who cannot afford the taxes, the repairs, the compliance penalties—is a laboratory for a researcher backed by institutions. This asymmetry is not a problem to be solved by good intentions; it is a structural fact produced by the same financialization this chapter describes. The researcher's institutional position—doctoral funding, university affiliation, international mobility, access to grant infrastructure—is itself a product of the knowledge economy that replaced the manufacturing economy Eden lost. To put it bluntly: the researcher can afford to treat the house as experimental infrastructure *because* the same global restructuring that destroyed Eden's textile economy created the conditions for the researcher's career. The retcon methodology must include this asymmetry not as confession but as *data*—a constitutive element of the practice rather than an embarrassment to be managed through reflexive prose. The question is not whether the practitioner benefits—of course the practitioner benefits—but whether the practice produces something that remains with the territory when the practitioner leaves. It is here that Joëlle Zask's distinction between genuine participation and "superfluity" finds its relevance: "superfluous" is the action that "leads to nothing, is embodied in no concrete situation," of which every trace has disappeared [^zask]. Zask shows, drawing on Donald Winnicott, that participation can only occur in a "good enough" environment—not ideal but adapted to those who inhabit it. It is what the economist Amartya Sen calls "capabilities": not abstract capacities nor offered chances, but concrete possibilities of use in a particular environment [^zask_sen]. The Eden project is measured against this criterion: not what it proposes, but what local conditions effectively allow to be made of it.

The parallel with BMC's own territorial contradiction is instructive. Dreier described the college as a "Yankee island in a Southern sea." The same examination must be applied here: a European-trained researcher purchasing property in a town with a 20% poverty rate, conducting workshops whose participants are predominantly international artists and students, producing publications whose primary circulation is academic—this is its own form of island, however engaged in interaction with the surrounding territory. The difference between the island that recognizes itself as such and the island that imagines itself a continent is the difference between critical practice and self-delusion.

The theoretical framework must confront the material conditions that the preceding section quantified. A household at Eden's median income—\$50,959 annually, approximately \$4,250 monthly before taxes, for an average two-person household (ACS 2019–2023, U.S. Census Bureau)—faces an arithmetic that leaves little margin. Housing, utilities, food, transportation, healthcare: the fixed costs that Poliks and Trillo identify as *drag* on capital are, for residents, simply the cost of survival. The "retained potentiality" that the reservative site concept highlights appears differently from this position. The vacant storefront is not a plenitude of possibilities but an index of economic failure; the abandoned factory is not a museum of what capital no longer needs but a reminder of wages no longer paid.

The practitioner arrives with a different arithmetic. The grant funding the project may represent a year's work for an Eden household. The artist's CV accumulates lines: residencies, exhibitions, publications. Each line has exchange value in the "lifted" circuits of the art world. A successful project in Eden can generate returns—new grants, teaching positions, institutional recognition—that eclipse the project's direct investment in the territory. This empirical observation will be taken up and theorized in section 3.2, which will situate the practitioner's position within the framework of exocapitalism—but it matters to state it first as a bare fact, before conceptual framing.

These risks cannot be eliminated; they can only be acknowledged and structurally incorporated into the practice. Acknowledgment requires more than reflexivity—more than sustained attention to dynamics that are then left intact. It requires practices that materially redistribute agency, resources, and authorship. Working against the risks requires institutional forms, collaborative structures, and modes of documentation that foreground the perspectives of those most affected by the conditions engaged—and that submit the researcher's own position to the same analysis applied to the archive.

The Eden Summer Institute attempts such practices without claiming to have achieved them. Its principle rests on a reciprocity—which can be conflictual: international and local co-funding (Fondation d'entreprise Hermès, Villa Albertine, Reidsville Area Foundation, and a multitude of local ad hoc supporters), management entrusted to territory actors. Yet what remains are fruitful encounters between artists and residents, likely lasting friendships, and narratives that will be transmitted before surely disappearing. In a decade of activity, the milieu has shown it can produce both singular works and complex discourses—by redistributing positions. For it is not always the invited artists who sign the works: Eden residents, through encounter, have discovered themselves as authors of forms they would not have imagined alone. This inversion is not anecdotal; it is a sign that the environment has functioned, in Zask's sense, as a space of effective capabilities rather than of opportunities offered and never seized. What occurs at Eden is neither the projection of an artist onto a territory nor the extraction of material by an outside gaze—it is a process of reciprocal individuation in which the project and the place transform each other [^simondon]. The pages of collaborative graphic novel produced over several sessions are one form; the investigations into public spaces and their definition when they await existence (Lou Bellegarde), the almost futile search for local decolonial archives as the matrix of a work (Jesse Hoyle) are others.

## Articulation, Method

### Two Temporal Regimes

The link between BMC and Eden is not found but constructed. It is a retcon: a retroactive continuity brought into being by the artist-researcher's gesture, a relation that historiography does not produce. There is no "structural parallel" to be discovered between an experimental college in the mountains and a mill town in the Piedmont—there is a decision to read them together, from a present that sets them in resonance.

What the retcon brings to light is nevertheless real: both are American experiences that engage the encounter with European thought, in an ad hoc territory, under structural—economic and political—tension. But the symmetry stops there; two inversions complicate it.

The first is political. BMC was born in a context where European fascisms made the United States a refuge—an "Eden," precisely, for intellectual and artistic refugees expelled by Europe. Albers, Jalowetz, de Kooning, Zadkine, the Dreiers: the college was populated by those the old continent had made unwelcome. America then presented itself as the default democratic space, the one where experimentation remained possible because institutions had not yet been captured by authoritarianism. Ninety years later, the vector has reversed. The Trump I (2017–2021) and Trump II (since 2025) administrations—with, between them, the contestation of the 2020 election, the January 6, 2021 Capitol assault, four criminal indictments, and a campaign conducted from the defendant's bench—have made the United States itself the place where democratic norms are actively dismantled. Rockingham County, which hosts Eden, voted 67% for Trump in 2024. The project thus unfolds not in the democratic asylum the refugees of 1933 imagined, but in a territory where institutional transgression has become the demanded product—a reversal the retcon must integrate as data rather than obstacle.

The second inversion is spatial. BMC was closed in on itself: an isolated campus in the mountains, voluntarily withdrawn from the surrounding territory—Dreier's "Yankee island in a Southern sea." Students and faculty lived, ate, worked, and deliberated within a closed perimeter whose porosity with the local community remained minimal (section 1.5 showed just how minimal). Eden inverts this model: the project treats the entire town as campus space—in the expanded sense of a *campus* that is no longer a pedagogical enclosure but an inhabited territory whose streets, wastelands, churches, porches, and vacant shops become the sites of practice. There is no dedicated building separating the experience from its context; the town *is* the infrastructure. This openness changes the very nature of experimentation: where BMC could control the variables of its environment (who enters, who stays, who decides), Eden must compose with the unpredictability of a living territory—its resistances, its own temporalities, its actors who are not enrolled students but residents whose presence precedes and exceeds the project. This displacement engages a conception of citizenship that Joëlle Zask, a reader of Dewey, formulates precisely: not the utilitarian relationship of a public that expects government to fulfill desires formed independently of any association, but a citizenship understood as "a matter of education and culture whose transmission depends on organized community and whose actualization depends on the active participation of individuals in the experiences they share with one another" [^zask_cit]. This is exactly what Eden's expanded campus puts to the test: not offering services to a population, but constituting a milieu where participation produces its own conditions of possibility.

BMC was assembled from the crisis conditions of the 1930s; when those conditions changed, the college became non-viable and dissolved. Eden was assembled from the South's textile expansion; when production moved offshore, the mills closed. What persists in both cases is not the living institution but its residue: archive in one case, territory in the other. The question organizing this essay—what to do with what remains when the conditions that produced it have ceased to operate—could only be posed by forcing the encounter between these two residues.

But analogy is not enough; one must enter into causality, and causality passes through time. BMC was undone by the *exhaustion of time*. The "hyper-democracy" Duwa speaks of [^18]—the pursuit of consensus on everything, including the elimination of Sunday lunch—demanded a temporal investment that ultimately consumed the institution from within. The personality conflicts, ideological schisms, and cascading departures of the 1950s are not accidents but consequences of a temporal regime that did not give itself the means to last: too much time spent deliberating, too few resources to absorb the costs of deliberation. Eden was undone by the inverse process: the *acceleration of time*. The Pillowtex bankruptcy of 2003—6,450 layoffs in a single day—is the punctual event that erased in months an economy built over a century. The time of offshoring does not negotiate; it cuts. In one case, the institution dies of its own slowness; in the other, the territory dies of capital's speed.

The *Little Red Schoolhouse* is the exact point where these two temporal regimes converge. It is a product of short time—a financial transaction made possible by the foreclosure wave of 2008, itself a consequence of speculative acceleration. But it is also an object of long time: a structure that decomposes slowly, whose walls bear the strata of a century of workers' habitation, whose physical degradation imposes its rhythm on anyone who undertakes to work there. Buying this house means acquiring simultaneously a residue of accelerated time (the crisis) and a support for slowed time (research-creation, the workshop, the patient digitization of surfaces). It is this double temporal inscription—not the structural analogy between two abandoned institutions—that founds the connection between BMC and Eden as a research object.

Bernard Stiegler's organology names what happens to the territory during this acceleration: an *organological amputation*. The human is constituted through the co-evolution of three systems of organs—physiological organs (the biological body), artificial organs (technical objects, from tools to infrastructure), and social organs (institutions, customs, patterns of collective life). These systems are co-constitutive: change in one propagates through the others. Eden's factories were artificial organs—technical systems organizing the transformation of raw cotton into finished textiles, requiring specific spatial configurations, specific temporal patterns, specific bodily disciplines. Workers' bodies were physiological organs adapted to textile labor through generations of habituation. Churches, unions, neighborhoods, and families were social organs coordinating collective life around industrial production. The closure of the factories was an amputation: when the artificial organs were removed, the physiological and social organs that had co-evolved with them did not simply adapt—they underwent their own degradation. The territory is not simply post-industrial; it is organologically traumatized.

But organology, while it accurately describes the trauma, does not grasp its entire mechanism. Stiegler thinks *maladjustment*—the speed of technical change exceeds the adaptive capacity of social and physiological organs, producing what he calls *symbolic misery*. The diagnosis assumes, however, that artificial organs remain within the human field: they transform too quickly, but they remain *our* techniques, *our* prostheses, objects of a possible care (*Sorge*). Yet this is exactly what Eden's situation calls into question. The textile mills were artificial organs in the full sense—technical systems that workers understood, to which their bodies were attuned, whose rhythms structured social organs. What replaced them is no longer of the same order. The smartphone through which an Eden resident searches for work on a gig economy platform, the Airbnb algorithm that sets a room's price based on signals no one in Eden controls, the financial mechanism by which Pillowtex was dismantled from a Manhattan office—these are not maladjusted artificial organs; they are structures that have left the organological field. They no longer maintain a co-evolutionary relationship with the territory's bodies and institutions; they operate on it from an outside. It is here that Poliks and Trillo's exocapitalism takes over from organology: *lift* names precisely what Stiegler cannot name—not a maladjustment between organs but an ontological decoupling, the moment when capital ceases to be an organ (however pathogenic) and becomes an autonomous environment in which humans are no longer agents but variables [^stiegler_limite].

[^stiegler_limite]: This critical reading does not invalidate organology; it marks its boundary. Stiegler himself, in his final works on the Anthropocene and entropy (*Qu'appelle-t-on panser?*, Les Liens qui libèrent, 2018–2020), attempted to extend the organological framework to planetary systems—but without the *lift*/*drag* vocabulary that distinguishes between techniques that remain within the co-evolutionary field and those that have torn themselves away. Exocapitalism completes organology where the latter stumbles on financialization.

### *Lift*, *Drag*, and the Practitioner

The theoretical framework of Marek Poliks and Roberto Alonso Trillo allows the practitioner's position in the contemporary economy to be named precisely [^exocap]. Their thesis is radical: capital no longer belongs to the human sphere—it governs itself. *Exocapitalism* designates a regime in which capital has torn itself away (*lift*) from the processes of material production and consumption to reproduce through recursive structures—financial derivatives, software platforms, arbitrage architectures—in which price progressively decouples from any material value. The theory inherits from post-operaismo (Lazzarato, Terranova) and post-Deleuzianism (the CCRU, Szepanski—for whom "capital might be the evil twin of Deleuze"), but breaks with classical Marxism on a decisive point: it is no longer labor that produces value, it is inefficiency—latency, delay, the stacking of intermediaries between capital and humans.

### Exocapitalism

To measure what exocapitalism contributes, it must be situated within the debate it inherits and displaces. Since the Accelerationist Manifesto of Williams and Srnicek (2013), the theoretical left has been divided over a crucial question: can capital's computational infrastructures be "repurposed" toward emancipatory ends, or does acceleration merely strengthen capital? The neo-rationalist accelerationists (Williams, Srnicek, Brassier, Negarestani) argue that algorithmic modeling and computational planning can serve a post-capitalist project—that the left must master abstraction, calculation, and big data rather than retreat into what they contemptuously call *folk politics*: localism, direct action, horizontalism. Franco "Bifo" Berardi, from the post-autonomist tradition, opposes them with a radically different diagnosis: "semiocapitalism" subsumes not only labor but affects, desires, cognitive capacities themselves. The human organism is structurally incapable of keeping pace with the infosphere—there is a "paradigmatic discordance" between network time and body time. For Bifo, acceleration does not liberate: it exhausts. What remains possible is not revolution but "therapy"—ironic detachment, aesthetic culture, the "slow patience of affectivity" that refuses endless production [^gardiner].

Poliks and Trillo's exocapitalism takes stock of this debate but introduces a distinction that neither the accelerationists nor Bifo make. Where the accelerationists see infrastructures to be reoriented and Bifo sees a system to flee through withdrawal, Poliks and Trillo describe a regime in which capital has ceased to be a localizable adversary: it has *torn itself away* (*lift*) from the very terrain where struggle—or withdrawal—might take place. *Drag*—the human attempt to capture resources from structures that escape the human scale—is not the opposite of *lift* but its symptom. This framework is retained here for three precise reasons. First, it names what neither Stieglerian organology nor classical Marxism can name: not a malfunction of capital but its *normal* functioning at a distance, without bodies, without place. Second, it provides the operators (*lift*, *drag*, *fold*) that allow the practitioner's position at Eden to be described precisely—neither resistant nor complicit but caught in a mechanism where their own *drag* can trigger *lift*. Third, if Eden situates itself within the questioning of a laboratory inherited from Black Mountain, exocapitalism allows Dewey to be confronted on a terrain Dewey himself could not anticipate: meliorism is a *drag* gesture—it assumes that capital remains within the field of human action. Exocapitalism shows why this assumption no longer holds.

A caveat is necessary, however. Exocapitalism is a recent framework (2025), still little discussed in the academic literature, issuing from a theoretical tradition (the CCRU, xenoeconomics) that values conceptual provocation as much as empirical rigor. Its strongest thesis—capital governs itself—is a productive hyperbole rather than an empirically falsifiable description. What we retain is not the cosmological thesis in its entirety but the analytical operators (*lift*/*drag*) which, applied to the Eden case, prove descriptive where other frameworks remain prescriptive. The case of the Airbnbs that appeared between 2024 and 2025—the project's *drag* triggering a new cycle of *lift*—is an empirical demonstration that the operators describe and that Deweyan or Stieglerian vocabulary cannot capture.

The genealogy of this framework requires clarification, because it engages a political risk the text must name. The idea that capital governs itself has a long history—from Marx (capital as "automatic subject") to Deleuze and Guattari (the decoded flows of *Anti-Oedipus*). It was the British philosopher Nick Land who, in the 1990s, at the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) co-founded with Sadie Plant at the University of Warwick, drew the most radical consequence. Land's thesis was a *program*: if capital is an autonomous process more powerful than any human agency, then it must be accelerated—intensify deterritorialization, abolish obstacles, including the democratic institutions that "slow down" the process. Ray Brassier, in his critical reading of Land, showed where this logic leads: if one renounces all human strategy in favor of accelerating the process, "*if you have no strategy, someone with a strategy will soon commandeer your tactics*"—one ends up endorsing neoliberal politics [^brassier_land]. That is what happened. Land became, in the 2010s, one of the founding figures of the neo-reactionary movement (*NRx*)—a thought that combines technological supremacism, rejection of democracy, and authoritarianism. The trajectory is not an accident: it is the inherent consequence of abolishing human agency as a pertinent category.

### SaaS and *Fold*

It is here that the third operator of Poliks and Trillo, left in suspense until now, intervenes: the *fold*. For them, the *fold* designates the operation by which the software economy proceeds not outward (expansion, conquest of new markets) but inward—through involution. Borrowing from Leibniz via Deleuze the idea that "folds go to infinity," Poliks and Trillo show that value in the SaaS economy (*Software as a Service*—the software subscription model where the user rents permanent access without ever owning the tool) is created by involution: layers of added value superimpose, compress, produce new dimensions of relation within an existing system without the addition of external material. The *fold* is, in their framework, the ontological operation by which difference emerges without constitutive change of the underlying material—"like origami from a sheet of white paper." The *fold* is not an alternative to *lift* and *drag*—it is the underlying mechanism through which software capital complexifies, the operating mode that makes *lift* possible by producing recursive strata of added value. For Poliks and Trillo, the *fold* is an operator of capital; it does not serve humans, it envelops them.

Two research projects conducted during the Eden Summer Institute directly address SaaS as invisible infrastructure. Laurent Isler shows the derangement of machines themselves, symbols of SaaS. Antoine Langlois, in a more positivist gesture, proposes a "natural" sonic poetic work carried by birdsong—which is not without resonance with the Twitter bird, crucified by Elon Musk's "X." Both work the same question from inverse positions: what becomes of creation when the infrastructure that carries it has become invisible and proprietary?

The hypothesis I advance here is that this operation can be turned around. Applied to the Eden project, the *fold* names what *The Studies* and Theodore accomplish: a folding of the archive back upon itself—but in the service of archival complexity rather than added value. The BMC corpus—correspondence, minutes, testimonies, monographs—is the underlying material, which does not change. What changes are the dimensions of relation that emerge from the folding: participants' comments that sediment around documents, the co-occurrences Theodore reveals between texts that institutions preserve separately, the improbable associations between a 1940 Dreier letter and a 1953 minutes that only the corpus's statistical distribution makes visible. The *fold* is what distinguishes the Eden project from a simple archive digitization. To digitize is *lift*—extracting documents from their material context to convert them into data. What the project does with that data is something else: it folds them, comments on them, traverses them with Theodore, produces internal complexity without external expansion. The patient deep learning uses the same operation as software capital—the *fold*, recursive involution—but reverses it: where SaaS folds to create extractable added value, the project folds to create new dimensions of reading in a finite corpus. This is not a resistance to the capitalist *fold*—it is its tactical appropriation, in Certeau's sense: using the dominant system's operations according to a logic that is not its own.

An objection must be formulated here, and it is constitutive. Theodore is built on language models that are themselves the ultimate product of *lift*: massive data extraction, training on corpora whose authors did not consent to the use, computational infrastructures with considerable energy footprints, concentration of technical power among a few companies. Using AI to detokenize the archive is using the instrument of extraction to try to undo extraction. The contradiction is not resolvable—it is the very condition of the work. It is here that the *fork* → *fold* sequence takes its most precise significance. The *fork* is the operation that opens: by duplicating the corpus, by branching narratives, it multiplies possible trajectories and prevents canonical closure. The *fold* is the operation that deepens: by folding the archive back upon itself, it creates new dimensions of reading without external expansion, without extraction of market value. The two together constitute an attempt to counter the effects of *lift* at the level of creation—not by refusing *lift*'s tools (which would be sterile Luddism) but by diverting them toward ends the extractive regime did not foresee. Theodore does not cease to be a product of *lift* because one uses it differently; but the use made of it—situated, constrained by a finite corpus, oriented toward archival complexity rather than added value—constitutes a *fork* in the logic of the tool itself. The retcon itself belongs to the regime of the *fold*: folding the past back upon the present to make continuities emerge that did not exist before the operation—without modifying documents, without adding material, working exclusively by involution. And the platforms that make it possible—the *Dining Hall* (visual fork), *The Studies* (textual commentary), Theodore (statistical distribution)—are the technical infrastructure of this *fold* applied to the archive. Where SaaS folds data streams to produce market value, the project folds archives to produce narrative—a collective, situated narrative whose dimensions multiply with each new reader's passage through the corpus.

[^brassier_land]: Ray Brassier, "Accelerationism" (transcribed lecture, Goldsmiths, University of London, 2010), available at moskvax.wordpress.com. Brassier shows that the impasse of Landian accelerationism lies in the dissolution of agency: "*once secondary production has been re-integrated or feeds back into primary production, [...] there is no longer any kind of fulcrum for the point of reversion*." The political consequence is that accelerationism, lacking a subject, ends up endorsing what capital already does—including the dismantling of democratic structures. On Land's subsequent trajectory toward the neo-reactionary movement, see Andy Beckett, "Accelerationism: how a fringe philosophy predicted the future we live in," *The Guardian*, May 11, 2017.

[^gardiner]: For a cartography of the accelerationism/Bifo debate, see Michael E. Gardiner, "Critique of Accelerationism," *Theory, Culture & Society*, 2016, which shows that both positions share the same sources (Deleuze and Guattari, operaismo) but diverge radically on the question of the body: for the accelerationists, the limits of the human organism are to be surpassed; for Bifo, they are the last terrain of resistance. Exocapitalism displaces this opposition: the question is no longer to surpass or preserve the body but to think a regime in which capital no longer needs the body at all.

In this framework, *drag* is not a resistance to *lift* but its symptom: the human attempt to insert oneself into lifted processes, to "coax capital into functioning as a medium for interhuman relations." *Drag*, counter-intuitively, accelerates *lift*. When a town like Eden attempts to capture value—through heritage tourism, the creative economy, grant attraction—it does not oppose the movement that impoverished it; it reproduces it at a lesser scale, rebuilding its own social processes in highly lifted forms. The practitioner does not observe this mechanism from the outside; there is no outside. The practitioner participates in *drag*. But any practice that aspires to the city as social sculpture—in the sense that Beuys meant an art form expanded to the very organization of collective life [^beuys]—poses an additional question: that of a socialization of private space. The house, the porch, the kitchen, the garden are spaces inefficient from capital's point of view—they do not produce measurable value, do not generate scalable yield. What they produce is pure *drag*: co-presence, trust, shared time—exactly what *lift* discards because it constitutes a fixed cost. Yet this inefficiency is the condition of possibility for artistic work at Eden: the socialized private space becomes the "good enough" environment (Zask, after Winnicott) where capabilities—not abstract capacities but concrete possibilities of use—become effective. The question, therefore, is not to resist *lift*—an impossible resistance, as Poliks and Trillo show—but to produce *drag* that does not merely slow extraction: a *drag* that deposits something in the territory, that leaves traces of a different nature than accounting traces.

[^beuys]: On the concept of *soziale Plastik* (social sculpture) in Joseph Beuys, see in particular his *Conversations* with Volker Harlan (1986) and the documentation of the action *7000 Eichen* (7000 Oaks, Documenta 7, Kassel, 1982), where tree planting by residents constitutes the work. The affiliation with Eden is structural rather than stylistic: not the charismatic figure of the artist-shaman but the entire town as material and form.

The practitioner arrives at Eden connected to circuits from which Eden has been expelled. They possess institutional affiliation (university, art world), funding sources (grants, commissions), audiences (academic, cultural). These connections are with lifted structures—the creative industries, higher education, the attention economy—that is, with sectors whose value has decoupled from local material production. The contemporary university, as Stefano Harney and Fred Moten showed in *The Undercommons* (2013), itself functions as an apparatus of extraction: it captures intellectual labor, converts it into degrees and rankings, and redistributes benefits according to logics that reproduce the hierarchies it claims to analyze [^undercommons]. The art world operates analogously: what Hito Steyerl describes as the growing alignment of contemporary art with financial flows—freeports, private foundations backed by tax strategies, biennials as *city branding* instruments—constitutes a *lift* of the art field itself, whose value circulates increasingly independently of the territories where works are produced [^steyerl]. The practitioner's presence at Eden does not sever these connections; it makes the practitioner a *conduit* between residue and lifted cores.

This conduit position is structural, not moral—it derives from exocapitalism's very topology. And it is here that the retcon reveals its economic dimension, beyond its narrative function.

BMC existed because it occupied a trough in capitalist value. In 1933, the North Carolina mountains were cheap—capital saw nothing to extract there. The Blue Ridge Assembly rent, then the Lake Eden property, represented derisory costs because no one else wanted these spaces. The college operated without endowment, without recognized degrees, funded by modest tuition and occasional patronage—an economic anomaly tolerated so long as capital looked elsewhere. In Poliks and Trillo's terms, BMC existed *in the drag*: it occupied a zone that *lift* had not yet reached, and this position allowed it to experiment with forms of collective life that institutions integrated into capital's circuits could not afford. The mining extraction episode confirms this a contrario: the college explored the possibility of mining the Lake Eden site's mineral resources—this would have been the passage from *drag* to *lift*, the conversion of terrain into deposit. The option was abandoned: no one was prepared for work of such scale, and the first dynamite blasts were an absolute deterrent—the violence of extraction made literally physical. Some modest revenues were drawn at the very beginning, then the project was buried. BMC remained in the *drag* not out of idealism but because *lift* demanded skills, capital, and a brutality that the community did not possess and did not wish to acquire.

Eden occupies the same type of interstice, but produced by the inverse movement. The \$1,000 house is the exact symmetrical of the 1933 derisory rent—except the cause is reversed. One is pre-*lift*: a space capital has not yet colonized. The other is post-*lift*: a space capital has left after extracting what it could. What the retcon retroactively reveals is therefore not a surface analogy between two abandoned institutions but a topological homology: BMC and Eden occupy the same position in the structure of capitalism—the trough, the devalued zone, the residue—at two different moments of the cycle. The retcon does not "replay" BMC; it reveals that BMC contained, without knowing it, an implicit theory of *drag*: the productive occupation of spaces abandoned by capital as a condition of possibility for experimentation [^lutticken]. To put it succinctly: is experimentation incompatible with financialization? The answer is more tortuous than a simple yes. BMC did not exist *against* capital—it existed in its blind spot. It was financed by private fortunes (the Forbes, the Dreiers), that is, by accumulated capital that redistributed itself in the form of patronage. What made it experimental was not the absence of capital but the absence of *expected return*: no one demanded a return on investment, neither in degrees, nor in patents, nor in measurable reputation. Capital was there, but dormant—capital momentarily insensitive to *lift*. The counterculture of the 1960s obeyed the same logic: it thrived on the economic surplus of the postwar boom, under conditions where the cost of living was low and the welfare state absorbed risks—this is what Fred Turner showed in *From Counterculture to Cyberculture*: the counterculture was not external to capitalism, it was its exploratory wing, and it directly fed Silicon Valley [^turner]. What is incompatible with financialization, then, is not experimentation as such—it is experimentation *without measurable return*. Financialization demands that everything be convertible into indicators: KPIs, impact factors, visitor numbers, engagement rates. What it cannot tolerate is latency without extraction—time spent that converts into nothing. Yet this is precisely the definition of experimentation: trying without guarantee of result, and accepting that failure does not produce exploitable data. In a regime of total financialization, where every interstice is immediately scanned for its *lift* potential, experimentation becomes structurally impossible—except in the troughs that capital has not yet spotted or from which it has already withdrawn. BMC occupied the first type of trough; Eden occupies the second.

[^turner]: Fred Turner, *From Counterculture to Cyberculture. Stewart Brand, the Whole Earth Network, and the Rise of Digital Utopianism*, Chicago, University of Chicago Press, 2006. Turner shows how the countercultural communities of the 1960s–1970s—particularly around Stewart Brand's *Whole Earth Catalog*—supplied the imaginary, networks, and organizational forms that then structured the California digital economy. The thesis is directly pertinent to BMC: the college's pedagogical and artistic experimentation was, likewise, recuperated by the very circuits it claimed to bypass—the canonization of Cage, Rauschenberg, and Cunningham in the art market being the most visible form.

But—and this is the lesson of the project's ten years—this topological position is no longer stable. BMC could last twenty-four years because the value trough remained a trough: no one sought to monetize the Appalachian Mountains in the 1930s–1950s. Today, the slightest trough is immediately detected by *lift* mechanisms. The project's history at Eden provides the empirical demonstration. During the first eight years (2015–2023), the project was welcomed with remarkable generosity by residents: free lodging in residents' homes, loan of premises, shared meals, time given. The project's economy rested on an informal hospitality the territory produced spontaneously—pure *drag*, human relations unmediated by transaction. Then the project grew. In 2024–2025, when collaborations became institutionalized—co-funding, partnerships with local structures, hosting of larger groups—housing had to be rented. Yet the available accommodations were now Airbnbs—Airbnbs that, ten years earlier, simply did not exist in Eden. The platform had in the meantime colonized the value trough, converting into monetizable assets the same vacant houses that, in 2015, were signs of disinvestment. The project, in growing, thus participated in the transformation it claimed to analyze: its own *drag*—its attempt to re-embed human relations in the territory—generated the signal that triggered a new cycle of *lift*. This is exactly what Richard Florida theorized (inadvertently) with the "creative class": artists as the vanguard of gentrification, their presence transforming the sign "no value here" into the signal "latent value to extract" [^florida_drag].

This mechanism does not invalidate the project—it constitutes its fundamental datum. The *drag* that triggers no *lift* is invisible *drag*, without effect, leaving no trace in the territory. The *drag* that triggers *lift* has touched something real—but must then negotiate the consequences of its own impact. The question, therefore, is not to produce pure *drag*—a fantasy of innocence—but to structure the fallout so that the territory retains a share rather than seeing it entirely siphoned by the platforms.

The topological homology between BMC and Eden belongs to this logic: not the reconstitution of a historical institution but the retroactive discovery of a common structure—the occupation of the value trough as a condition of experimentation.

The mechanism can be further specified. In Poliks and Trillo's terms, capital "hates fixed costs": it withdraws from any substrate that anchors price in matter. The practitioner, by staying at Eden, temporarily reintroduces fixed cost—physical presence, temporal commitment, relation—into a territory that capital left precisely because it had become too fixed, too slow, too material. This is a *drag* gesture in the technical sense: an attempt to re-embed human relations in flows that have made them superfluous. But the gesture is ambivalent. The conduit can channel resources toward Eden: attention, funding, institutional connections. It can also channel extraction from Eden: images and narratives converted into cultural capital, experiences transformed into publications, the territory's condition converted into career advancement. What Maurizio Lazzarato calls "debt"—not only financial but existential, the fact that the contemporary subject is first and foremost an indebted subject, produced by and for credit circuits—applies here in both directions: the practitioner is indebted to the institutions that fund them (and that expect measurable returns), and the territory risks finding itself indebted to a project that extracted more than it deposited [^lazzarato].

The ethical task is not to escape this ambivalence—escape is impossible—but to negotiate it deliberately. *Drag* that serves the common rather than accelerating *lift* requires structural commitments, not merely ethical intentions: direct economic redistribution, infrastructure deposit, skills transfer, network extension, long-term commitment. The absence of these forms guarantees extraction. The project that pays no resident, leaves no infrastructure, transfers no skills, extends no network, and never returns is pure *lift*—whatever its theoretical sophistication, whatever the territorial intelligence it produces.

### Three Postures Toward the Legacy

If the practitioner's position in the territory is always already caught in the dynamic of *lift* and *drag*, the same is true for the manner in which an institutional legacy is received. The question—what to do with BMC, what to do with Eden—is not posed in the abstract; it unfolds into concrete postures, each inscribed in a value economy that determines what is retained, what is discarded, and what is converted into yield.

The first posture is that of preservation. It treats the legacy as an object of retrospective knowledge—to be inventoried, contextualized, commemorated, but not mobilized in the present. BMC becomes material for art history and the history of educational institutions: the archives are conserved (Black Mountain College Museum + Arts Center in Asheville, Western Regional Archives), exhibitions succeed one another (*Experiment in Art*, Hammer Museum, 2002; *Leap Before You Look*, ICA Boston, 2015), monographs add layers to a narrative whose temporal closure is not interrogated. This sequence can be read as extraction applied to the legacy itself: publications, exhibitions, and the museum extract from BMC the elements convertible into value in the circuits of art and publishing—canonical names, reproducible works, heroic narratives—and leave the residue: the racial question, pedagogical failures, governance conflicts, the territory. Preservation reproduces in the symbolic order the same mechanism as deindustrialization in the economic order: it extracts what has value and abandons the rest. Eden is symmetrically the object of documentary attention—reportage on Piedmont deindustrialization, photographic series on emptied mill towns, oral archives—in a logic where the territory is constituted as a subject of study without the study modifying the territory's conditions. In both cases, the relationship to the legacy remains external: the inheritor observes without materially engaging in what they observe.

The second posture is that of repetition. It treats the legacy as a transferable formal matrix—a model whose parameters would be reproducible with contextual adjustments. "New Black Mountains" have proliferated since the 1990s: Black Mountain School (New York), Mildred's Lane (Pennsylvania), Mountain School of Arts (Los Angeles), and a series of initiatives that replicate the format—interdisciplinary residency, horizontal governance, art/life integration—while dissociating it from the territory and material conditions that gave BMC its historical necessity. On Eden's side, repetition presents itself in the guise of *creative placemaking* programs promoted by the National Endowment for the Arts and ArtPlace America: a protocol for revitalization through the creative economy, heritage tourism, and the attraction of the "creative class" theorized by Richard Florida [^florida_drag], applicable indifferently to any declining municipality. The posture is in both cases extractive: it isolates from the legacy the elements capable of symbolic or economic yield and ignores what resists transposition.

The third posture is that of abandonment. It treats the legacy as historically closed—interesting for periodizing twentieth-century American art, but devoid of operative relevance for the present. BMC is reduced to a paragraph in the teleological narratives of the neo-avant-garde, absorbed into the genealogy leading from Cage to Fluxus, from Rauschenberg to Pop, retaining from the institution only what prepares what follows it. Eden, similarly, dissolves into macro-economic analyses of Southern deindustrialization, its fate subsumed under statistical categories that leave no purchase on the singularity of the place. The posture here is that of closure: the legacy is declared over and the question of what might be done with it no longer arises.

None of these three postures is wrong; each responds to legitimate disciplinary demands. But none allows posing the question that drives this research: what happens when one works *with* a legacy rather than *on* it, *in* a territory rather than *about* it?

Anthropology encountered this question before us. From the early 1970s, Clifford Geertz posits that anthropology is not the interpretation of a culture but the interpretation of an interpretation [^geertz]. With *Writing Culture* (1986), James Clifford and George Marcus radicalize the gesture: ethnographic description is intrinsically collaborative, writing is a co-fabrication, and the participant observation inherited from Malinowski maintains an asymmetry that the reflexive turn renders untenable [^writcult]. Practitioners emerging from this critique recognize their interlocutors as co-creators, assume the bricolage of their method, and conceive the field as a space of meaning production rather than hypothesis validation [^terr_risque].

The Eden project situates itself in this lineage but radicalizes it on the performative plane. Since Austin, the performative designates an act capable of producing a new social reality [^austin]. *Performance studies*—born of the encounter between anthropology (Turner), sociology (Goffman), and experimental theater (Schechner)—constituted this intuition as a disciplinary field [^perfstud]. At Eden, the oppositions between doing and thinking, collecting and analyzing, experience and interpretation are not deconstructed in theory—they are rendered inoperative by practice: when a resident draws a graphic novel page that superimposes 1948 and 2024 or discusses the evolution of public spaces, when a summer workshop transforms the junkyard into a performance stage, the distinction collapses in the gesture itself. What the reflexive turn formulated as critical program and what performance studies constituted as a field, the retcon puts into act as a working condition.

### Reactivation and the Fourth Posture

The retcon offers a fourth posture: neither preserve nor repeat nor abandon, but reactivate—and this reactivation proceeds from *fork* to *fold*. The fork opens: it multiplies trajectories, branches from the canonical narrative, puts neutralized virtualities back into circulation. The fold deepens: it folds the archive back upon itself, produces new dimensions of relation between documents that institutions preserve separately, creates internal complexity without external expansion. The archive is treated not as a closed collection but as an open field—an ensemble of materials whose potentials exceed their actualizations, whose significance changes as new conditions make new readings possible. The territory is treated not as backdrop but as co-producer—an active participant in any practice that unfolds within it, shaping outcomes through its specific constraints and affordances.

This posture has historical neighbors that must be identified to situate it. Two examples, without their being the only ones: Theaster Gates, with the Rebuild Foundation in Chicago, transforms abandoned buildings on the South Side into cultural infrastructures—libraries, archives, exhibition spaces—articulating artistic practice and territorial regeneration. Jeanne van Heeswijk, with *Freehouse* in Rotterdam, builds creative cooperatives in disinvested neighborhoods, making communal governance a material of the work. Both practices share with the Eden project the territorial commitment and the refusal to separate the artistic gesture from its material conditions. But they operate by *substitution*: they bring to the territory what it lacks (infrastructure, economy, visibility). The Eden project does not bring a solution to the territory—it opens narrative branches that neither the archive nor the territory explicitly contained, but that their resonance makes possible.

This posture is neither neutral nor innocent. The practitioner who reactivates an archive does so from a position—with interests, resources, and access that others do not have. Reactivation serves ends that must be examined: what questions does it answer? what needs does it address? what does the practitioner gain, and what remains with the archive's source community? These questions cannot be resolved by methodology alone; they require continuous negotiation, the acknowledgment of asymmetry, the willingness to be held accountable.

But the alternative—leaving the archive in its institutional closure, leaving the territory to the sole mechanisms of the market—is no more neutral. Abandonment is itself an operation, with beneficiaries and consequences. It shields from interrogation the arrangements that produced the present situation: the sequence leading from the plantation economy to the textile industry, from the textile industry to offshoring, from offshoring to vacancy, appears in the narrative of abandonment as a natural succession rather than a series of identifiable decisions, taken by identifiable actors, under conditions that could have been otherwise. Similarly, treating the BMC archive as historically closed means accepting the sorting performed by its reception—a sorting that retained Cage and Rauschenberg, marginalized Richards and Wallen, and erased the racial question—as though it belonged to historical necessity rather than to the contingency of power relations in the art world. The retcon does not claim to escape the complicity inherent in any intervention in a field of unequal forces. What it does, and what neither preservation nor repetition nor abandonment does, is make this complicity explicit, constitute it as an object of examination and a condition of practice—not an obstacle to overcome before beginning the work, but a component of the work itself, available for critique on the same terms as the artifacts it produces.

### The Retcon in Action

The Eden Summer Institutes claim a form of autonomy within the project, being at once experimental forms directly tied to Eden and forms in themselves. *Retcon Black Mountain*, more broadly, is the suite of tactical opportunities for posing the question of modernity's haunting at the heart of collective emancipation mechanics. This is done through workshops in European art schools, summer residencies at Eden, interventions on digital platforms like Telegram or Reddit, via blockchains—so many sites where the BMC archive is reactivated not as heritage to preserve but as an operator of disruption in contexts that did not expect it.

Narratives are fabricated collectively in the form of a graphic novel—not as illustrations of an argument but as *storyboards* of a moving film, in constant definition. The video project, conceived over twenty-four years, uses these pages as narrative matrix: each workshop session produces new ones that retroactively modify the meaning of those preceding them. Practitioners and Eden residents collaborate on pages that superimpose temporalities, or are themselves the agents, characters, motors: 1948 and 2024 and 2052 become indistinguishable. Localities are also paralleled: Black Mountain, Eden, Paris, Berlin, Mexico City, Marseille, and tomorrow Casablanca, Saigon, Oslo. The historical BMC appears, but transformed—populated by figures who existed and did not exist, engaged in activities that took place and did not take place. The pages are neither historical fiction (they do not claim accuracy) nor pure invention (they are constrained by archival material). They are retcons: reconfigurations of given facts into new constellations, and the film that will emerge from them will itself be a retcon of the graphic novel that preceded it.

The survey—colonial, urban, administrative—measures the land to make it legible for appropriation. The surveyor claims the view from above: exhaustive, objective, detached from what is surveyed. Research-creation proposes a *counter-survey*: knowledge produced from inside rather than from above. The artifact surveys the territory by traversing it, by dwelling in it, by attending to it with the entire sensorium rather than with the standardized instruments of measurement. What it captures is not dimension but quality—not the surface area of the abandoned factory but what one feels standing in its shadow; not the census tract's population but the texture of a conversation at the gas station, the silence of the abandoned downtown.

### The Territory as Apparatus

The territory is not a décor but an *apparatus*—in the sense Foucault gives to the *dispositif* [^foucault_disp]: a heterogeneous assemblage that produces effects, shapes what can be perceived and thought, enables certain relations while obstructing others.

Eden constitutes a particular apparatus whose components have been described in Movement II: infrastructure exceeding its use, the social configuration marked by the absence of the working class, the temporal structure where the memory of prosperity coexists with the experience of decline. But naming Eden as apparatus adds something the description did not contain: the recognition that these components are not the décor *before which* practitioners work but the machine *through which* their perception is produced. The apparatus makes certain things visible—decline, absence, persistence, the gap between infrastructure and use—and obscures others: what happens beyond the practitioner's access, what residents choose not to share, what the apparatus's structure renders imperceptible. The practitioner does not see Eden; they see what the Eden-apparatus gives them to see.

Territorial knowledge is embodied—held in bodies, acquired through corporeal practice, resistant to complete linguistic articulation. We know places through accumulated sensorimotor experience: feet that learn the specific sidewalks, ears that attune to the soundscape, nose that registers characteristic smells, skin that senses the particular humidity and temperature. This knowledge cannot be transmitted by description; it must be acquired through presence. This is why the residential format—weeks of continuous presence—is an epistemological condition, not a logistical luxury. It is also why the intelligence produced is necessarily *distributed*: held not in an individual but in the relations between practitioners and between practitioners and territory, available as long as the collective persists and the presence lasts, partially lost when it disperses. The filmmaker, through sustained proximity to the sound artist, begins to perceive acoustic qualities they would previously have ignored; the sound artist begins to think visually. Each practitioner becomes something they were not—not through the addition of skills but through the transformation of the mode of attention. This emergent capacity, irreducible to individual contributions, is what the Eden-apparatus produces when it functions.

### The Reversibility of the Retcon

A methodological objection must be addressed. In comics, the retcon is an act of power: a publisher—DC Comics, Marvel—decides that a given event did not happen, that a given character has returned to life, that one continuity replaces another. It is a top-down, corporate rewrite that erases to replace. The retcon as I practice it at Eden is of a different nature: it erases nothing, it *adds*. Nor is it a franchise seeking a surplus of value—it is, on the contrary, a retraction that allows for densification and preservation. It does not decide which continuity prevails—it opens an additional branch from the territory and its material conditions. The difference is that of the *fork* and the *overwrite*: the comics retcon overwrites; the retcon-as-work bifurcates. Furthermore, the retcon is reciprocal—the graphic novel pages are proof: sessions designed to produce one type of artifact produced another; residents seized the digitization equipment, AI tools, and the house itself, rather than the BMC archive. The territory retcons the practitioner as much as the practitioner retcons the archive. Finally, the method carries within itself the problem it addresses: borrowing from the apparatus of corporate serialization to work on a territory destroyed by corporate capital is a condition to be made explicit, not an irony to be resolved.

### Co-Presence of Temporalities and Opening Toward Part II

The retcon produces a temporal structure that is neither linear nor cyclical but what I call *prismatic*. *1933*—founding of BMC: crisis economy, fascist displacement, pedagogical dissent. *1944–1952*—the summer institutes: crystallization into heritage, partial racial integration. *1957*—dissolution: the archive becomes residue. *2003*—Pillowtex bankruptcy: Eden's economic base destroyed. *2008*—financial crisis: the foreclosure wave that produces a \$1,000 house. *2015*—the purchase: an answer to a question not yet asked. *The present of the workshop*—each project session, where practitioners and residents engage the archive under conditions it had not anticipated. *2052 and beyond*—the speculative futures of collaborative narratives. None of these moments is origin and none is endpoint. The 2003 bankruptcy rewrites the meaning of the 1957 dissolution; the 2015 purchase rewrites the meaning of the 2008 crisis; the present of the workshop rewrites the archive.

This first part has established the coordinates—historical, territorial, methodological, and epistemological. Black Mountain College provides an archive—not a model to imitate but a material to reactivate, whose unactualized virtualities (Goldowski's cybernetics, Wallen's governance, women's structural labor, the desegregation debate) exceed what mythology has retained. Eden provides a territory—not a backdrop for artistic intervention but an apparatus that shapes what intervention can produce, a reservative site where disinvestment has paradoxically preserved potentiality. The *Little Red Schoolhouse* provides an infrastructure—a material stake in the territory, a site of sustained practice. The retcon provides the method. Exocapitalism (Poliks and Trillo), in critical dialogue with Stieglerian organology, provides the diagnostic framework. The counter-survey and distributed intelligence provide the epistemological categories.

The second part submits John Dewey's pragmatism—the philosophical architecture of Black Mountain College—to a dialectical examination. If the Eden project inherits this framework, it must be determined what still holds and what has ceased to function when confronted with a territory Dewey could not imagine.

The argument running through both parts is that thinking Eden requires resources that neither BMC's historical practice, nor Dewey's philosophical framework, nor conventional extractive epistemology can provide alone—but that developing these resources requires working *through* BMC and Dewey, not around them. The legacy cannot be circumvented; it must be traversed. Site-based research-creation—collective, embodied, situated—is the form this traversal takes; the retcon is its central operation, the one that allows reworking the past from the conditions of the present without falsifying it.

## Part II: Rethinking John Dewey in the Face of Exocapitalism

Why return to Dewey? Not out of disciplinary piety—pragmatism is not a heritage to celebrate. But because Dewey is the philosopher at the origin of BMC's program—and more than that, because he is reread today whenever the discussion turns to public art, participation, and of course teaching of and through art. His thought is fertile, illuminating, visionary. The Eden project, within which the two Eden Summer Institutes are inscribed, inherits this framework—through the residential format, through the integration of doing and thinking, through territorial engagement, through the conviction that collective practice produces knowledge. Pragmatism is not one theoretical choice among others; it is an inherited condition. What remains to be determined is what survives when it is confronted with a territory Dewey could not imagine—a territory where capital has withdrawn, where structures of incorporation have dissolved, where progressive temporality no longer obtains.

Part I established the terrain—archive, territory, infrastructure—and the diagnostic framework—organology, exocapitalism, *lift*/*drag*/*fold*. Part II proceeds differently. Part I advanced through narration and description—the reader needed to traverse Black Mountain, Eden, the house, the workshops, so that the material would resist the abstraction to follow. Part II advances through argumentation. It is shorter because it is denser: each section performs dialectical work (pose, critique, salvage) that narration does not need to do. The imbalance between the two parts is not a defect of construction—it reflects the dissymmetry of the object. Each limit identified in Dewey's framework is tested against what ten years of work at Eden have produced as experience—not as illustration but as data.

A preliminary distinction is necessary. Eden is a town—a territory of 15,000 inhabitants in Rockingham County, North Carolina, with its history, its residents, its institutions, its own life that precedes and will outlast the artistic project. The *Eden project* is something else: a set of practices—the *Little Red Schoolhouse* acquired in 2015, the Eden Summer Institutes launched in 2018, the archival infrastructure (Dining Hall, The Studies, The Library, Theodore), the practitioner residencies—that operates *within* the town without being confused with it. The Eden Summer Institute is the most visible component of this project, but it is not its totality. In the pages that follow, "Eden" designates the town and its territory; "the Eden project" or "the project" designates the artistic and pedagogical enterprise that unfolds there over time; "the Eden Summer Institute" (ESI) designates the intensive summer sessions, particularly those of 2024 and 2025. Conflating the two would be precisely the meliorist temptation that the critique identifies: believing that the project can transform the town, or that the town is the project's material.

The argument unfolds in six stages. The first two specify what Dewey actually provides—resources of genuine value that any successor framework must retain. The third identifies the structural limits of these resources when confronted with Eden's conditions. The fourth confirms these limits through Bourne's critique, developed in greater detail—the subordination of values to technique. The fifth articulates what survives—fallibilism without meliorism, inquiry without consensus, aesthetic experience without progress—whose terms will be specified. The sixth proposes that these survivals can be *rearmed*—tactically turned against the regime that rendered them inoperative in their original form—and that their recombination with Black Mountain College's past and Eden's present is what retcon, as method, accomplishes.

## The Deweyan Legacy

Dewey's pragmatism did not arrive at BMC by intellectual accident. John Rice had read *Democracy and Education* (1916) and *Experience and Nature* (1925) before founding the college; Josef Albers, without being Deweyan in a doctrinal sense, shared the conviction that learning proceeds through doing and that perception is educated through direct experience of materials. When Dewey stayed at Black Mountain in 1934–35, he did not discover an institution applying his philosophy from the outside—he recognized a practice that had already internalized it. What followed—twenty-four years of pedagogical, artistic, and communal experimentation—constitutes the most complete case of instantiation of Deweyan pragmatism in an educational institution. This legacy remains active: the framework continues to structure, often unwittingly, the way contemporary art thinks about participation, teaching, and territorial engagement. Understanding it is the condition for critiquing it.

### BMC as Laboratory of Democracy

Part I established the historical coordinates of Black Mountain College. What matters here is not the history but the philosophical architecture. BMC was not simply influenced by Dewey; it was, by its own assessment, the singular instantiation of his educational philosophy. Dewey stayed at the college twice during the 1934–35 academic year, and it was in a 1940 letter to Theodore Dreier that he formulated his best-known assessment: BMC exists at the "very 'grass roots' of a democratic way of life" [^d1]. The formula was a philosophical affirmation: democracy, for Dewey, designated not a form of government but a mode of associated living, and BMC embodied this understanding in its governance, its curriculum, and its daily practice. Rice described Dewey as the only man "perfectly fit and fitted to live in a democracy" [^d2] (DUBERMAN, 1972, p. 94). Art occupied the central position—"an integral part of the life of the College and of equal importance with the subjects that usually occupy the center of the college program" [^d3] (cited in TANNER, 1997, p. 152)—not as ornament but as democratic method.

Mary Jane Jacob formulates what results for contemporary practice: "We find in Dewey an unwavering believer in art, not art as subject or objects, but as a way of being in the world" [^d4] (JACOB, 2018, p. 1). This formulation—art as a way of being rather than a category of objects—was what BMC instantiated. And it is what the Eden Summer Institute inherits: the residential format, the integration of European and American practitioners, the North Carolina location, the commitment to collective making as knowledge production. But reference is not repetition.

The question organizing this part is whether Dewey's framework—the philosophical architecture that BMC instantiated—provides adequate resources for what the Eden project attempts, or whether it contains structural features that obstruct such thinking. The answer requires first specifying what Dewey actually provides (section II), then identifying the structural limits of this framework confronted with Eden's conditions (section III), confirming them through Bourne's critique of the subordination of values to technique (section IV), before articulating what survives the critique (sections V–VI).

### Democracy as a Way of Life

Joëlle Zask, the principal French interpreter of Dewey, formulates his central conviction precisely: "Democracy is not an alternative to other principles of associated living. It is the idea of community itself" (ZASK, 2011, p. 29). This is not a political preference but an ontological affirmation. Democratic association is the form through which human beings become what they are capable of becoming. Education, consequently, is the process through which this becoming is accomplished.

Zask emphasizes Dewey's distinctive understanding of self-government. Against the liberal conception of freedom as negative freedom—freedom from constraint—Dewey understood freedom as participatory action: "to govern oneself is not to exercise one's 'free will,' it is to select one's purposes, orientations, thoughts, or conduct in light of the environment, and to reorient the whole when needed" (ZASK, 2011, p. 32).

This understanding links individual self-government to collective participation: "whoever is affected by the consequences of any given activity must have both the right and the power to regulate them in favor of their interests" (ZASK, 2011, p. 31). Democracy is therefore not simply a political arrangement but an ethical form of life. And this form of life, for Dewey as for BMC, was not abstract: it was rooted in a territory—the rural mountains of North Carolina where students and faculty worked the land, maintained buildings, cooked together. Whether this territorial rootedness, transposed to Eden—a territory shaped by deindustrialization and what Part I called the residue of *lift*—can still be thought within Dewey's framework remains to be seen.

### Dewey's School

Dewey did not theorize in the abstract. From 1896 to 1904, he directed his own *Laboratory School* at the University of Chicago—an experimental school where children learned through inquiry, manual labor, and the solving of concrete problems rather than through lecture. The Lab School was exactly what its name indicated: a laboratory, a place where pedagogical hypotheses were tested and revised in real time. Students cooked, wove, built, cultivated—not as recreational activities but as modes of access to knowledge. Arithmetic was learned by measuring boards, chemistry by cooking, history by reconstructing the material conditions of the societies studied. Laurel Tanner, who reconstructed the school's operations in detail, shows that the guiding principle was not "learning by doing" in the sense of a slogan—it was the idea that all knowledge emerges from a lived problematic situation and that the educator's role is to structure environments where such situations arise naturally [^tanner_lab].

Rice knew this experience. When he founded BMC in 1933, he was not starting from nothing—he was starting from the Lab School, whose principles he radicalized by applying them to adults, by adding art as the central discipline, and by anchoring them in a residential community where the boundary between life and learning was abolished. The relationship between pragmatism and the Lab School is dialectical, and it is important to specify it because it prefigures the one the Eden project maintains with its own theory. Pragmatism precedes the school: Dewey had already formulated the principles of experimental inquiry and organism-environment transaction before 1896. But the Lab School transformed pragmatism in return. *Democracy and Education* (1916)—the book Rice had read—is written *after* the school's closure, nourished by what eight years of practice had confirmed, corrected, made concrete. *Art as Experience* (1934) comes even later, thirty years after Chicago. Theory produces the school, the school transforms theory, the transformed theory produces the framework Rice instantiates at BMC. This is a feedback loop—and it is exactly what retcon, as method, formalizes: practice reconfigures the framework that made it possible.

BMC was, in this sense, the Lab School carried to its logical conclusion: if education is experience, then the entire institution must be a milieu of experience—not just the classroom but the refectory, the farm, the building under construction, the governance.

[^tanner_lab]: Laurel N. Tanner, *Dewey's Laboratory School: Lessons for Today*, New York, Teachers College Press, 1997. Tanner shows in particular that the Lab School was closed for institutional and political reasons—conflicts with the university administration—and not for pedagogical ones, a pattern that BMC would reproduce thirty years later.

## What Pragmatism Offers

Before identifying pragmatism's limits, we must specify what it provides. The dialectical method requires that critique preserve what had value. Dewey's contributions are genuine and any successor framework must retain them.

### Fallibilism and the Social Character of Inquiry

Dewey insisted that no belief is immune to revision—and that revision is never the affair of an isolated individual. *Fallibilism* is the principle that all knowledge is provisional, revisable, open to correction by subsequent experience: there are no definitive truths, only hypotheses more or less well confirmed by inquiry. *Inquiry*, for Dewey, designates the process by which an organism confronted with an indeterminate situation—a problem, a perturbation, a doubt—transforms it into a determinate situation through intelligent action; and this process is *social* by nature, because no isolated individual possesses the full range of perspectives necessary to evaluate their own hypotheses. The two terms are inseparable: it is *because* knowledge is provisional that it requires interlocutors, and it is *because* inquiry is collective that it can correct itself. John J. Stuhr captures the implications: Dewey "envisioned a self-correcting community of inquirers who would proceed experimentally in accord with fallibilist norms of 'warranted assertibility,' rather than pretending to discover timeless truths that correspond to what the world is really like" [^d5] (STUHR, 2003, p. 45).

This double principle—experimenting and doing so together—permeated BMC's practice. Josef Albers's *Werklehre* (section 1.3) embodied the first dimension: students approached materials with questions rather than plans. Participatory governance embodied the second (sections 1.1–1.2): the community was constituted through collective deliberation, and the summer sessions intensified this social character by multiplying perspectives. The participatory dimension that Joëlle Zask highlighted in *Participer* (2011) implies, epistemologically, that those who bear the consequences of a situation must also participate in its inquiry—failing which, the knowledge produced remains abstract, disconnected from the conditions it claims to grasp.

The Eden project inherits this double disposition. The artifacts produced during residencies are probes, not definitive statements; the practitioners who gather at Eden cannot produce adequate understanding in isolation—they need engagement with residents whose knowledge of the territory is irreplaceable. But the project faces a challenge BMC did not confront. At BMC, the community that produced knowledge was also the community for which knowledge was produced. Students and faculty were simultaneously inquirers and beneficiaries. In the Eden project, this unity fractures. When Lou Bellegarde conducted her inquiry into public spaces awaiting existence in Eden, or when Jesse Hoyle searched—almost in vain—for traces of local decolonial archives, the knowledge produced belonged neither to the practitioner alone nor to the territory alone but to the friction between the two. It is this fracture of the inquirer/beneficiary unity—not a defect of method—that section IV (Bourne) will examine as a structural deficiency.

Fallibilism itself undergoes a semantic transformation when transposed from one context to the other. At BMC, the experimental apparatus was inscribed in a cumulative temporality: the failure of one pedagogical hypothesis fed the revision of the next, and this progressive sedimentation of experimental knowledge constituted a horizon of progress—modest, not guaranteed, but structurally presupposed. The Eden Summer Institute cannot reproduce this presupposition. Eden's territorial condition does not belong to a phase of continuous improvement but to the residue of economic and demographic processes that concluded without succession. Fallibilism, once dissociated from the meliorist teleology that gave it direction, acquires a distinct significance: it no longer designates experimental intelligence oriented toward progress but a sustained experimental attention to conditions that nothing guarantees will improve—an epistemic disposition without promise of resolution. In other words: one continues to experiment, but without believing the experiment will improve things.

### The Live Creature and Aesthetic Experience

At Eden, aesthetic experience—if it exists—comes not from the overcoming of a conflict but from attention to what persists after it. The practitioners who arrive for the summer workshops do not encounter a territory in crisis—the crisis has passed, the mills closed twenty years ago—but a territory in suspension, where life continues without the structures that gave it form. What there is to perceive is not dramatic: it is the light in an empty storefront, the silence of a downtown built for a population twice its current size, the care a resident brings to her garden despite everything, a front porch composed of flags, pennants, miscellaneous objects—a vernacular assemblage that Elliot Rogosin will photograph or that RaShaun Wilson will rearrange in his pictorial collages. Dewey had a name for this quality of attention. He called it the experience of the "live creature"—and it is his most powerful contribution, the one whose limits will prove most instructive.

*Art as Experience* opens with a diagnosis: "By one of the ironic perversities that often attend the course of affairs, the existence of the works of art upon which formation of an esthetic theory depends has become an obstruction to theory about them" [^d6]. The problem is separation—art cut off from the conditions of its making and from the experiences it makes possible. Yet Eden's situation is a separation of a different order: not art cut off from life, but life cut off from the conditions that made it viable.

The opening pages of *Art as Experience* constitute one of the most sustained critiques of aesthetic ideology in the philosophical tradition. Dewey argues that "the work of art is often identified with the building, book, painting, or statue in its existence apart from human experience. Since the actual work of art is what the product does with and in experience, the result is not favorable to understanding" [^d7]. The argument goes beyond methodological complaint—it is an ontological affirmation: the work of art is not the object but the experience the object makes possible. To identify art with objects is already to have succumbed to the separation that alienates art from life.

Dewey's task is to "restore continuity between the refined and intensified forms of experience that are works of art and the everyday events, doings, and sufferings that are universally recognized to constitute experience." Dewey does not reduce art to the banal; he specifies their common ground: "Mountain peaks do not float unsupported; they do not even just rest upon the earth. They are the earth in one of its manifest operations" [^d8].

The key concept is the "live creature"—the organism in active transaction with its environment:

> The first great consideration is that life goes on in an environment; not merely in it but because of it, through interaction with it. No creature lives merely under its skin; its subcutaneous organs are means of connection with what lies beyond its bodily frame, and to which, in order to live, it must adjust, by accommodation and defense but also by conquest (DEWEY, *Art as Experience*, 1934, p. 13).

This biological grounding is not reductionism but foundation. "These biological commonplaces are something more than that," Dewey writes; "they reach to the roots of the esthetic in experience." The world contains much that is "indifferent and even hostile to life"; aesthetic experience emerges when the live creature achieves "a going-over of factors of opposition and conflict" and their transformation "into differentiated aspects of a higher powered and more significant life." Ruth Anna Putnam captures the resulting rhythm: "Sometimes doing predominates on the side of the organism—the organism does something to its physical or social environment; at other times undergoing predominates—the organism is acted upon by the environment; at still other times, there seems to be activity on both sides" [^d9] (PUTNAM, R.A., 2017, p. 109). This rhythm of doing and undergoing structures Deweyan phenomenology—and it is precisely this rhythm that breaks down at Eden, where undergoing is no longer the condition of a subsequent doing but the permanent state of a territory subject to forces it can neither see nor contest. What must be measured is that Dewey's "live creature" is a *body*—a carnal organism in transaction with a physical and social environment. It is this insistence on the body that gives Dewey his strength and makes him, in an exocapitalist regime where capital operates without bodies (algorithms, financial flows, extraction at a distance), an unexpected resource. The stakes will run through the remainder of this part: the Deweyan categories that survive critique are all categories of the body—doing, co-presence, transaction with the environment, sensory attention.

Aesthetic experience emerges from this fundamental condition. The live creature exists in a rhythmic transaction with its environment—perturbation and restoration, tension and release, fragmentation and reintegration. Dewey's formulation is precise: "The moment of passage from disturbance into harmony is that of intensest life" [^d10]. This is not mysticism but phenomenology: the structure of experience as such, when that experience reaches its fullness.

Dewey specifies two conditions that would preclude aesthetic experience:

> There are two sorts of possible worlds in which esthetic experience would not occur. In a world of mere flux, change would not be cumulative; it would not move toward a close. Stability and rest would have no being. Equally is it true that a finished world would offer no traits of suspense and crisis, and would present no occasion for resolution (DEWEY, *Art as Experience*, 1934, p. 17).

This passage will prove crucial for thinking Eden. The territory is neither pure flux nor a finished world, but something Dewey's schema does not anticipate: a world whose processes have concluded but whose residue persists, providing neither the cumulative movement toward a close nor the occasion for resolution that aesthetic experience requires. This is, moreover, the difficulty encountered by every artist who has come to work in Eden. Amelia Rina, in a 2016 text—prior to Trump's election—takes kudzu as an operator: this invasive plant that absorbs the Carolina landscape, covering trees, poles, abandoned façades, becomes for her the metaphor of an American fascism at work, a silent proliferation that suffocates what it envelops. Sarah Feuillas, arriving for a residency, repeats from invented memory the gestures of a potter from a lost local tradition—a tradition of which only a few traces vaguely claimed on the shelves of the nearby Walmart remain. In both cases, the artist does not find at Eden the "indeterminate situation" Dewey describes—the problem awaiting resolution—but a condition whose very indeterminacy has solidified.

What distinguishes "an experience" from mere experience is consummation and integration:

> Experience occurs continuously, because the interaction of live creature and environing conditions is involved in the very process of living.... We have an experience when the material experienced runs its course to fulfillment. Then and then only is it integrated within and demarcated in the general stream of experience from other experiences (DEWEY, *Art as Experience*, 1934, p. 35).

This understanding grounds the Eden project's post-documentary approach—but it also reveals its tension. The artifacts produced are not documents about Eden but materializations of engagement with the territory. The question is whether they constitute "an experience" in Dewey's sense—consummation, integration, fulfillment. Most do not. The collaborative comics sessions break off when practitioners depart; the mappings remain open; the narratives have no ending. What the project produces resembles less a Deweyan "experience" than an accumulation of fragments—not the integration Dewey demanded but a sustained attention to what resists integration.

### Doing, Separation, and the Practitioner's Position

Dewey revolutionized the understanding of doing. "The intelligent mechanic engaged in his job, interested in doing well and finding satisfaction in his handiwork, caring for his materials and tools with genuine affection, is artistically engaged" [^d11] (DEWEY, 1934, p. 5). Mary Jane Jacob draws the consequence: "We are what we do. Dewey's revolution was to value doing" [^d12] (JACOB, 2018, p. 13). Larry Hickman, in *Pragmatism as Post-Postmodernism*, showed that this valorization rests on a deeper philosophical argument: the Western tradition, from Plato to modern science, has systematically "mislocated" technology by subordinating it to contemplation. For Dewey, "intelligence with respect to materials is fully on a par with intelligence with respect to enterprises that we normally take to be 'conceptual'" (HICKMAN, 2007, p. 118). This is not a plea for craft but an epistemological thesis: inquiry through materials precedes and conditions conceptual inquiry. Albers's *Werklehre* (section 1.3) and the Eden project workshops belong to the same gesture—with the difference that the materials, in Eden's territory, include the territory itself. Brian Dixon, building on Ralph Sleeper's reading, pushes the thesis to its ontological consequence: for Dewey, "knowing is thus an activity of 'doing and making'" and "all doing is re-doing, and all making re-making" (SLEEPER, 1986, p. 124, cited in DIXON, 2020, p. 160). For the project, this means that the artifacts produced do not document Eden: they remake it, partially, provisionally, with each workshop.

But Dewey was not naïve about the conditions under which doing is exercised. *Art as Experience* contains a devastating critique of capitalism's separation of art from life—and this critique describes with troubling precision the position of the project's practitioners:

> The growth of capitalism has been a powerful influence in the development of the museum as the proper home for works of art, and in the promotion of the idea that they are set apart from the common life. The nouveaux riches, who are an important by-product of the capitalist system, have felt especially bound to surround themselves with works of fine art which, being rare, are also costly. Generally speaking, the typical collector is the typical capitalist (DEWEY, *Art as Experience*, 1934, p. 8–9).

Dewey traces the economic cosmopolitanism that disrupts art's link to place: "The mobility of trade and of populations, due to the economic system, has weakened or destroyed the connection between works of art and the *genius loci* of which they were once the natural expression." The artist, consequently, has been "pushed to one side from the main currents of active interest." The result is "a peculiar esthetic 'individualism'"—artists who "feel obliged to devote themselves to their work as an isolated means of 'self-expression.'"

The Eden project's practitioners arrive at Eden as figures of this aesthetic individualism—artists whose practice is separated from "the normal flow of social services," whose work is legible primarily in the circuits of contemporary art, whose presence in the territory is itself a function of grant economies. What Dewey saw clearly was the constitutive nature of this separation: "the conditions that create the gulf which exists generally between producer and consumer in modern society operate to create also a chasm between ordinary and esthetic experience." The gulf is not psychological but structural—a consequence of capitalist organization that no individual choice can bridge. The project's doing serves ends that Dewey's framework cannot fully theorize: practitioners make, artifacts accumulate, but the relationship between doing and purpose remains in suspense. More deeply, the Deweyan understanding of practice as a continuous state of creative attention presupposes conditions—time, resources, social recognition—that are not equally available in Eden and in the circuits from which practitioners arrive. Dewey's critique provides the diagnosis without the therapy—the understanding of the conditions the project must navigate but not the resources to navigate them.

## Structural Limits

Beyond these genuine contributions, Dewey's framework contains constitutive features that obstruct what the Eden project attempts. The critique that follows is not a rejection but a dialectical negation—the identification of limits that subsequent thought must overcome.

### The Meliorist Faith

Pragmatism is constitutively meliorist. Dewey's entire project presupposes that intelligent action can improve conditions, that inquiry produces cumulative knowledge, that problems—without ever being definitively resolved—can be progressively addressed. As Stuhr observes, pragmatism appeared offering "uncertainty and plurality as an answer to past and exhausted ideas of authority," perfectly suited to "the expanding, industrializing America that arose after the end of the Civil War."

Meliorism—the conviction that the world's salvation is neither guaranteed nor excluded, that human effort *might* make a difference—was not accidental in Dewey's philosophy; it was its driving conviction. The pragmatist, as Diggins notes, could be "exuberant and constructive rather than pessimistic" because pragmatism presupposed that the future could be shaped, that present conditions were phases of continuous improvement, that intelligence applied to problems would produce better results than intelligence withheld.

Stuhr is clear about what this requires of anyone who would extend pragmatism:

> These past resources, however, are only resources, and they only partially help. They are not permanent, ready-made, or complete solutions for persons who must think and live in the future. Accordingly, anyone who would think or live as a pragmatist in the future, anyone who would extend but not try to repeat pragmatism, must strive to think and live differently from the pragmatists of the past (STUHR, *Pragmatism, Postmodernism, and the Future of Philosophy*, 2003, p. 3).

But the challenge runs deeper than updating Dewey's propositions for new conditions. Eden presents an entirely different temporal structure. The territory is not a problem awaiting solution but a condition to inhabit. Eden's situation—19.8% poverty rate, median household income of \$47,000 (against \$72,000 for North Carolina), demographic decline, infrastructure exceeding its use—is not a phase of continuous improvement. It is the aftermath of capital's withdrawal—what Part I named *lift*. Deweyan meliorism presupposes that the structures within which "better" and "worse" are measured remain operational. *Lift* is precisely what has deactivated them.

It is remarkable that Dewey himself glimpsed the problem without being able to name it. Hickman recalls that in his 1930s writings, Dewey identified "finance-capital" as one of the "new religions" of his time—alongside political nationalism—endowed with established dogma and centralized institutional authority. Moreover, Dewey warned that the capture of applied science by these forces channels it toward "private economic class" ends, with consequences "disastrous both to science and to human life" (HICKMAN, 2007). This diagnosis anticipates what the exocapitalist framework would name ninety years later—but Dewey still formulated it as a reparable deviation, not a structural decoupling.

The exocapitalist framework developed in Part I allows this condition to be named precisely. Meliorism is a *drag* gesture—it assumes that capital remains within the human organological field and can be reoriented by intelligent action, collective deliberation, experimental inquiry. Yet *lift*, as Poliks and Trillo conceptualize it, is precisely the moment when capital leaves this field: it is no longer reorientable because it is no longer there. Improvement presupposes a substrate to improve; when the substrate has withdrawn, meliorism spins in a void—not because it is false in principle, but because the conditions of its exercise have dissolved. Pragmatism has no category for thinking this withdrawal.

The Eden project cannot operate as if its interventions were going to improve Eden's conditions. This is, moreover, the political reproach made to me during the various town council meetings to which I was invited: art can do nothing. The formula, from the mouths of elected officials, was not an aesthetic position—it was a pragmatic observation, and in a Deweyan sense: if inquiry produces no measurable result, what is the point of inquiry? Brian Dixon proposed conceiving design as "the melioristic discipline"—a process that "cannot be seen as offering guaranteed success" but that functions as "an open inquiry that may or may not achieve the desired result" (DIXON, 2020, p. 149). The formulation is honest and Dixon is right to reject the rhetoric of guaranteed success. But the meliorist prudence—"things might improve if one goes about it properly"—remains a progressive framework. It presupposes that the scale of "better" and "worse" is operational. At Eden, it is the conditions of possibility of meliorist evaluation itself that have come undone. The workshops do not bring back departed jobs; the artifacts do not reverse demographic decline; the residency does not restore the economic base that once sustained the community. Ten years of practice at Eden authorize me to state this without cynicism: each summer, the project produces encounters, artifacts, moments of reciprocal attention—and each autumn, when the practitioners depart, the territory remains in the condition where it was, neither better nor worse. This is not failure; it is the fundamental datum. What the project can do is inhabit the territory, produce artifacts that materialize engagement with what remains, and refuse the meliorist hypothesis that such engagement necessarily serves progressive ends.

### Eden Summer Institutes

The 2024 Eden Summer Institute provides the most precise example. For a month, Bana Haffar, Céleste Rogosin, Pat McCarthy, Skylar Blackwell, RaShaun Wilson, and I worked with Eden volunteers to co-create an audiovisual work. We organized open and free workshops—sound creation, poetic text, photogrammetry of people, places, objects. In the first days, almost no one came. Then, in the final sessions, the space became a site of debate and collective decision-making about what the film would show and say. The resulting work speaks of the heat of the Carolina summer, of melting ice—*ice cream*, *ice cube*—as metaphor for the town's slow collapse, and of ICE, the federal immigration agency created in 2003 in the wake of September 11, which had not yet produced the devastating effects that 2025 would reveal. It is no coincidence, moreover, that Pat McCarthy's obsession with *ice machines*—those ice dispensers scattered throughout the town, vernacular infrastructure of Carolina heat—had already converged, during an earlier session, with the work of Kasia Ozga and Caitlyn Schrader, who conducted a performance around ice whose participants happened to be children of Mexican origin: this was, retrospectively, an intuition of the coming omnipresence of ICE, the word already circulating in the territory before policy charged it with terror.

The film was projected in a vacant downtown commercial space. For the screen, I went to the local Walmart to buy a television; I returned it the next day. The screening attracted a large, varied audience of a racial and generational diversity rare in Eden—where gathering spaces, when they exist, most often remain segregated by residential geography and denominational affiliations. That evening, no one necessarily grasped the political meaning of the triple *ice* motif; what operated was simpler and more fundamental: the principle of doing together, of co-presence around a collectively fabricated object—a gesture almost contrary to the individualism that structures contemporary art in general, where the work is signed, the author identified, the audience passive. Here, the authors were in the room, mingled with the viewers, and the distinction between the two had lost its relevance. The projection—a true co-creation between artists and volunteer residents—was made possible by big-box retail infrastructure: a positive parasitism, a *drag* gesture using the means of *lift*. By Deweyan criteria, the workshop "worked": it produced encounters, artifacts, a shared aesthetic experience. That evening, something took place—and that something cannot be measured by any criterion, converted into any indicator, reproduced on demand. It took place, that's all. By meliorist criteria, it changed nothing: the space reverted to vacancy, Walmart continues to siphon downtown commerce, and Eden's conditions remain what they were. The observation is factual. What the project produces, including when it "works," is the reenactment of a separation—between what the territory was and what it has become—that the artistic gesture makes visible without being able to repair it.

### The Emptiness of Experience

Dewey made "experience" the central category of his philosophy. All knowledge emerges from experience; there can be no knowledge outside it. But can there be knowledge within experience when what demands to be thought is historical catastrophe?

As John Patrick Diggins argued in "Pragmatism and Its Limits" [^d13], the concept proves "cognitively empty" precisely when historical experience most needs to be thought. He invokes Henry Adams, who "turned the question over and over after writing volumes of books trying to understand the meaning of historical events, after observing politics firsthand, after interviewing statesmen like Garibaldi and feeling that the significance of history eluded even its leaders who thought they were in control." Adams's conclusion: "experience ceased to educate."

Dewey's concept of experience, Diggins notes, "had nothing to do with the past; nor did he identify it with knowledge itself and did not even suggest in what way we might learn from experience." Without indicating what lessons we should draw from history, Dewey "simply exhorted us to turn our faces toward the future."

Stéphane Madelrieux, in a particularly rigorous Francophone synthesis of Dewey's thought, helps us understand why. Dewey's entire body of work, Madelrieux shows, rests on a diagnosis: the experimental method has transformed our relationship to nature, but our moral beliefs remain tributary to "pre-modern tendencies that we reproduce through our habits and our interactions with our social environment" (MADELRIEUX, 2016). The Deweyan project consists in bridging this gap—extending the experimental method to human affairs. But this extension presupposes that human affairs lend themselves to experimentation, that is, that they offer a terrain where inquiry can produce revisable results. When the terrain itself has been withdrawn—when *lift* has removed the structures within which inquiry could be exercised—the dichotomy Madelrieux identifies is not resolved: it collapses.

Eden's condition is precisely such a rupture. The territory's situation is not a present experience to engage but a historical process to understand. The mills that closed, the jobs that departed, the population that declined—these are not objects of present experience but traces of past processes whose consequences continue to unfold. Eden's residents *experience* deindustrialization—they live it daily, in the vacant storefronts of Washington Street, in the factory visible from the *Little Red Schoolhouse* window—but this experience teaches them nothing about the financial mechanisms that produced it. The Pillowtex algorithm that offshored production, the leveraged buyout mechanism that dismantled the company from Manhattan—these processes are structurally opaque to lived experience.

It is here that the exocapitalist framework developed in Part I specifies what Dewey could not name. Eden *appears* disconnected—without jobs, without capital, without a functioning productive network. But the apparent disconnection is itself an effect of connection. Capital did not disappear from Eden: it withdrew as a local substrate while continuing to operate *through* the territory at a distance—through the financial markets that dismantled Pillowtex, through the platform algorithms that, between 2024 and 2025, make Eden reappear in the circuits of rental yield as Airbnbs. Computational reality—that of data flows, pricing algorithms, extraction-at-a-distance mechanisms—is not visible at the scale of a town's reality that appears precisely disconnected. But it is this very invisibility that constitutes the problem: not the system's absence but its operation in opaque, rhizomatic, simultaneous mode. Exocapitalism does not describe a binary world (connected/disconnected, present/absent) but a regime in which capital functions as an autonomous environment—a computational pluriverse of which Eden is a node, not an exterior. Dewey thought from lived experience as access to the real. But when the real is structured by computational operations that do not present themselves to experience, the category of experience loses its purchase—not because it is false, but because it is incomplete. Understanding Eden requires historical and structural thought that Dewey's framework cannot provide.

### The Fallacy of "More Democracy"

In *The Public and Its Problems*, Dewey declared that the answer to democracy's problems is "more democracy." This formulation reveals pragmatism's deepest limitation for thinking Eden.

Stuhr acknowledges that "there appears to be no great nation, state, or culture that significantly, much less fully, satisfies the criteria for a democratic way of life. Certainly the current United States is not an example." Roberto Frega, in the most ambitious work in the Francophone reception for thinking pragmatism as social philosophy, showed that Deweyan democracy cannot be reduced to a political regime: it designates a form of life that traverses all levels of collective experience—micro (daily interactions), meso (organizations, institutions), and macro (political structures). Democratic experimentalism, in this reading, requires institutions capable of fostering inquiry at every level (FREGA, 2006; 2015). But Frega's reading makes this point even more visible: if democracy is a form of social life at every level, then its collapse in Eden is not merely political—it is ontological. The territory has not only lost its democratic institutions; it has lost the forms of associated living that made them possible.

Zask's reading of Dewey clarifies what democratic life would require: self-government, as she defines it (section 1.2), is not the exercise of abstract free will but the concrete capacity to reorient one's purposes in light of the environment. Yet this self-government presupposes an environment within which reorientation is possible—resources, capabilities, social structures that allow for adjustment. The condition of the town of Eden is precisely the absence of such resources.

### The Lost Individual and the Problem of Incorporation

In *Individualism, Old and New* (1929–1930), Dewey diagnosed what he called "the lost individual"—a being who retains the formal status of individual but lacks the resources for genuine individuation. The old individualism—possessive, atomistic, derived from classical liberalism—had been emancipatory in its time but had become, with the emergence of industrial society, a prison. The individual was "lost" because the social structures that once enabled individuation had dissolved.

Camille Roelens, in his thorough study "(Re)discovering John Dewey's 'New Individualism,'" provides the most comprehensive contemporary analysis of this diagnosis. He notes that for Dewey, the position at the heart of liberalism "is not found in equality, reason, or conscience, but above all in the individual, their rights, and the realization of their capacities" (ROELENS, 2023, p. 4). Roelens specifies Dewey's thesis: "What he calls the old individualism is more or less the possessive and atomistic individualism of classical liberalism, essentially English" (ROELENS, 2023, p. 4). The United States was largely built on this imaginary, long exploiting what it had of emancipatory potential within a cultural and material framework that mitigated its defects.

But with the emergence of industrial society, "the reign in minds of this old individualism, deprived of the historical and cultural context that gave it concrete footing, led to what Dewey calls the lost individual, that is, a being who has only the formal status of an individual but who lacks nearly all the effective resources of individualization" (ROELENS, 2023, p. 4–5).

Dewey's solution was reconstruction: the development of a "new individualism" through what he called "incorporation"—the integration of individuals into social structures that would provide the material and cultural conditions for genuine individuation. Roelens helps specify what reconstruction would require: "collective intelligence and social incorporation—which justifies 'paying special attention to education' that will enable its development—in the service of making available to each person the effective possibilities of acting, choosing, and thinking in order to define and live 'a personal way of inscribing oneself in the world'" (ROELENS, 2023, p. 5).

The problem for Eden is that the incorporation processes Dewey presupposed have, in this territory, dissolved. The factories that once incorporated workers into social structures—providing not only wages but an identity, a community, a purpose—have closed. The institutions that mediated between the individual and society—unions, churches, civic organizations—have atrophied. What remains is precisely the "lost individual" without the social substrate that Dewey's reconstruction would have rebuilt. But this reconstruction presupposes conditions—a functioning economy, viable institutions, collective resources—that Eden's territory no longer provides. The project cannot reconstruct society on the territory; it can only work within conditions shaped by processes it does not control. Thinking individuation without subjecting it to a horizon of progress or conditioning it on social incorporation—that is, thinking the process of individuation as such, in its transductive and pre-individual dimension—requires the resources Gilbert Simondon provides and that Dewey, prisoner of the meliorist schema, could not mobilize.

The diagnosis is all the more acute in that Dewey himself, in *Liberalism and Social Action* [^d14] (1935), had identified liberalism's tendency to hypostatize its means into ends: liberals "are not slow to hypostatize these means into ends or at least into things valuable per se and for their own sake" (ROELENS, 2023, p. 6), becoming conservatives even as the institutions they defend "become more harmful than profitable to the pursuit of the ends that gave rise to them." Dewey saw the problem; he lacked "a sense of history" to resolve it. His reconstruction presupposed that reconstruction is possible—that social arrangements can be revised to better serve liberal ends. Eden's situation is not the result of deficient social arrangements that could be reconstructed; it is the consequence of capital's departure. More precisely: the incorporation structures Dewey presupposed—the factory, the union, the school, the church—were structures where bodies gathered in the same place. What *lift* withdrew was not only capital but the material conditions for the gathering of bodies. The individual is "lost" today not because social structures have decomposed but because they have passed from the corporeal register (the factory, the square, the union hall) to the computational register (the platform, the algorithm, the flow). Deweyan reconstruction assumed that it would suffice to rebuild incorporation structures *of the same kind*; exocapitalism shows that the very nature of the structures has changed. The observation is legible in the landscape: downtown storefronts display *for sale* or *to rent*, but Amazon boxes pile up in front of doors—capital has returned, in a form that requires neither commerce nor employment nor human presence.

### The Problem of Community

Dewey believed that democracy could only be accomplished when "the idea of community is fully realized." But as Diggins argues, the Deweyan conception of community is "utopian and future-oriented" rather than grounded in historical or present reality:

> Dewey's going back and forth between the inadequacies of present democracy and the promises of ideal community leaves unresolved the problem of how to get from democracy to community by an act of intelligent choice alone. It may well be that the values of community must be present and deeply felt before people will choose community as a way of life [^d15] (DIGGINS, 1998, p. 218).

The only experiences of communal living that endured in American history, Diggins notes, "were communities with strong authority systems, either religious or personal and charismatic" (DIGGINS, 1998, p. 219). BMC's own history confirms this limit. The college achieved a remarkable coherence during its operation—twenty-four years of experimental pedagogy that produced Dorothea Rockburne, Ruth Asawa, or Jonathan Williams. But this coherence depended on conditions—founding vision, committed faculty, specific historical moment—that could not be institutionally reproduced. BMC closed in 1957 (section 1.7).

Eden has residents; it has social networks; it has institutions (primarily churches) that coordinate collective life. But it does not have "community" in Dewey's ideal sense. The artists coming to Eden form no community: they arrive, work, depart—their presence is intermittent, their engagement temporary, their territorial anchoring nonexistent. What there is is the residue of a community organized around factories that have closed. The Eden project encounters this existing social fabric and must decide how to engage with it. The temptation—which Dewey's framework encourages—is to interpret existing arrangements as raw material for democratic reconstruction. This temptation must be resisted. Claude Gautier and Sandra Laugier, in their work on the "politics of the ordinary," showed that the ordinary is neither the obvious nor the banal but what resists theorization—"a difficulty in thinking the ordinary" that is also a political resource (GAUTIER & LAUGIER, 2006). Their approach, nourished by Wittgenstein and Cavell as much as by Dewey, suggests that politics emerges not from the reconstruction of social arrangements but from attention to what is already there—to the ordinary forms of life that theory overlooks. For Eden, this means that the project does not have the capacity, the mandate, or the right to reconstruct the territory's social arrangements. What it can do is pay attention to the life that already exists and participate in it—temporarily, partially, asymmetrically. The collaborative comics, the mapping of vacant spaces, the house digitization sessions where residents took charge of the high-resolution scanning tools—all of this represents not democratic reconstruction but collaborative artifact-making within existing social conditions. When an Eden resident seizes the AI equipment during a workshop to produce a narrative about her own street, the asymmetry does not disappear but it is negotiated. This is what collective practice looks like under residual conditions—not the democratic cooperation of the Deweyan ideal but the partial, negotiated, sometimes failed engagement of differently positioned actors.

## Bourne's Critique

The most profound critique of Dewey's pragmatism came not from traditionalists but from one of his most gifted admirers. In "Twilight of Idols" [^d16] (1917), Randolph Bourne argued that Dewey's pragmatic justification of America's entry into World War I revealed the inherent deficiency of instrumentalism:

> Dewey's younger disciples have absorbed the secret of scientific method as applied to political administration. They are liberal, enlightened, aware.... They have become efficient instruments of the war-technique, accepting with little question the ends as announced from above.... To those of us who had taken Dewey's philosophy almost as our American religion, it never occurred to us that values could be subordinated to technique [^d17] (BOURNE, 1917, p. 690).

Bourne identified what Dewey's framework lacked: resources for evaluating ends. Pragmatism's emphasis on method, technique, and consequences provided no basis for questioning the purposes announced "from above." An approach could "work"—achieve its stated objectives—while serving repugnant aims.

Bourne drove the point home with devastating precision: "If the American intellectual class rivets itself to a 'once for all' decision, it will have abdicated its freedom of conscience, its moral power" [^d18] (BOURNE, 1917, p. 693). The pragmatist who justifies war as a means to democratic ends has already accepted the framing of the war—has already subordinated values to technique.

Within the framework of an Eden Summer Institute, what does the residency do for the town? The question is asked often, overvalued, sometimes in vain. The methodology that "works" for practitioners can extract value from the territory without returning anything in exchange. Pragmatism provides no resources for saying this is wrong. To make that judgment requires evaluative resources that Dewey's framework does not possess—a way of assessing arrangements by criteria other than consequences for declared objectives.

Bourne's critique reaches the Eden project with a precision Bourne himself could not have foreseen. Consider the concrete mechanics. A practitioner arrives at Eden with institutional funding—Villa Albertine, Fondation Hermès, doctoral grant. The funding requires *deliverables*: a film, an exhibition, an article, a thesis chapter. These deliverables are evaluated by the funding institutions according to their own criteria—artistic quality, reach, originality, even if the evaluations are mostly conducted with goodwill. Eden's territory provides the material: its streets, its empty storefronts, its residents, its light, its heat, its history. The technique works—the workshop produces artifacts, the portfolio grows, the career advances. But the *values* that determine what counts as a result are not those of the territory; they are those of the legitimation circuits on which the practitioner depends. The subordination is structural, not intentional: no one decides to exploit Eden, but the framework within which the project operates—grant application, activity report, thesis defense—imposes its own ends, and the technique complies. Bourne saw intellectuals placing themselves in the service of a war they had not chosen; the Eden project risks, at each session, placing itself in the service of a recognition economy whose terms it did not set.

For my part, I attempt to respond through methodological commitments: the artifacts produced during residencies remain in the territory; the workshops include residents as participants, not subjects; the comics and mappings become part of the local archive, not only the practitioners' portfolios. We invite thinkers for public and free interventions, subsequently available digitally at the Eden Public Library. But these methodological choices cannot resolve what Bourne identified as pragmatism's structural deficiency. They operate within a framework that subordinates values to technique, hoping that better technique will serve better values.

## What Survives

What survives this critique? More than it appears. The dialectical method requires that we preserve what had value while surpassing what was limiting. Certain elements can be saved—but only by detaching them from the framework that gave them their pragmatist meaning.

### Fallibilism Without Meliorism

The disposition toward experimentation, the treatment of failure as information, the refusal to demand certainty before acting—all of this remains precious. But these elements must be freed from progressive teleology.

Fallibilism becomes: we do not know if this will work, we do not know if "working" is the right criterion, we do not know if accumulated successes constitute progress—and we act anyway, because what remains requires action in uncertainty. This is fallibilism without faith, experimentation without the expectation of improvement.

Concretely: when a workshop with students from the University of Greensboro produces ephemeral and fragile inflatable sculptures, ultimately seen by few people compared to the resources deployed, when a house digitization apparatus interests only practitioners and the small world of contemporary art enthusiasts, when a collaborative archive attempt fails for lack of participants—Deweyan fallibilism says: this is a lesson for the next iteration. Fallibilism detached from meliorism says something else: this may be all there is—and the attempt still had value, not as a step toward a better result but as an act of attention under conditions that guarantee nothing. The Eden project circulates between milieus—the territory, the art school, the gallery, the doctoral seminar—whose evaluation criteria are incommensurable: what appears a failure in Eden may constitute a result elsewhere, and vice versa. Fallibilism detached from meliorism integrates this incommensurability rather than resolving it.

### Collective Inquiry Without Consensus

The social structure of knowledge production, the recognition that inquiry requires interlocutors—all of this remains precious. But these elements must be dissociated from Dewey's hypothesis that collective inquiry aims at consensus.

Stuhr points toward an alternative: inquiry tied to "practices of power, the operation of interests, and the constitution of selves," transformed from "the mere determination of shared problems to the proliferation of experiences and the creation of a more pluralistic universe" (STUHR, 2003, p. 167).

Collective inquiry becomes: multiple perspectives collide, producing friction rather than agreement. The project's transatlantic composition is not obstacle but resource. The aim is not for practitioners and residents to reach a shared understanding but for their different engagements to produce artifacts irreducible to either perspective. The comics produced during workshops bear the marks of this irreducibility. The video work conceived on-site by Joe Cave, streamed on the television of a motel room rented for the night at the Eden Inn and accessible to passing guests, bears these marks equally—a contemporary art work broadcast through the infrastructure of precarious hospitality, seen by an audience that is not one in the institutional sense. The resulting artifacts are not harmonious syntheses but registers of friction.

This transforms what "collaboration" means. Not: working together toward common ends. But: working in proximity, allowing different frameworks to abrade one another, producing artifacts that bear the marks of friction rather than consensus. The usual marks of disinterest, suspicion, doubt are permitted, and therefore validated. They allow the territory—the only space that practitioners and residents actually share—to become the site where both practices meet without merging.

### Aesthetic Experience Without Progress

The Deweyan phenomenology of aesthetic experience—as emerging from the live creature's transaction with its environment, as the intensification and consummation of ordinary experience—survives critique. The rhythm of perturbation and restoration, tension and release, fragmentation and integration characterizes experience as such, whether or not the sequence constitutes progress.

But recall Dewey's specification: aesthetic experience requires neither pure flux nor a finished world. Eden's condition—the residue of processes that have concluded—seems to fall outside this schema. The territory is neither in flux (the mills will not reopen; the departed population will not return) nor finished (life continues; residents persist; the territory endures).

What the project discovers is that aesthetic experience remains possible under residual conditions—not as the rhythm of problem and resolution Dewey described, but as attention to what persists when the processes that shaped it have withdrawn. This is aesthetic experience unmoored from progressive temporality.

### Critique of Capitalism Without Solution

Dewey's diagnosis of capitalism's effects on art—the museum as mausoleum, the artist as eccentric, the work as commodity—remains precious. It identifies genuine problems the project must navigate.

But the critique must be separated from Dewey's implicit hypothesis that identifying the problem indicates its solution. Dixon, rereading Deweyan valuation theory through the lens of design, shows that for Dewey values emerge from situations of "need, deficit, and conflict" and that valuation functions as a transformational process: one identifies a lack, imagines an end-in-view, acts to reconfigure the situation (DIXON, 2020, p. 162–163). Design becomes a "transformational act of value creation." Yet in the town of Eden, the loop short-circuits: need and deficit are massive, but the means of transformation—capital, infrastructure, functioning social fabric—have withdrawn. Valuation spins without purchase, not because residents lack imagination or intelligence, but because the existential conditions of transformation have been displaced elsewhere by *lift*. Eden is not a town in the driver's seat. The project operates within the very systems Dewey critiqued; it cannot simply extract itself from them. What Dewey's critique provides is not an exit but an understanding—the recognition that the conditions the project navigates are not natural but produced, not inevitable but historical.

These four operations—detaching fallibilism from progress, inquiry from consensus, aesthetics from teleology, critique from the hope of solution—are not merely conceptual gestures. They describe what the project already does, without always naming it. When I return to Eden every summer for ten years, what I practice is not Dewey's pragmatism—it is a use of his categories under conditions he did not foresee. But "salvaging resources" is not enough. The question remains what one *does* with them—whether these categories, extracted from their original framework, can become something other than vestiges. The following section proposes that they can be *rearmed*, that is, tactically turned against the very regime that rendered them inoperative in their meliorist form.

## Rearming Pragmatism

### The Persistence of the Pragmatist Attitude

Ruth Anna Putnam argues that what endures in pragmatism is not a doctrine but an attitude: "James said that pragmatism is a new name for some old ways of thinking" [^d19] (PUTNAM, R.A., 2017, p. 108). If pragmatism names not a doctrine to accept or reject but an attitude to cultivate, then one must identify which elements of this attitude remain viable in a territory that *lift* has emptied of its presuppositions.

Putnam specifies the core of this attitude: "pragmatists believe that the distinction between moral knowledge and the rest of knowledge is a very superficial distinction, marking the ends of a continuum. Descriptions of facts, value judgments (moral, cognitive, aesthetic, etc.) and theories (scientific, economic, literary, philosophical, etc.) are deeply and inextricably entangled" [^d20] (PUTNAM, R.A., 2017, p. 107). This recognition—that fact and value are not separable domains but intertwined dimensions of experience—survives the critique of meliorism.

But one must go further. Returning to Dewey in 2026 is not a nostalgic gesture—a return to a sepia philosophy, a Progressive-era America that no longer exists. It is a *tactical* gesture, in the sense Michel de Certeau gives the term: an operation that, lacking a proper place, "plays with the terrain imposed upon it" (CERTEAU, 1990, p. 60). The exocapitalist regime operates in the computational register—pricing algorithms, disembodied financial flows, extraction at a distance. This register is that of *lift*. The Deweyan categories—the body in transaction with the environment, co-presence as the condition of inquiry, collective making as knowledge production, aesthetic experience as attention to what is there—operate in another register: that of corporeality, locality, shared duration. This is the register of *drag*, in Poliks and Trillo's vocabulary (*Exocapitalism*, 2025). Returning to Dewey means returning to the register that *lift* cannot directly capture—not out of naïveté but out of calculation. When capital operates at a distance and without bodies, insisting on the body, on community, on making together is not an archaism: it is a counter-movement.

This reconfigures what "salvaging resources" means. The point is not to preserve conceptual remnants out of disciplinary piety. The point is to rearm categories. Fallibilism is not a vestige: it is the only tenable epistemic posture when decision algorithms claim certainty. The social character of inquiry is not a memory: it is what platforms dissolve by individualizing every interaction. Aesthetic experience as the live creature's transaction with its environment is not a phenomenological relic: it is what resists the dematerialization of experience by digital interfaces. And Deweyan pedagogy—learning by doing, in a collective, through engagement with materials and a place—is not a program to apply but a *tactic* to adapt: what the project workshops do when they gather practitioners and residents around a scanner, a pencil, a table belongs to a rearmed Deweyan gesture, not to repetition.

The limit remains. Tactics do not transform the strategic terrain. The Eden project can practice co-presence, collective making, corporeal attention—but these practices do not reverse *lift*, they do not restore the local economy, they do not reconnect the territory to the circuits from which it was excluded. What the Deweyan tactic produces is not transformation but *local resistance*—a friction zone where the corporeal register and the computational register come into contact without resolving into one another.

### After Meliorism

The preceding section established the diagnosis: meliorism—the Deweyan conviction that collective effort can improve conditions—spins in a void when the very substrate of improvement has withdrawn. What remains to be thought is what follows for practice, that is, for the relationship between the Eden project and the territory.

But it would be wrong to conclude that the Eden Summer Institute is a BMC that fails. The two are not comparable in terms of form, temporality, or agency—and it is this non-comparability that must be thought.

BMC was a permanent institution: twenty-four years of communal living, students enrolled for years, resident faculty, shared governance, a farm, buildings constructed together. Continuity was the condition of experimentation—one could fail because one stayed. The Eden Summer Institute is of a different nature: one month per year, invited practitioners who arrive with already-formed practices and depart toward professional lives structured by the circuits of art and the university. Eden's residents are not co-participants in an experimental community—they are people living lives that precede and will outlast the project's presence. There is no enrollment, no degree, no residential continuity. The form is that of punctual intervention, not communal living.

If Eden nonetheless situates itself within a personal version of the BMC continuum—and this is the thesis of this work—then this inscription requires three adaptations.

The first is *form*. BMC could be a school because it had students. The Eden Summer Institute is not a school—it is a dispositif for producing artifacts in a territorial situation, whose form is negotiated each year with the conditions of the field, the practitioners present, the residents available, and the material means. The form is not given, it is produced—and it can fail to be produced, as when a workshop attracts no one or a planned collaboration does not materialize. This formal precarity is not a defect: it is the direct consequence of the absence of the stable structures BMC possessed and that Eden does not.

The second is *temporality*. BMC inhabited a cumulative time: each year added to the previous one, students returned, teachers stayed, the archive sedimented. The Eden Summer Institute inhabits a discontinuous time: each session is a restart, the practitioners change, the project's memory rests on the archival infrastructure (The Studies, Theodore) rather than on the continuity of persons. What persists from one year to the next is not a community but a corpus—and it is this corpus, enriched by the computational *fold* described in Part I, that ensures the continuity that bodies cannot.

The third is *agency*. At BMC, Dewey's philosophical framework structured the institution from within: it said what to do, how to do it, why to do it. At Eden, the framework no longer structures—it constitutes a *milieu*. Deweyan pragmatism, detached from its meliorist teleology, provides not a program but a conceptual environment within which practitioners operate: fallibilism as disposition (one tries without guarantee), inquiry as method (one works in situation), aesthetic experience as criterion (one attends to what is there). These elements do not direct the work—they make it possible. The framework constitutes a milieu in Simondon's sense: it forms the pre-individual field from which the individuations of practitioners, residents, and artifacts can emerge—without predetermining the form they will take.

### Against Binarism

Thinking Eden in these terms requires a manner of thinking that is not binary. Dewey's framework knows only two modes—problem and resolution, perturbation and restoration, lack and improvement. Yet Eden is neither "disconnected" (the binary of decline) nor "reconnectable" (the binary of meliorism)—it is traversed by operations that do not present themselves at the scale of lived experience. The *lift* that emptied Eden and the *drag* the project attempts are not opposed forces but simultaneous movements within the same system—and it is this simultaneity that Dewey, a thinker of sequence (problem → inquiry → resolution), cannot grasp.

One might object that exocapitalism constitutes an analytical bias. The objection is legitimate. Exocapitalism does not refute pragmatism: it describes a capitalist regime that differs structurally from the one in which Dewey thought. The passage from a territorialized industrial capitalism to a deterritorialized computational capitalism *dilates* Deweyan categories beyond their functionality—inquiry presupposes a stable territory, fallibilism presupposes a possible accumulation, aesthetic experience presupposes an organism in transaction with an identifiable environment. These presuppositions were not errors; they were adequate to the conditions of industrial capitalism. It is the change of regime that renders them inoperative. Exocapitalism is therefore the diagnosis that explains *why* Dewey is no longer sufficient—it is not the framework that replaces him. What Dewey provides—fallibilism, the social character of inquiry, the phenomenology of aesthetic experience, the critique of capitalism's effects on art—survives the critique. These elements enter the successor framework not as pragmatism but as salvaged resources, detached from the meliorist teleology that originally organized them.

The Eden project inherits from Black Mountain College not a methodology to apply but a question to pursue: what becomes of collective artistic practice when the conditions that gave it a progressive meaning have dissolved? The epitaph engraved on Dewey's tomb in Burlington—drawn from *A Common Faith*—illuminates the stakes with a precision Dewey himself may not have measured: "The things in civilization we most prize are not of ourselves. They exist by grace of the doings and sufferings of the continuous human community in which we are a link" (DEWEY, *A Common Faith*, 1934; cited in GARRISON, HICKMAN & IKEDA, 2014, p. 41). The decisive word is *continuous*. The human community Dewey speaks of is an unbroken chain of transmission and rectification. Yet in Eden, it is continuity itself that has ruptured: the mills have closed, the population has declined, the institutions that carried transmission have dissolved. The project does not inherit a continuous community but a residue—and the question is whether the residue can still be a link.

Garrison also notes that Dewey, through contact with Eastern thought, came to appreciate Taoist *non-action* as a form of "active patience"—"persevering while nature has time to do her work" (GARRISON, HICKMAN & IKEDA, 2014, p. 195). This patience is not meliorism—it does not assume that intervention will produce progress. It assumes only that attentive presence has value in itself, independent of its results. This is perhaps the most unexpected Deweyan resource for thinking Eden: not the pragmatism of transformative action but the patience of a making that accepts not transforming. The answer cannot come from Dewey. It must be invented in the encounter with conditions Dewey could not anticipate. This is perhaps Dewey's most precious legacy for the twenty-first century: not a method to apply but a problem to inherit. How to enable genuine individuation when the social conditions of individuation have been disrupted? How to cultivate democratic life when democratic institutions have been hollowed out? How to practice collective making when the economic bases of collective life have ceased to operate? These questions cannot be resolved by returning to Dewey. But they cannot be posed without him.

### Haunting and Melancholy

But the dilation of Deweyan categories does not only produce a theoretical insufficiency. It produces an existential condition that must be named—failing which the analysis remains in the register of critique without reaching that of experience.

There remains a fact the analysis has not named. Eden is sustained by melancholy. Not a feeling but a structure—in the sense that Freud distinguished mourning (which accepts the loss and frees itself from it) from melancholy (which maintains the lost object as a spectral presence, neither living nor dead) [^freud_mel]. The separation is irreversible but the lost object continues to structure the present without being able to be rejoined. Walter Benjamin gave this structure its best-known figure: the angel of history, pushed toward the future by the storm of progress while gazing at the ruins accumulating at its feet [^benjamin_these]. Mark Fisher, in an entirely different register, named *hauntology* the cultural condition in which the cancelled futures of the twentieth century continue to haunt a present incapable of producing new ones [^fisher_haunt]. Eden partakes of all three: the impossible mourning of an industrial past (Freud), the accumulation of ruins that no progress redeems (Benjamin), the spectral persistence of futures that will not be realized (Fisher). The modernist dream—BMC's pedagogical utopia, Dewey's meliorism, the industrial promise of the twentieth-century American project—took place and ended. This end has already occurred, but in a past that cannot be precisely dated: the mills did not close in a single day (except Pillowtex), the college did not extinguish in an instant, meliorism was not refuted by an argument. Something separated from us—or we from it—and this separation is the material with which the project works.

Every artistic gesture at Eden reenacts this separation. The purchase of the house in 2015 is a *reenactment*: it reproduces at an individual scale the founding gesture of BMC (occupying a space that capital has abandoned) while confirming that the conditions of the original gesture no longer exist. The Eden Summer Institute workshops reenact the format of BMC's summer sessions—and observe each year that the experimental community does not reconstitute itself, that practitioners depart, that the territory persists in its condition. The 2024 ESI film, the comics, the mappings—all of this is the reenactment of a loss whose object cannot be localized. It is not the loss of Eden (the town exists, its residents live, downtown stands), nor the loss of BMC (the archives exist, the works circulate, the name is famous). It is the loss of the belief that collective action transforms conditions—and this belief, which is meliorism, haunts every gesture of emancipation by encumbering it with a horizon it knows to be inaccessible.

During the 2024 Eden Summer Institute, David de Beyter dragged a wrecked limousine across the packed earth of an automobile junkyard. The steering was locked; the wheels set themselves in the inverse position of the road. De Beyter forced the vehicle to execute a turn that its mechanics forbade. The performance is brief: a cloud of red dust, a luxury vehicle that refuses the path imposed on it, the physical impossibility of making the industrial object conform to the trajectory it was supposed to follow. The limousine—machine of the twentieth-century American promise, of mobility, speed, success—can no longer turn. It traces in the dust the curve of a futurity that will not be realized. The fact is brutal, not poetic: the machine is broken, the path is impassable, the dust settles.

During the same Eden Summer Institute, Pierre Gaignard built a still from automobile carcasses and exhaust pipes salvaged from the county's junkyards. The machine—an assemblage of steel tubes, exhaust pipes, and makeshift joints—distills rosemary growing in the gardens adjoining Eden's buildings and houses. What escapes is a vapor whose scent is neither perfume nor motor but both at once—the falsely natural dream of air freshener manufacturers inseparable from the combustion residues encrusted in the metal. The still does not recycle: it does not transform waste into resource, it does not restore lost value. It *distills*—it extracts from the territory a sensible quality that does not separate the natural from the industrial.

Both gestures—the limousine and the still—are *reenactments* in the sense Lütticken gives the term: not the faithful reproduction of a past gesture but its re-staging under altered conditions, revealing what the original gesture contained without knowing it [^lutticken_rappel]. They reenact, in the workshop's present, an ending that took place in an unknown past, separated from our present though structuring the future. De Beyter reenacts the mechanical impossibility of the industrial promise. Gaignard reenacts the coexistence of the vegetal and the ferrous in a territory where one has not replaced the other. Neither repairs. They are material observations—objects that say what meliorism cannot say: that the separation took place, that the modernist dream haunts us—in Fisher's sense—and that this haunting is the condition in which the project operates, not an obstacle to overcome.

Dewey had enabled BMC to inscribe a ground zero—a renewal: everything begins again, experience is the only criterion, education reinvents itself from the base. This ground zero was itself a modernist gesture: the tabula rasa, the blank page, the *Werklehre* that starts from raw materials. Today, Dewey's pragmatism reads differently—no longer as a program but as an incomplete method whose categories (fallibilism, inquiry, aesthetic experience, collective making) function only on condition of being recombined with what Dewey could not think: the past as a resource that is not progressive but structuring, the future as a horizon that is not guaranteed but speculative, and the present as the site where this recombination produces—or fails to produce—an effective presence. Yet this operation—recombining past and future to bring forth a presence that did not exist before the recombination—is neither more nor less than the retcon. The retcon, as Part I defined it, is not a correction of the past but a reactivation of its virtualities under new conditions. It is the method that acknowledges melancholy—the irreversible separation—without resigning itself to it. It does not restore Dewey's ground zero; it works from the point where we are, with the remains of what has taken place, to produce a retroactive continuity that is neither repetition nor progress but *recombination*.

The first of these questions—that of individuation—calls for a framework Dewey could not provide. How to think the process by which a being constitutes itself when the social conditions of that constitution have come undone? Dewey thought individuation *through* incorporation—integration into functioning social structures. Simondon thinks individuation *as* process: not the insertion of a preformed individual into a given milieu, but the co-emergence of the individual and its milieu in a metastable regime where resolution is never definitive [^simondon_ouverture]. Eden's territory, as this part has described it—neither flux nor consummation, neither problem nor solution, but a residual condition inhabited by unresolved tensions—resembles what Simondon calls a "pre-individual" milieu more than what Dewey calls a "problematic situation." The stakes are no longer improvement but transduction—in Simondon's terms, the operation by which an activity propagates step by step through a domain, each constituted region serving as the principle of constitution for the next, so that transformation extends progressively without any global model governing it. Applied to Eden, transduction would designate the passage from one regime (residual, post-industrial, melancholic) to another (experimental, archival, speculative) without loss of the potential energy contained in the disequilibrium—and without the meliorist promise that this passage constitutes progress. It is toward this thinking that the subsequent chapters will proceed.

Let us recapitulate what this part has established. Dewey's pragmatism, aesthetic experience, fallibilism, the social character of inquiry—these categories remain precious resources for linking BMC to Eden. But they do not suffice to name what is at stake. Exocapitalism—a reality that is not physical but incarnated in every empty storefront, every Amazon box on a doorstep, every pricing algorithm operating at a distance on the territory—redefines the conditions under which a work can come into being. It is this observation that compels rethinking the approach: no longer the progressive improvement of a milieu but the sensible capture of a haunting. For what haunts Eden also haunts Europe—the modernist promise of continuous progress, of an industry that gives form to life, of a pedagogy that emancipates. This promise has come undone on both sides of the Atlantic, according to different chronologies and intensities, but with the same result: a present structured by a past that will not return. The Eden project—at once a town and a corpus, a house and an archipelago of practices, a multitude of artifacts produced at different speeds and scales—works from within this shared haunting. It does not resolve it. It distills it, extracting from a territory what it contains without separating the residue from the living. The melancholy of this detachment, announced even in the toponymy—"Eden"—is less an obstacle to overcome than the condition in which any work of art might operate. From *Lake Eden* to Eden, the recombination of past and present produces a presence that did not exist before the work—the *retcon* is what allows us to inhabit this gap.
