## I. From Ritual to Imagination

On the Acropolis of Athens, west of the Erechtheion, stands an olive tree. According to the city's founding myth, this is the exact spot where Athena planted the first olive tree during her contest with Poseidon for the patronage of Athens — the gesture through which the goddess offered the inhabitants a tree rather than a spring of salt water, and through which the city received its name. The episode, immortalized in the sculptures of the Parthenon's west pediment, belongs to mythology. But the tree itself is real, and its history is documented. Herodotus reports in his *Histories* (Book VIII, 55) that when Xerxes' Persian armies burned the Acropolis in 480 BCE, the sacred tree burned with the temples — but that the next day, when Athenians sent by the king climbed to the sanctuary to perform a sacrifice, they found that a shoot about one cubit long had sprung from the charred trunk.[^1] Seeds from the rescued tree were then replanted throughout Attica, so that the olive groves surrounding Athens are, according to tradition, descendants of Athena's tree — the *moriai*, sacred olives, property of the state. In 421 BCE, when the Oath of Plataea prescribing that the Persian ruins be left standing as war memorials had expired, the Erechtheion was built around the sacred site, incorporating the tree into its architecture.[^2]

Since then, at each destruction — and there were many, over two and a half millennia of invasions, fires, sieges, and occupations — a branch was saved and replanted. The last time was during the Second World War: German occupation forces severely damaged the tree. Members of the American School of Classical Studies at Athens saved a branch, cultivated it in a nursery, and in 1952 the tree was replanted at its original location by Princess Sophie of Greece (later Queen Sofía of Spain).[^3]

What exactly does one do when one replants this tree? One is not reconstructing a historical event: the contest between Athena and Poseidon never took place. One is not commemorating: the point is not to remember a past fact but to reproduce a gesture. One is not representing: no one plays the role of Athena. One *reenacts*, in the most literal sense — one puts *back into action*, one *acts again* — the founding gesture of a goddess. And this reenactment, repeated over twenty-five centuries, is far from innocent: it perpetuates the city's founding fiction, keeps active the legitimacy of an origin story. The olive tree of the Acropolis may be the oldest reenactment in the world — and it reminds us that every reenactment, even the humblest, even the most "natural" (what could be simpler than replanting a tree?), always serves a narrative.

The 1952 replanting adds a further layer of analysis. Those who replant are not Athenians but American archaeologists. The American School of Classical Studies at Athens — an institution founded in 1881, the spearhead of American classical archaeology in Greece — perpetuates a mythological gesture in the name of science. The world's oldest reenactment is thus renewed by the very people whose discipline claims to demythologize the past. This paradox runs through the entire history of reenactment: the gesture that claims transparency — replanting, redoing, putting back into action — is always caught in a web of motivations, interests, and beliefs that far exceed the gesture itself. In 1952, replanting Athena's olive tree is also, for postwar American archaeology, an assertion of civilizational continuity between ancient Greece and the contemporary West — a continuity that the Marshall Plan, launched five years earlier, was precisely meant to guarantee on the economic plane.

### The words for it

In French, there is no exact equivalent for the English word *reenactment*. It is variously translated as *reconstitution*, *commémoration*, *reprise*, *adaptation*, *remake* — and one could add *hommage*, *citation*, *détournement*, *cover*, *version*, *rejouer* (with all the term borrows from games, theater, sports, and, since the 1980s, the *replay* of the tape deck and the video game console), without exhausting the semantic field. None of these terms covers exactly what the English word designates; each illuminates one facet while obscuring others. The remake (Pierre Huyghe remaking *Rear Window*, to which we will return) emphasizes the reproduction of an object; adaptation (Ostermeier staging Brecht) stresses the transposition of a text into a new context; homage centers on devotion to the model; détournement on its subversion. Reenactment itself carries a more radical promise in its etymology: *re-enact*, to put *back into action*, not to remake an object but to *replay a gesture*. This essay, originally written in French about an American subject, assumes the resulting distance as a methodological position: to think BMC through Eliade, Kierkegaard, Deleuze, Benjamin, Simondon, Stiegler, and Hui — that is, from a continental philosophical tradition for which the question of repetition is first ontological (what returns?) before being practical (how to redo?) — yields a different light than the Anglo-Saxon empiricist tradition of Collingwood or the performance theory of Schneider and Taylor. This is neither an advantage nor a handicap: it is an *angle*, and that angle is part of what this essay produces. An examination of some of these distinctions — without claiming exhaustiveness — will establish the theoretical coordinates for our inquiry.

We should begin with the oldest, since it is the foundation from which all others descend or diverge. Ritual is the most originary form of reenactment: not artistic, not military, not historical — religious. Every liturgy is structurally a reenactment: the Catholic Mass replays the Last Supper, the Passover Seder replays the Exodus, and the Shia *ta'ziyeh* — inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list since 2010 — replays each year the martyrdom of Imam Hussein at the Battle of Karbala (680 CE), with participants taking the roles of the combatants in a sacred theater that can extend to self-flagellation (*matam*). (In 2002, filmmaker Abbas Kiarostami organized three *ta'ziyeh* performances in Rome — displacing the sacred reenactment into the secular space of contemporary performance, and posing from the outset the question that will run through this entire essay: what remains of ritual when it is removed from its place?) Mircea Eliade, in *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949), showed that for archaic societies, ritual is not a commemoration — it is a *reactualization*: "the festival is not simply the commemoration of a mythical event; it reactualizes the event."[^4] Ritual abolishes profane time (linear, irreversible) and returns participants *in illo tempore* — "in that time" — to the sacred time of origins, which is always present beneath historical time. Participants do not *remember* the myth: they *relive* it, they become contemporaries of the gods and ancestors.

This is why ritual admits neither failure nor approximation. From the standpoint of the participant, reenactment is not an imperfect reproduction of the original event: it *is* the original event, rendered present once more. The priest who celebrates the Eucharist does not *represent* the Last Supper — he *reactualizes* it; the bread and wine are not symbols of the body and blood, they *are* the body and blood. Kierkegaard, who was not writing about ritual but about the category of repetition, nonetheless identified the foundational structure: "Repetition and recollection are the same movement, except in opposite directions, for what is recollected has been, is repeated backward, whereas genuine repetition is recollected forward." What the rite *repeats forward* is not the past as such: it is the *power* of the past over the present — its capacity to refound the order of the world.

Two dimensions distinguish ritual reenactment from all the secular forms that derive from or break away from it. The first is *collective immersion*: ritual does not address individual subjects, it absorbs them. The participants do not maintain critical distance; they are caught up in a shared movement that dissolves the boundary between actors and spectators. The second is *substantialization*. In commemoration, bread and wine *symbolize* the body and blood — they *represent* an absence. In ritual (Catholic transubstantiation being the limiting theological case), the bread *becomes* the body, the wine *becomes* the blood — the elements do not refer to the sacred, they *are* the sacred. Ritual reenactment is the only kind that claims to abolish entirely the distance between the original and its copy: there is no copy, there is only the return of the original itself. This is the horizon — impossible but structuring — of every reenactment: the promise that the repeated gesture is not *like* the original gesture but *is* the original gesture. The olive tree of the Acropolis is exactly that: a ritual gesture that, in replanting, claims to bring back not the memory of Athena but Athena herself — the substantial continuity of the city with its divine origins.

The secular forms of relating to the past are distributed, from this ritual foundation, according to what they retain and what they abandon of these two dimensions.

Commemoration retains the collective dimension of ritual but renounces substantialization. The gesture is ritual in form — one gathers around a date, a place, a name — but symbolic in content: no one claims to bring back the past, only to affirm that it deserves not to be forgotten. Its relationship to time is vertical — it inscribes the past into a moral continuity with the present. Pierre Nora showed, in *Realms of Memory* (1984–1992), how commemoration participates in the construction of a collective memory that is never neutral: to commemorate is always to choose what deserves to be commemorated, and thus to exercise power over memory.[^5] Reenactment begins where commemoration no longer suffices — where one no longer wants merely to *honor* the past but to *relive* it.

Reconstruction abandons the collective dimension (an archaeologist reconstructs alone at a desk) and substantialization, but retains from ritual the ambition to *restore* a prior state in its fullness. One reconstructs a crime scene, a vanished monument, a medieval meal. The gesture aims at fidelity to a state of affairs; it assumes the past is a puzzle whose pieces can be recovered. Reconstruction follows an indexical, almost forensic logic — it seeks to reestablish what was. Its horizon is correspondence: the result is judged by its conformity to a documented referent. In this sense, reconstruction is fundamentally archival: it begins from traces and seeks to fill their gaps. Reenactment borrows from reconstruction its concern for material detail (Horwitz's *hardcores* who lose weight to resemble the starving soldiers of 1864) but adds what reconstruction cannot offer: living bodies in real space, engaged in the very act they are reconstituting.

Repetition (*reprise*), in the sense given to it by Kierkegaard in *Repetition* (1843) and then by Deleuze in *Difference and Repetition* (1968), performs the most decisive displacement. For Kierkegaard — who published the text under the pseudonym Constantin Constantius, a name that is itself a repetition, as though the form could not help performing its content — repetition is neither a recollection (which looks backward) nor a hope (which looks forward): it is "a recollection forward,"[^6] a category of the future. The protagonist of *Repetition* tries to relive a past experience — a trip to Berlin — and fails: exact repetition is impossible, and it is in this very failure that the nature of genuine repetition reveals itself, as the production of difference rather than the reproduction of the same. Deleuze radicalizes this intuition: true repetition is never repetition of the same — it is "difference without concept," the production of singularity in return.[^7] To repeat is not to redo: it is to bring forth, in the very act of repetition, an irreducible difference. Repetition fully assumes the gap between the original and its new occurrence — and that gap is not a defect but the very site of creation. If reconstruction aims at correspondence and commemoration at continuity, repetition aims at *difference* — and it is in this that it is the category closest to critical reenactment, the kind that seeks not to reproduce but to produce.

This is why Benjamin associates the aura with "cult value" (*Kultwert*): the aura is, at its origin, an effect of ritual. The cult statue is not an image of the god — it *is* the god, present in the stone. Mechanical reproduction, by destroying the aura, accomplishes the secularization of that presence: it transforms substance into sign, ritual into spectacle, sacred reenactment into artistic reenactment. Contemporary reenactment — that of Pierre Huyghe, Jeremy Deller, Sondra Perry, whose works we will examine in the following sections — operates in the space opened by this secularization: it can no longer claim substantialization (Huyghe's *Remake* does not *become* *Rear Window*), but it carries the memory of substantialization, and sometimes its nostalgia.

Finally, *reenactment* proper is distinguished by its promise of immediacy (*to act again*, not merely to *redo* or *cite*), which engages a specific epistemology deeply rooted in the Anglo-Saxon empiricist tradition: the idea that one understands by doing, that direct experience of the past gesture yields knowledge that neither document nor narrative can provide. This is the thesis R.G. Collingwood defends in *The Idea of History* (1946) when he argues that the historian must "re-enact the past in his own mind" — replay past thought in his own mind to access a properly historical understanding, distinct from mere philological or testimonial knowledge.[^8] For Collingwood, reenactment is not a metaphor: it is a real cognitive operation by which the historian produces in his mind an act of thought identical (not merely similar) to that of the historical agent under study. William H. Dray devoted an entire book to examining this thesis and its difficulties — particularly the question of whether identity between past thought and its present "re-enaction" is logically possible, or whether every reenactment necessarily introduces a difference that distinguishes it from its original.[^9]

Borges posed this problem in "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" (1939), demonstrating that the literal reproduction of a text in another historical context produces an autonomous work: the same words, written in twentieth-century France rather than seventeenth-century Spain, acquire an entirely different meaning. Borges calls this "the technique of deliberate anachronism and erroneous attribution," and his parable haunts all reenactment practices: it establishes that perfect repetition is a logical impossibility, since the context of reception is integral to meaning. But it also establishes — and this is more interesting for our purposes — that this impossibility is productive: it is *because* the copy cannot be identical to the original that it produces something new. Artistic reenactment, as we will see through the works of Pierre Huyghe and Jeremy Deller, does not merely acknowledge this impossibility: it instrumentalizes it, making the gap between original and copy the very site of creation.[^10]

### From theater to reenactment

A detour is needed here — or rather, we should recognize that it is not a detour at all. If this essay addresses Black Mountain College and its reenactments, the question of theater cannot be avoided: BMC was a place where theater, performance, pedagogy, and the visual arts intermingled to the point of making their boundaries indiscernible. We will therefore examine — through three deliberately chosen figures: Brecht, Kane, and Artaud as he was received at BMC itself — the question of whether theatrical adaptation constitutes a reenactment, and at what point staging ceases to be interpretation and becomes a putting-back-into-action. This trajectory is not a parenthesis: it builds the conceptual tools without which the analysis of BMC would remain incomplete.

Every performance of *Hamlet* is, in a sense, a reenactment: living bodies replay in real space acts and words that have already been performed thousands of times. Staging Brecht today — for instance when Julie Duclos directs *Fear and Misery of the Third Reich* at the Théâtre de l'Odéon (2025), those tableaux written in exile between 1935 and 1938 that dissect the rise of everyday fascism in Nazi Germany — is both adapting a text *and* replaying the 1930s so that one recognizes the 2020s. Yet one does not speak of "reenactment" for theater: one speaks of staging, adaptation, revival.

The distinction is ontological. Nelson Goodman, in *Languages of Art* (1968), distinguishes *allographic* arts — where the work exists independently of its executions (a theater text, a musical score: each performance is an *instance*, not a copy) — from *autographic* arts, where the work is inseparable from its singular execution (a painting, a performance: the act is the work).[^11] Reenactment becomes problematic only for autographic arts: replaying Marina Abramović's *Rhythm 0* (1974) raises a problem that replaying *Hamlet* does not, because *Hamlet* is *designed to be replayed* and *Rhythm 0* is not. Shakespeare's text is a *score* that invites interpretation; Abramović's performance is an *event* that resists repetition. When Abramović herself re-performs her works at the Guggenheim in 2010 (*The Artist Is Present*), she transforms an autographic event into an allographic score — and it is precisely this gesture that provokes debate.

But this apparently clear distinction blurs as soon as one presses it. The case of Brecht is particularly instructive for our inquiry. The *Verfremdungseffekt* (alienation effect) is structurally the inverse of reenactment: where reenactment seeks to *reproduce* a past experience, to cancel temporal distance, alienation seeks to *make strange* what is familiar, to *produce* distance. To stage Brecht today is thus to reenact an anti-reenactment device — a productive paradox that echoes the logic of Huyghe's *Remake*: in both cases, the repetition exhibits the conditions of its own impossibility.

The playwright Sarah Kane (1971–1999) pushes further. Her "experiential theater" — in which the spectator is not meant to understand violence but to *undergo* it — replays the entire theatrical tradition of cruelty, from Euripides to Artaud, compressed into a form so dense it becomes explosive. Edward Bond, defending *Blasted* (1995) against its critics, noted that Kane's images were "ancient" — present in Greek tragedy, Noh, Jacobean drama — and that Kane was doing what every artist does: bringing ancient imagery, "changed and unchanged," into the furnace of her own time.[^12]

But it is *4.48 Psychosis* (2000), her final play, completed shortly before her suicide, that pushes furthest the dissolution of the boundary between text and lived experience. The work contains no identified characters, no stage directions, no conventional dramatic structure: it presents itself as a stream of fragmented voices that reenact psychosis from the inside — not *about* clinical depression but *from within* its psychic space. Leah Sidi has shown that the very form of the play — its fragmentation, syntactic ruptures, silences, refusal of resolution — does not *represent* trauma but *generates* it in the spectator, producing the affective rhythms of psychosis and despair.[^13] *4.48 Psychosis* is not allographic in Goodman's sense: there is no stable "score" to "interpret." Each staging — the Belarus Free Theatre's, Deafinitely Theatre's in sign language, Claude Régy's in Paris (2002) — must *invent* the distribution of voices, the space, the bodies. The play is less a text than a *reenactment protocol* — a set of minimal conditions from which to replay, at each performance, psychic collapse. Kane thus radically inverts the Brechtian relation: where the *Verfremdungseffekt* produces critical distance, Kanean theater abolishes all distance — it *puts suffering into action* without mediation, and it is this abolition that constitutes its political gesture.

In 1949–1951, the poet and ceramicist M.C. Richards, then teaching at Black Mountain College, undertook — at the instigation of the pianist David Tudor — the first English translation of Artaud's *Le Théâtre et son double*.[^14] This translation, excerpts of which appeared in the journal *Origin* in 1953 before publication in book form by Grove Press in 1958, introduced Artaud into the American field and directly fed what is known as *Theater Piece No. 1* (summer 1952) — the event conceived by John Cage and realized with M.C. Richards, Charles Olson, Robert Rauschenberg, David Tudor, and Merce Cunningham.

The event reenacted nothing, reconstructed nothing. It floated in a state of indetermination and in the singularity of its form — it is considered the first *happening* in art history — certainly not a reenactment but an *activation*: Artaud's text — which had remained a program on paper during the author's own lifetime — passed for the first time into space and into bodies. The arrangement of chairs followed Artaud's prescription of the spectator "engulfed and physically affected" by the action. Richards read poems from the top of a ladder — or a basket, or on horseback: accounts diverge, and this very uncertainty is significant. Olson read, Tudor played piano, Rauschenberg played records, Cunningham danced, Cage was silent for durations determined by chance. Nothing was coordinated, nothing was rehearsed, nothing was scriptable.

What matters for our purposes is that *Theater Piece No. 1* is, by design, *non-reproducible*. The absence of a score, a script, a prior rehearsal, the uncoordinated simultaneity of actions, the dependence on the specific people present that day in that place: everything conspires to make the event a purely autographic act in Goodman's sense — an act that by nature resists any repetition. And it is remarkable that this project was accompanied precisely by *The Theater and Its Double*: Artaud's text — itself a program never fully realized during his lifetime, a theatrical utopia that existed only as writing — found at BMC not a "staging" but an *activation* that, in the very act of realizing it, made it irreversibly singular. The most famous text on the non-reproducibility of theater engenders a non-reproducible event. It is this structure — a program that, in being realized, produces the irreproducible — that later attempts to "redo BMC" would have to confront.

For to redo *Theater Piece No. 1* is not like reviving a play by Brecht or Kane: there is no text to reinterpret, only an event that happened once, in a specific place (the dining hall of the Lake Eden Campus), with specific people, under conditions that no one can reproduce. This is why attempts to "redo" BMC — which we will examine in detail in the following sections — constantly oscillate between theatrical logic (reviving a pedagogical model, restaging a device) and the logic of reenactment (attempting to reproduce the irreproducible). And this is why the question posed by Kane — at what point does staging cease to protect and begin to reenact? — is a question that BMC posed before her, as early as 1952, by treating Artaud not as a text to be staged but as a program to be *put into action*.

Yet this apparent transparency of the gesture — "we simply redo" — is deceptive, and this is precisely the stake of this essay. Of all the practices of relating to the past discussed here, reenactment may be the most naturalized: the one that presents itself as the most obvious, the most straightforward, the least burdened with theoretical presuppositions. This is precisely what makes it ideologically powerful. Where reconstruction displays its hypothetical dimension (one *reconstructs* because one does not know exactly), where commemoration assumes its symbolic function (one *honors*, one does not reproduce), where repetition (in the Deleuzian sense) claims the difference produced in the return, reenactment tends to deny the temporal distance separating it from its object. It posits that the past is available, replayable, and that replaying it suffices to understand it. It essentializes the gesture by presenting it as "natural" — as natural as replanting a tree.

### The politics of reenactment

This political dimension of reenactment is far from abstract. Horwitz has shown how American Civil War battle reenactments — the most massive and codified reenactment practice in the world, mobilizing tens of thousands of participants each year — are never neutral exercises in historical knowledge.[^15] They are shot through with powerful political affects: nostalgia for a vanished order, glorification of the Confederate "Lost Cause," reconfiguration of the past in the service of present identities. The opposition between historical fidelity and infidelity masks the essential point: that "fidelity" itself is a political choice, and that the "most faithful" reenactment is not necessarily the most neutral. (Paul Virilio recalled in *War and Cinema* (1984) that the Wehrmacht reenacted its own battles before cameras — the Norway campaign of 1940, in which Allied forces were defeated, was reconstructed after the fact for Reich propaganda films: reenactment as weapon of war, reconstruction in the service of intimidation.)

Rebecca Schneider, in *Performing Remains: Art and War in Times of Theatrical Reenactment* (2011), extends this analysis by showing how reenactment, in its pursuit of "literal precision," produces a "temporal drag" — a temporal pull, a slippage — that reveals the power dynamics inscribed in every attempt to replay the past.[^16] Schneider is particularly interested in how reenactment troubles the distinction between the "live" and the archive, between performance (assumed ephemeral) and the document (assumed permanent). By replaying a past event, reenactment claims to revive what is dead — but in doing so, it reveals that the past is never simply "past": it continues to act in the present, often in unrecognized ways. Reenactment makes visible this persistent action of the past — but it can also, paradoxically, neutralize it by transforming it into spectacle.

Jennifer Allen, in "Einmal ist keinmal," her essay for the catalogue of the exhibition *Life, Once More* (2005), draws a radical consequence from this persistence: every reenactment presupposes a *missing body*.[^17] The very term *enactment* refers simultaneously to law (the decree, the edict) and to theater (dramatization, performance) — and in both cases, a single body has been replaced by an infinite series of interchangeable bodies. Democratic law is embodied by no one (unlike the absolute monarch who signed "Such is our pleasure"): it is *enacted* by the abstract collectivity and then *reenacted* by countless citizens. The theatrical script works the same way: it substitutes for the original body an infinite series of possible bodies. Behind every reenactment, there is what Allen calls a "little death": the disappearance of the original body that makes substitution possible — and necessary. This idea illuminates the fundamental paradox: reenactment does not seek to reproduce an event but to *fill an absence* — that of the body that lived the event and is no longer there to bear witness. It is a work of mourning as much as a work of memory.

Allen pushes the analysis further: reenactment uses the body as the medium for reproducing the past, making it a form of natural history, egalitarian in principle — everyone has a body, hence the same means of reproducing history. There is no property in reenactment, no possession that outlasts the breath. But this equality of principle is contradicted by the power structures that determine *which* past is replayed, *by whom*, and *for whom*. Allen shows that reenactment rests on a linear construction of time that spatializes the past and treats it as an architectural site — and that this spatialization has violent consequences: non-Western societies appear as belated reenactments of Western history, compelled to "catch up" with a present over which the West appoints itself guardian. Johannes Fabian, in *Time and the Other: How Anthropology Makes Its Object* (1983), gave this mechanism a decisive name: the "denial of coevalness."[^18] Anthropology, Fabian shows, constituted itself by placing its object in *another time*: so-called "primitive" societies are described not as different in space (which would imply they coexist with us) but as backward in time (which situates them in our past). Reenactment inherits this temporal structure: it presupposes a *bygone* past, separated from the present by a distance that the replaying gesture claims to bridge — but this distance is not neutral, it is the product of a history of domination. Lütticken, in a 2022 essay that extends his 2005 analyses, gives this mechanism a striking name: "temporal colonialism" — "the fantasy of time travel reveals itself as temporal colonialism, as history is reenacted in order to perpetuate the present order that sprang from it."[^19] The violence of reenactment lies here: in the presupposition that the past is a territory to visit, and that only some hold the ticket.[^20]

Horwitz describes the most committed participants in these reconstructions — the *hardcores* — as seeking a "period rush": a momentary illusion of being truly in the past, like a sudden breach in the wall of time.[^15] Lütticken, in the programmatic essay he devotes to the same exhibition as Allen, notes that this immersive experience distinguishes reenactment from mere spectacle: the reenactor does not want to *watch* the past, he wants to *inhabit* it — even if only for a few hours, even if only in fiction.[^21] The "period rush" is the secular version of what Eliade described as the return *in illo tempore*: the participant in ritual does not *remember* mythic time, he *returns* to it — and the reenactor, likewise, does not remember Gettysburg, he is there. Except that ritual believes in substantialization (the bread *becomes* the body), while the reenactor knows he is playing — and this awareness of fiction, far from diminishing the experience, constitutes its condition. One can experience the "period rush" only if one knows it is fictive — just as one can be frightened at the cinema only if one knows the film is not real. Reenactment is a ritual that has internalized its own skepticism — a post-auratic ritual, if you will, whose power rests no longer on belief in the real presence but on the *willing suspension of disbelief* (Coleridge).

Walter Benjamin must be invoked here, for his concept of the *aura* works beneath the entire question of reenactment, even though Benjamin obviously did not use the term. In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935–1939), Benjamin defines the aura as "the unique apparition of a distance, however near it may be" (*einmalige Erscheinung einer Ferne, so nah sie sein mag*).[^22] The aura is what belongs to the here-and-now of the original (*Hier und Jetzt*), to its embeddedness in tradition, to the uniqueness of its existence in the place where it is found. Mechanical reproduction — photography, film — destroys this aura by detaching the reproduced object from its rootedness in tradition and substituting for its unique existence a serial one. (One might also think of the Duchampian readymade and the long tradition it opens: Duchamp's gesture — signing an industrial urinal, that is, designating an object *already* in series as a unique work — is the exact inverse of Benjaminian mechanical reproduction, and yet its complement: if Benjamin shows that reproduction destroys the aura, Duchamp shows that an act of designation can *fabricate* one, however ironically. Reenactment oscillates between these two gestures: destroying the aura of the original through repetition, or fabricating a new one through the very act of repetition.)

Reenactment maintains a paradoxical relationship with the aura. On one hand, it appears to be the anti-mechanical-reproduction par excellence: it does not copy an object, it *replays* an act, it mobilizes living bodies in real space, it is by definition irreducible to seriality. In this respect, it claims to restore exactly what mechanical reproduction destroys — the here-and-now, the uniqueness of the event. On the other hand, this very claim is its trap: by seeking to restore the aura, reenactment merely confirms that it is lost. Reenactment is, structurally, a mourning that mistakes itself for a resurrection. Benjamin had sensed this: in "A Short History of Photography" (1931), he notes that the aura is not a property of the object but an effect of distance — and that "to bring things closer to oneself" (*die Dinge sich näherzubringen*), even through reproduction, is a desire as passionate as it is destructive.[^23] Reenactment pushes this logic to its limit: it does not merely bring the past closer, it claims to *inhabit* it.

Among the countless practices of homage, détournement, repetition, remake, and reenactment that run through art history, the most productive works are those that do not attempt to restore the aura but to exhibit the conditions of its impossibility. Benjamin, however, poses the problem from the machines of his era — photography, cinema — that is, from devices that *record* and *play back*. The question shifts considerably when thought from machines that *calculate* and *generate* — contemporary machines, those of artificial intelligence. To think this shift, one must take a step that Benjamin could not take, and that the philosophy of technology has taken after him.

### Reproduction is an imagination

I propose a simple idea: we have never needed machines to reproduce. The human mind is already, in itself, a reproduction device. When we remember a face, we do not retrieve a photograph stored in our brain: we *reconstruct* an image from partial traces, filling the gaps with invention. Memory is always a montage — a reproduction that, in the very act of reproducing, *imagines*. Gilbert Simondon, in *Imagination and Invention* (lectures delivered between 1965 and 1966), described this process with precision: the mental image is not a passive copy of reality, it is a *cycle* passing through phases — perceptual impression, memory that transforms it, imagination that recombines it, invention that draws something new from it. At each phase, the image is modified. The cycle is never closed: each "reproduction" is already a transformation.[^24]

Bernard Stiegler extended this analysis by showing that memory technologies — from writing to cinema, from the phonograph to the digital — are not mere tools in the service of remembrance: they *condition* what we can remember. Stiegler calls these supports "tertiary retentions": externalized memories (a book, a film, a digital archive) that shape from within our capacity to perceive and to remember. The consequence is radical: there is no "natural" memory prior to its technical prostheses. All memory is already technically equipped — and therefore all reproduction is already a technical operation of imagination.[^25]

Yuk Hui pushes the argument a step further. If Stiegler's tertiary retentions are past memories externalized in technical supports, Hui shows that technology does not merely conserve: it *anticipates*. He proposes the concept of "tertiary protention" to designate the way in which technical systems — computational systems in particular — do not merely store past data but generate projections, models, scenarios. A predictive algorithm, a language model, a simulation: these are machines for producing the future from the past. Technical reproduction is no longer merely a question of memory — it is a question of *machinic imagination*, of fabricating possible futures from inherited data.[^26]

We arrive here at a thesis that reverses the Benjaminian perspective: to reproduce is not the opposite of imagining — to reproduce *is* an imagination. Every act of reproduction — whether mental, mechanical, or computational — is an operation that transforms its material in the very act of rendering it. Memory imagines. Photography imagines. AI imagines. And reenactment, therefore, is not a more or less successful attempt to *copy* the past: it is an *equipped imagination* of the past — an operation that, by replaying through a device (bodies, a place, a protocol, sometimes a machine), necessarily produces something other than what it replays.

This thesis changes the question. Instead of asking "Is this reenactment faithful to the original?" (the reconstruction question), or "Does this reenactment produce difference?" (the Deleuzian repetition question), one can ask: what *kind* of imagination does this reenactment deploy? Does it imagine the past to confirm it (like the olive tree of 1952, which imagines civilizational continuity) or to displace it (like Huyghe's *Remake*, which imagines what *Rear Window* becomes when stripped of its means)? This question — what kind of imagination? — will be the thread running through our examination of Black Mountain College.

Sven Lütticken, in the catalogue *Life, Once More: Forms of Reenactment in Contemporary Art* (Witte de With, 2005), notes that reenactment had become, at the turn of the twenty-first century, a veritable "cottage industry" in the field of contemporary art.[^21] But Lütticken situates this proliferation in a larger context: that of a generalized crisis of historicity, in which the relationship to the past — and the future — can no longer be taken for granted. If artists begin to replay the past, it is not out of nostalgia but because the temporal categories themselves — before, after, progress, rupture — have lost their self-evidence. Reenactment would then be less a relationship to the past than a symptom of our inability to project ourselves into the future — unless, in Hui's terms, it is an attempt to *produce* protentions (anticipations of the future) from retentions (memories of the past). Lütticken's essay "From Re- to Pre- and Back Again" (2022) explores this hypothesis by analyzing the shift from reenactment to "pre-enactment" — the staging of a future that has not (yet) taken place.[^19]

Reenactment is thus, paradoxically, both the most naïve and the most charged form of our relationship to the past. The most naïve because it claims transparency of gesture; the most charged because this very claim is an act of imagination — and all imagination is political. This is exactly what the olive tree of the Acropolis shows us: an apparently simple gesture — replanting a tree — that deploys a powerful imagination, that of civilizational continuity, and naturalizes it to the point of invisibility.

It is with this double perspective — reenactment as naturalized ideological practice, and as imagination of the past — that we propose to examine a particular case: Black Mountain College (1933–1957). Since its closure, this experimental "free university" of North Carolina has been the object of a remarkable number of attempts to "redo" — museum retrospectives, artist residencies, pedagogical programs, festivals, research-creation projects — which constitute, to varying degrees, reenactments. The question running through them all is this: of what kind of imagination are these reenactments the name?

## II. Reenactment in Contemporary Art: A Repertoire

Since the 1990s, reenactment has become one of the major strategies of contemporary art — what Sven Lütticken calls, not without irony, a "cottage industry" of relating to the past. This proliferation is no accident. It coincides with the emergence of a technical culture of *replay*: the VHS and the remote control make the gesture of "rewind and replay" quotidian; video game consoles introduce saving and reloading — the possibility of *redoing* a sequence until it is mastered; sampling in music and remixing in digital culture make the reworking of pre-existing material a mode of creation in its own right. Nicolas Bourriaud, in *Postproduction* (2002) — an extension of his *Relational Aesthetics* — theorizes this shift: the contemporary artist is no longer a producer of original forms but an *operator of postproduction*, a "semionaute" who navigates existing signs, selects, recontextualizes, re-edits. The emblematic figures of this culture are the DJ and the programmer — two figures who do not create *ex nihilo* but from materials already at hand.[^27] Artistic reenactment belongs to this landscape: it is the *performative* version of postproduction — not re-editing a video tape but putting bodies back into action.

What follows does not claim to be an exhaustive inventory of these practices: the field is too vast, the positions too mobile. We offer instead an oriented repertoire — a set of cases chosen because each, in its own way, allows us to pose the question running through this essay: *what kind of imagination does this reenactment deploy?* We will distinguish several approaches, beginning with the one that poses the problem most directly: re-performance.

### Replaying the body

Re-performance — the act of replaying, with living bodies, a past performance artwork — is the site where the question of reenactment poses itself with the greatest ontological violence. Performance art, as it constituted itself in the 1960s–70s, rests on an implicit pact: what takes place takes place only once. The artist's body, in the here-and-now of the action, is simultaneously medium, support, and work. Nothing subsists — except traces (photographs, videos, witness accounts) that are precisely *not* the work. Diana Taylor, in *The Archive and the Repertoire* (2003), theorized this distinction between the archive (documents that survive the event) and the repertoire (embodied memory transmitted through gesture, voice, body). Performance belongs to the repertoire: it disappears with the body that carries it.[^28]

To replay a performance is thus to attempt to bring back what, by definition, does not return. When Marina Abramović presents *Seven Easy Pieces* at the Guggenheim in New York (2005) — seven evenings, seven re-performances of canonical works by Bruce Nauman (*Body Pressure*, 1974), Vito Acconci (*Seedbed*, 1972), Valie Export (*Action Pants: Genital Panic*, 1969), Gina Pane (*The Conditioning*, 1973), Joseph Beuys (*How to Explain Pictures to a Dead Hare*, 1965), and two of her own pieces — the gesture is ambiguous. On one hand, it claims to solve the problem of transmission: how to transmit an art that, by nature, refuses fixation? Abramović answers: through the body, still and always. On the other hand, the institutional context radically transforms the nature of the act: these performances, conceived in alternative spaces for limited audiences, replayed at the Guggenheim before hundreds of ticketed spectators, shift from "cult value" to "exhibition value" in Benjamin's sense. The reenactment *succeeds* (the performances are transmitted, the audience accesses them) while *failing* (the conditions that made these acts events — risk, marginality, uncertainty — have vanished).

This is exactly the tension that Goodman allows us to name: Abramović attempts to transform an autographic art (the work = the singular event) into an allographic art (the work = a score that others can execute). And this attempt is itself an act of imagination — not the restitution of the past but the *fabrication* of a performance tradition, with its classics, its canons, its "role revivals." The imagination at work here is an *institutionalizing* imagination: it creates the conditions for a history of performance art by making it reproducible — at the cost of what made it irreducible.

### Replaying the event

The second family of reenactments targets not artworks but historical events — and it is here that the practice meets its oldest tradition, that of the battle reenactments described by Horwitz. But the contemporary artists who seize this tradition turn it against itself.

Jeremy Deller, with *The Battle of Orgreave* (2001), produced one of the most discussed works of contemporary reenactment. On June 18, 1984, at Orgreave in Yorkshire, a mounted police charge against striking miners had marked a turning point in the British coal strike — and, beyond that, a moment of rupture in the social history of the United Kingdom under Thatcher. Seventeen years later, Deller organized a reenactment of this confrontation on the site itself, with former miners, retired police officers, and professional *reenactors* — those hobbyists of Napoleonic and medieval battle reconstructions who constitute, in Britain, a codified social milieu. The film, directed by Mike Figgis, is projected on two facing screens — miners on one side, police on the other — reproducing the front line. The operation is twofold: to inject a recent and still painful political event into the supposedly "apolitical" tradition of British reenactment (reenactors typically perform battles old enough not to offend anyone), and to pass the event once more through the very bodies of those who lived it. The imagination at work here is a *retributive* imagination: the reenactment restores class conflict's visibility by forcing it to traverse once more the bodies that carry its memory.

Rod Dickinson, with *The Milgram Re-enactment* (2002), shifts the problem toward the scientific protocol: he meticulously reconstructs Stanley Milgram's obedience experiment (Yale, 1961), replayed as an artistic performance before an audience. Artur Żmijewski, with *Repetition* (2005), goes further still: he reenacts the Stanford prison experiment (Zimbardo, 1971) with unemployed Poles — not as *representation* but as *real experience*, presented at the Polish Pavilion of the Venice Biennale. Dickinson and Żmijewski pose the same question by opposite routes: what happens when you replay a protocol designed to reveal the violence of power relations? Does the reenactment *reproduce* the violence (and if so, is it ethically justifiable?) or *illuminate* it (and if so, is it politically necessary)? The distinction is not always as clear-cut as one would wish — and it is this very indistinction that constitutes the strength, and the danger, of these works.

### Replaying the image

The artistic remake constitutes perhaps the most theoretically dense form of contemporary reenactment. As François Bovier showed in his analysis of Pierre Huyghe,[^29] the remake goes further than the Borgesian parable analyzed above: it does not reproduce literally, it *expropriates* the original and opposes it with a simulacrum. The cinematic reenactment is thus neither homage, nor appropriation, nor metacritique: it is, in Masséra's formulation, "mise en situation as critique of mise en scène."[^30]

These practices must be situated in context. Pierre Huyghe and Philippe Parreno — who collaborated on the project *No Ghost Just a Shell* (1999–2002) — are central figures in what the critic Nicolas Bourriaud theorized in 1998 as "relational aesthetics": an art that would take human relations and their social context as its horizon rather than the production of autonomous objects.[^31] The question then arises: do their remakes, their reconstructions, their algorithmic "resurrections" (*Marilyn* by Parreno, 2012) amount to relational aesthetics applied to memory?

The answer is more complex than it seems. Huyghe himself has always maintained distance from this label, and his works hardly correspond to the Bourrialdian model of shared conviviality. What is "relational" in *Remake* or *The Third Memory* is not the relationship between spectators — there is nothing convivial in the spectacle of amateur actors failing to replay Hitchcock — it is the relationship between temporal strata: Hollywood 1954 and a Parisian building under renovation in 1994; a 1972 bank robbery, its live television coverage, its 1975 Hollywood adaptation, and its 2000 artistic reconstruction. If there is a relational aesthetics here, it is a relational aesthetics *of time* — where the material is not present sociability but the friction between heterogeneous memories.

Claire Bishop criticized relational aesthetics for presupposing that all relations are inherently good, that conviviality suffices as a political horizon.[^32] Reenactment as practiced by Huyghe, Parreno, or Omer Fast presupposes nothing of the sort: the relationship to the past can be violent (Deller at Orgreave), spectral (Sondra Perry with *phantom. menace.*), parasitic (Huyghe with Hitchcock), impossible (David Claerbout with *Vietnam, 1967*).[^33] This is an *antagonistic* relational aesthetics, in Bishop's sense — where what is at stake is not reconciliation but conflict between incompatible temporal regimes.

The founding work is *Remake* (1994–1995) by Pierre Huyghe: a shot-by-shot reshooting of *Rear Window* (Hitchcock, 1954) with amateur actors in a Parisian building under renovation. The operation is first a *subtraction*: amateur video replaces 35mm Technicolor, Pizza Hut replaces dinner at Maxim's, a thermometer reading 13°C opens the film in place of the inaugural pan. The actress playing Lisa "is not going to take an interest in the person Grace Kelly plays but in the way Grace Kelly plays the character" (Huyghe): this is reenactment squared, what Jean-Christophe Royoux calls "audiovisual karaoke."[^34] The actors, discovering the script only hours before shooting, exhibit "their difficulties, hesitations, silences" — this is precisely what Huyghe records. The palimpsest is inverted: normally the second text covers the original, but here *Remake* paradoxically renders *Rear Window* more visible, restoring visibility after twenty years of distribution rights being blocked. The gesture is Benjaminian: the *arte povera* quality of the operation shows that the destruction of the aura liberates critical possibilities that devotion to the "great original" had blocked. The Huyghean "aesthetics of reconstruction," which Bovier identified as inaugural, is in fact an aesthetics of *de-auratization* — not the loss of the aura as an unfortunate accident, but as a deliberate critical operation.[^35]

With *The Third Memory* (1999–2000), Huyghe radicalizes the procedure: it is no longer a film he replays but a real event filtered through its own fictionalization. John Wojtowicz, the actual robber who inspired *Dog Day Afternoon* (Lumet, 1975), directs his own version of events on a reconstructed set. Huyghe "gives him the possibility of forging anew a conscious and subjective representation of his own person" (Bovier). Four temporal layers are superimposed: the real event (1972), the live media coverage, the Hollywood film (1975), the artistic reconstruction (2000) — memory, contaminated by its own representation, no longer knows where the original lies. *L'Ellipse* (1998) extends this logic by addressing the gaps of fiction itself: Huyghe invites Bruno Ganz, twenty years after *The American Friend* (Wenders, 1977), to traverse the locations "lost in the splice" — the spaces between shots become the subject of the work, as though reenactment no longer served to replay what took place but to *fill what was never filmed*. With *Blanche Neige, Lucie* (1997), the displacement is complete: Lucie Dolène, the French voice of Snow White, sings *Someday My Prince Will Come* on a film set while subtitles recount the lawsuit she brought against Disney for violating her rights. The reenactment becomes the restitution of a voice literally "stolen" by the culture industry — and the artist, by making her sing again, renders the expropriation visible.

Philippe Parreno, Huyghe's accomplice, shifts the device toward the spectral. *Marilyn* (2012) evokes Monroe in her suite at the Waldorf Astoria, reconstructed in a studio. Three algorithms "resurrect" the actress: one for her gaze (subjective camera), one for her voice, one for her handwriting. The reenactment becomes a technological séance — Allen's missing body is no longer replaced by another body but by computation. Omer Fast, with *Spielberg's List* (2003), pushes the mise en abyme a notch further: he interviews the Polish extras from *Schindler's List* (Spielberg, 1993) in Krakow, on two video channels that superimpose testimonies and film excerpts. It is a reenactment of a reenactment — and the question it poses is vertiginous: when a Polish extra replays a Jewish deportee for a Hollywood film about the Holocaust, who is reenacting whom?

These works together enact what Bovier identifies as a double mechanism: "the passage of a private person into a fiction or a media phenomenon on the one hand, and a gesture of inverting or reversing the effects of that publicity on the other." Cinematic reenactment is never a simple repetition: it *inverts* the power relations between original and copy, author and spectator, private memory and public narration.

But does staging that historicizes an image — that replays, reconstructs, translates a motif from the past into the present — necessarily confer a political charge on the reenactment? Jeff Wall (b. 1946, Vancouver) allows us to pose this question sharply. His "cinematographic photographs" — large backlit transparencies presenting entirely staged scenes — are often explicit formal reenactments: *The Destroyed Room* (1978) translates Delacroix's *Death of Sardanapalus*, *A Sudden Gust of Wind (after Hokusai)* (1993) transposes a print from *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* to suburban Vancouver. Art historian Michael Fried reads these tableaux as "hermetically sealed" systems that absorb the viewer in the manner of great eighteenth-century French painting — which is the exact opposite of art that interpellates politically.[^36] Wallian reenactment is *absorptive* rather than *transformative*: the spectator admires the construction but does not engage. This takes nothing from the artistic quality — but it underscores that staging history does not *suffice* to constitute a critical reenactment.

Cindy Sherman (b. 1954, Glen Ridge, New Jersey) poses the problem from the other side: not the staging of an identifiable original, but the reenactment of a *genre* — of a repertoire of codes without an original. In the *Untitled Film Stills* (1977–1980), Sherman photographs herself in poses evoking 1950s–60s Hollywood cinema — the femme fatale, the housewife, the ingénue, the fugitive — without ever citing a specific film. It is a reenactment without source: what is replayed is not a scene but a *structure of representation*, the stereotype itself. The gesture does not reproduce an image — it reveals the apparatus that produces images. Douglas Crimp, in "The Photographic Activity of Postmodernism" (1980), sees this as the fulfillment of a postmodern logic in which "the representation of representation" replaces the quest for the original.[^37]

With the *History Portraits* (1989–1990), Sherman moves from cinema to history painting — and the shift is decisive for our purposes. She reenacts Raphael, Caravaggio, Fouquet, Holbein, using grotesque prosthetics, false breasts, plastic noses. The resulting image is neither homage nor parody: it is a *de-idealization* that, by making the artifice of the classical body visible, reveals what history painting concealed — the fabrication of flesh, the convention of beauty, the violence of the pose. If Wall historicizes by *absorbing* (the viewer enters the image and stays), Sherman historicizes by *expelling*: the grotesque prevents absorption, forces critical distance, makes devout contemplation impossible. Shermanian reenactment is an *emetic* reenactment — it brings up what the original had swallowed.

The theoretical problem that Wall and Sherman together pose is this: does formal reenactment — translating Hokusai into photography, replaying Raphael in prosthetics — produce anything beyond a virtuosity of citation? The answer depends on the *apparatus*: Wall maintains the illusion (the viewer believes in the image), Sherman destroys it (the viewer sees the seams). It is the destruction that produces knowledge. The apparatus must do something more than cite: it must reveal, transform, or produce a residue that did not exist before the operation.

This category touches a deep theoretical nerve, for the link between repetition and trauma is constitutive of psychoanalysis. In *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), Freud observes that the traumatized subject compulsively *replays* the traumatic scene — not for pleasure, but because the event overwhelmed the subject's psychic capacities, who retrospectively attempts to master it through repetition. Freud names this compulsion *Wiederholungszwang*, "repetition compulsion": the trauma that could not be integrated into conscious narrative returns as act, symptom, involuntary staging. The subject does not *remember* the trauma — he *reenacts* it.[^38]

Contemporary clinical psychology has, in a sense, turned this compulsion into a therapeutic method. Treatments for post-traumatic stress disorder rest on a controlled form of reenactment: the patient is led to relive the traumatic scene in a safe setting, to replay it verbally or bodily, in order to progressively reduce its affective charge. The principle is paradoxical and profoundly Freudian: it is by *repeating* the trauma that one is freed from it — provided that the repetition takes place in a space where it can be elaborated, transformed into narrative, integrated into narrative memory rather than suffered as irruption.[^39]

The works in this category stand exactly at this frontier — between the compulsion to repeat and critical elaboration. The most brutal demonstration is perhaps *The Act of Killing* (2012) by Joshua Oppenheimer (produced by Werner Herzog and Errol Morris): in Indonesia, former executioners of the 1965–66 massacres replay their own crimes in cinematic genres of their choosing — western, musical, film noir. The film operates exactly at the Freudian tipping point: the executioners begin by replaying with jubilation, reenactment as triumphant repetition, with no apparent repression, since the regime that commissioned the massacres is still in power. But gradually the device turns against them. Anwar Congo, the principal executioner, ends up retching while replaying a strangling scene — his body produces the symptom that his consciousness refuses. The reenactment ceases to be controlled: it becomes the site of a return of the repressed that the executioner can no longer contain. Reenactment is not an exercise in voluntary memory; it is a device that brings up what conscious narrative has buried.

Walid Raad, with *The Atlas Group* (1989–2004), poses the problem from the other side — no longer the body that cracks, but the archive that is missing. His fictive archives of the Lebanese Civil War do not constitute a reenactment in the strict sense, but a fabrication of documents that radically questions what it means to "document" a conflict — and therefore what it would mean to "reenact" one. If collective trauma exceeds documentary capacity, if "real" archives are fragmentary, falsified, instrumentalized, then archival fiction paradoxically becomes more faithful to trauma than the document. Raad produces false archives that tell the truth — exactly as the symptom, in psychoanalysis, tells the subject's truth through displacement and deformation. War traumas, in particular, are ceaselessly replayed by contemporary artists — as though colonial and military violence demanded to be put back into action in order to become thinkable. Singaporean artist Ho Tzu Nyen, in *Phantoms of Endless Day* (2025, LUMA Arles commission), uses artificial intelligence processes to endlessly recompose the images of an unfinished film about the last days of the Second World War in Southeast Asia — Japanese soldiers, communist guerrillas, British agents, and mythical figures caught in an algorithmic jungle where timelines ceaselessly bifurcate. The reenactment no longer passes through the body but through computation, and colonial trauma replays itself at each generative loop in a different form.

Another line of contemporary reenactment seeks neither to transmit (Abramović), nor to confront (Deller), nor to restage (Huyghe): it seeks to *repatriate* — to bring a work, a gesture, a memory back to the ground from which it was torn.

Cuban artist Tania Bruguera, with *Homenaje a Ana Mendieta* (1985–1996), re-performed for ten years, in Havana, the works of Cuban-American artist Ana Mendieta, who died in 1985 at thirty-six under circumstances never fully elucidated. The gesture is less a citation than an act of counter-exile: bringing back into Cuban soil a body of work that American art history had "claimed." Bruguera inaugurates a practice of re-performance as feminist and postcolonial gesture — the imagination at work is a *reparative* imagination, which uses repetition not to reproduce but to restore. Palestinian artist Taysir Batniji, with *To My Brother* (2012, exhibited at Kunstverein München in 2024 and at Bridget Donahue, New York), performs a repatriation of another kind: he takes the Super 8 footage of his brother's wedding — his brother, killed by the Israeli army in 1987 — and transforms it into an installation — the wedding film slowed, stretched, suspended, becomes a film of mourning. It is not a reenactment strictly speaking but a *displacement*: the joyful image, torn from its familial context and transported into the space of art, turns into an archive of the disappeared. Allen's "missing body" is no longer a theoretical category here: it is a brother.

Spanish artist Dora García, with *Second Time Around* (2018), shifts this logic of filiation toward the field of knowledge: she reenacts a seminar given by Jacques Lacan in 1972 at the Catholic University of Louvain — a seminar famous for the heckling that accompanied it. The reenactment functions here as a tool of transmission *and* disruption: to replay a lecture is both to transmit content and to reproduce the conditions of its interruption. The imagination is *pedagogical and subversive* at once — a tension that, as we will see, lies at the heart of the BMC question.

All the works examined so far look backward — they replay, repatriate, restore a past. But some artists reverse the device: they put into action not a documented past but an imagined future, *through the forms of the past*. This is what Yuk Hui, with the concept of "tertiary protention," allows us to think: technology no longer as a recording machine (Benjamin) but as an anticipation machine.

Israeli artist Yael Bartana, with the trilogy *…And Europe Will Be Stunned* (2007–2011), stages the "Jewish Renaissance Movement in Poland" — a fictive movement calling three million Jews to return to Poland. The three films scrupulously borrow the aesthetics of 1930s–40s propaganda films, inverting nationalist and Zionist rhetorics. Bartana herself names this gesture a "historical pre-enactment": the staging of a future event (the "return") through the forms of the past (propaganda). The imagination here is *counterfactual* — it does not reproduce what has taken place but fabricates what *could* take place, using the visual forms of what has *already* taken place as raw material.

German-Japanese artist Hito Steyerl, with *November* (2004), works an adjacent territory: she retraces the trajectory of her childhood friend Andrea Wolf, who became a PKK fighter and was killed in 1998. The film mixes archival images, reenactments, and reflections on how documentary images transform into propaganda. Steyerl does not pre-enact a future but *de-enacts* an official narrative — she undoes, image by image, the narrative fabrication that transformed a singular life into an icon of armed struggle. Reenactment as counter-investigation.

Swiss director Milo Rau pushes the logic of pre-enactment into the juridico-political field. His *Congo Tribunal* (2015–2017) — an artist's tribunal with no official status, devoted to violence in eastern Congo — does not replay a trial that took place: it *performs* a trial that never happened and may never happen. Rau conceives this gesture as the pre-enactment of a "justice to come": an illocutionary speech act (in Austin's sense) that, without possessing the performative power of a verdict (perlocutionary), produces effects through publicity and affect.[^19] Lütticken notes that Rau "has used dramatic time not to represent, but to intervene in and (potentially) transform historical time." The imagination here is *paralegal* — it fabricates the formal conditions of a justice whose political conditions do not yet exist. Pre-enactment is then distinguished from reenactment not by its object (past vs. future) but by its regime of belief: where reenactment knows the event took place and attempts to recover it, pre-enactment knows the event has not taken place and attempts to make it possible. As Lütticken writes: "it opens onto an unwritten future." What distinguishes pre-enactment from futurism or speculation is that it does not *project* a future from the present — it *constructs* one with materials from the past. Bartana borrows the aesthetics of 1930s propaganda, Rau borrows the form of the tribunal: the future is not invented *ex nihilo*, it is assembled from inherited, diverted, reversed forms. Pre-enactment is a reenactment whose object does not yet exist.

The collective Forensic Architecture, founded by the architect Eyal Weizman in 2010, displaces reenactment toward a territory unprecedented in the field of art. Drawing first on citizen field information — mobile phone videos, open satellite imagery, geolocated testimonies — then cross-referencing them through 3D modeling, video analysis, spatial reconstruction, and artificial intelligence, Forensic Architecture reconstructs events — bombings, police killings, environmental destruction — from digital data. Their reconstructions are presented both in courts (as evidence) and in art spaces (Documenta 14, Venice Biennale, Turner Prize 2018). Reenactment here passes from body to data, from gesture to computation: one no longer *replays* with living bodies, one *reconstructs* with algorithms. The imagination is *forensic* — it reconstructs not what the past *meant* but what *materially happened*, with a precision that human testimony cannot achieve. It is the opposite pole from Abramović's re-performance: where she passes memory through the body, Forensic Architecture passes it through the machine. But in both cases, the stake is the same: to produce *proof* — proof that something took place, and that this thing demands justice.

### Machinic imagination

The most recent practices displace reenactment toward a territory that neither Benjamin nor Schneider could anticipate: that of artificial intelligence as a tool of "resurrection," counter-narrative, and mythological fabrication. This displacement corresponds exactly to Hui's thesis: technology no longer merely conserves the past (tertiary retention), it *generates* futures (tertiary protention). Reenactment enters the era of machinic imagination.

But the political stakes of this displacement must be measured. Jennifer Allen showed that reenactment rests on a linear temporality that spatializes the past and treats it as a territory to visit — and that this spatialization is violent: non-Western societies appear as "belated reenactments" of Western history, summoned to replay its stages in order to "catch up" with the present. The artists who work with AI reenactment occupy precisely this fault line: they turn the most "Western" technological tool — the language model, the dataset, the neural network — against the temporality that produced it. If classical reenactment replays the past *of* the canon, these artists replay *against* the canon — they fabricate counter-memories, counter-genealogies, counter-mythologies.

Sondra Perry (b. 1986), with *phantom. menace.* (2023), assigns to AI the role that the body played for Abramović: a medium for summoning the past. Perry asks a generative AI model to produce the scene of two African American men shaking hands in a barbershop, then replays this scene across multiple contexts and versions. The faces blur at the moment of contact — the AI fails to stabilize the identity of the Black bodies it attempts to generate. This failure is not a technical accident: it is the *subject* of the work. Perry exhibits the racial biases inscribed in the training datasets of generative models — the fact that these machines, fed predominantly on images of white bodies, *do not know how* to reproduce certain bodies. Reenactment passes here through the machine and fails *through* the machine, and it is this failure that constitutes the revelation. The imagination is *diagnostic*: it uses the attempt at reproduction as a test, and the test result is that certain bodies resist technical reproducibility — not because they are "irreducible" in the romantic sense, but because the machines that reproduce them are themselves products of a racial history. Exhibited at Kunstverein München (2024) and at Bridget Donahue, New York.

Josèfa Ntjam (b. 1992, Saint-Étienne), with *swell of spæc(i)es* (2024), a LAS Art Foundation commission for a collateral event of the Venice Biennale, constructs speculative mythologies blending African cosmologies, science fiction, and natural science, via AI, 3D animation, photomontage, sound, and performance. A member of the collective Black(s) to the Future, Ntjam practices what might be called an *Afrofuturist pre-enactment*: not replaying the past but fabricating the myths of a possible future, drawing on Afro-diasporic oral narratives and traditions of resistance. Her work extends Bartana's gesture (pre-enactment) but in a cosmogonic rather than national register: the point is not to replay History but to create alternative *molecular genealogies* — origin stories that come not from the documented past but from an imagined future. The imagination here is *cosmogonic*: it fabricates worlds rather than reproducing events.

Sahej Rahal (b. 1988, Mumbai), with *Ancestors* (2022), *Distributed Mind Test* (2023), and *Anhad* (2023), inverts the logic of reenactment from another cultural horizon. *Ancestors*, presented at the CSMVS in Mumbai, was a "future archaeological site" — a post-human tomb populated with artifacts indeterminate between relic and fiction. *Distributed Mind Test* is a multiplayer video game in which participants embody fragments of a non-human consciousness. *Anhad* is an AI simulation whose entities carry Hindustani music notes, generating an infinite song. Rahal does not reconstruct a documented past: he fabricates the archives of a speculative future, blurring the boundary between what archaeologists call *terminus post quem* and *terminus ante quem* — one no longer knows whether the found object belongs to the past or the present. It is here that Hui's "tertiary protention" takes its most radical form: technology no longer replays a human past but *generates* non-human futures, integrating chaos and randomness as possible sites of repetition. We are far from the *replay* in the console sense — rewinding a sequence, replaying it identically. What is at play in Rahal's work belongs to the *open world*: an open space where digital entities, spectators, and algorithmic forces endlessly replay the tensions between the human and the non-human, without script, without score, without predetermined outcome. The imagination is *archaeo-speculative*: it turns the tools of memory (the archive, the dig, the dating) toward the future — and reenactment, having passed through the machine, ceases to be a matter of fidelity to the past to become a matter of possibility.

### From reproduction to production: taking stock of the repertoire

This necessarily partial repertoire traces an arc from bodily re-performance (Abramović) to machinic imagination (Perry, Ntjam, Rahal), passing through the reenactment of historical events (Deller, Żmijewski), the cinematic remake as critical operation (Huyghe, Parreno, Fast), confrontation with trauma (Oppenheimer, Raad), heritage and repatriation (Bruguera, García), counterfactual pre-enactment (Bartana, Steyerl), and forensic reconstruction (Forensic Architecture). Two cases — Wall and his *absorptive* reenactment, Abramović and her *institutionalizing* reenactment — serve as counter-examples: they show that neither formal virtuosity nor fidelity to the gesture suffices to constitute a critical reenactment.

Several constants emerge. The most productive reenactments are those that seek not to *reproduce* but to *produce*: to produce new knowledge (Deller reveals what official archives conceal), a new experience (Oppenheimer brings up what conscious narrative has buried), a new object (Huyghe fabricates a "third object" that is neither the original nor the copy), new evidence (Forensic Architecture reconstructs what power has tried to erase). In other words: the most interesting reenactments are those in which *reproduction is an imagination* — where the act of replaying the past is, in the same gesture, an act of invention.

This thesis — which we posed in the introduction through Simondon, Stiegler, and Hui — is empirically confirmed in the field of contemporary art. It will now be tested against the BMC case: a place that was itself a laboratory of invention, and whose reenactments oscillate, as we will see, between museum reproduction and pedagogical imagination.

## III. BMC as Object of Reenactment

The choice of Black Mountain College as the terrain of inquiry is inseparable from the research-creation project *Retcon Black Mountain*, within which this essay is situated. Of all the pedagogical experiments of the twentieth century — the Bauhaus (1919-1933), the Freie Universitat Berlin (1948-), CalArts (1961-), the experimental universities of the 1960s (Antioch, Goddard, Evergreen) — BMC is perhaps the one whose attempts at "redoing" pose the sharpest problems. A museum has been dedicated to it since 1993 (BMCM+AC, Asheville). An annual festival (*{Re}HAPPENING*) has been held since 2010 on the Lake Eden campus site. Academic conferences (*ReVIEWING Black Mountain College*) have followed one another since 2009. Two major retrospectives — *Starting at Zero* (Arnolfini Bristol / Kettle's Yard Cambridge, 2005) and *Leap Before You Look* (ICA Boston / Hammer Museum Los Angeles / Wexner Center Columbus, 2015-2017, cur. Helen Molesworth) — circulated through the anglophone world. The exhibition *Black Mountain: An Interdisciplinary Experiment 1933-1957* (Hamburger Bahnhof, Berlin, 2015) traveled in the opposite direction, from the Germany that had seen the birth of the Bauhaus of which BMC is partly the heir. The gallerist David Zwirner devoted a group exhibition to BMC artists in London in 2023. And since the late 2000s, a constellation of projects in France, Germany, Canada, and the United States have claimed BMC as a basis for founding pedagogical programs, residencies, and workshops — not to mention major exhibitions devoted to its most illustrious members, rediscovered generation after generation: Josef and Anni Albers, John Cage, Merce Cunningham, Ruth Asawa, Robert Rauschenberg, Buckminster Fuller, each the subject of retrospectives that, each time, rekindle interest in the place that formed or welcomed them.

This proliferation is all the more remarkable given that BMC lasted twenty-four years (1933-1957), never enrolled more than a hundred and fifty students at one time, awarded very few degrees, and closed in debt and dispersal. The college left no heritage buildings (the Lake Eden campus became a Christian summer camp, Camp Rockmont; the original Blue Ridge Assembly campus is a Methodist retreat center). Its archives are dispersed among the BMCM+AC, the North Carolina State Archives, the special collections of many universities, and the personal papers of former students and faculty. It produced no codifiable "method," no transferable curriculum, no recognized degree.

It is precisely this absence of a formalized legacy that fuels the reenactment drive. BMC left works (Rauschenberg's *White Paintings*, Asawa's wire sculptures, Olson's *Projective Verse*, Cage's music), narratives (the memoirs of Duberman, Dawson, Rumaker, Lane), photographs — but it did not leave a *model*. Or rather: it left a model that, by design, resists its own reproduction. BMC was experimental in the strong sense — not a place where a known method was applied, but a place where methods were invented as one went along, where failure was part of the process, where each semester's program was built around the faculty present. The founder, John Andrew Rice, had no pedagogical plan: he had a conviction — that art should be at the center of liberal education — and an organizational model — democratic self-governance by the faculty. It would, moreover, be incomplete to reduce BMC to its artistic dimension alone, however determinant. As David Silver has shown in *The Farm at Black Mountain College* (Atelier Editions / BMCM+AC, 2024), the campus farm was, during the best years, a genuine laboratory of agrarian self-governance — "a testing ground for self-sufficiency, community living and collaboration" — whose systemic success is as instructive as the artworks that emerged from it. Likewise, the college's democratic governance, though perpetually conflictual, constitutes in itself a political experiment irreducible to its artistic productions.

The paradox is structural, and it is the one we posed with Goodman: BMC is an *autographic* act — a singular event, irreducible to a score. Redoing BMC is not like reviving *Hamlet* (allographic art: the text preexists and invites interpretation). It is like redoing Cage's *Theater Piece No. 1* — the event that had no script, no score, no prior coordination, and whose unique occurrence is its only form of existence. Every reenactment of BMC therefore confronts an aporia: if it is faithful to the spirit of the place (experimentation, invention, risk), it cannot *reproduce* — it must *invent* something else, in which case it is no longer a reenactment. If it reproduces the forms (communal living, interdisciplinarity, arts at the center), it betrays the spirit — because BMC's *form* was not a fixed model but the provisional result of an ongoing process.

It is this aporia that makes attempts to "redo BMC" so instructive for a theory of reenactment. They function as tests: each, in its own way, negotiates the relationship between fidelity and invention, between the memory of a place and the production of a present. We propose to examine the principal ones according to three logics: celebration and activation (the memorial festival, the archival-performative project), conservation and exhibition (museum retrospectives, archives), and continuation (pedagogical filiations).

### The memorial festival

**The {Re}HAPPENING (2010-).** The *{Re}HAPPENING*, organized annually since 2010 by the BMCM+AC, is the most visible and regular BMC reenactment project. For one day, around a hundred contemporary artists occupy the historic Lake Eden site — BMC's campus from 1941 on — for performances, installations, concerts, screenings, and workshops. The event is explicitly inspired by Cage's *Theater Piece No. 1* (1952), considered the first *happening* in art history. But the *Theater Piece* is not replayed: one returns to the place, reactivates a spirit, repeats a festive gesture.

The distinction is crucial. The *{Re}HAPPENING* is not a reenactment: it is a *memorial festival*. It is distinguished from reenactment by several traits: it is cyclical (annual, like a ritual), collective (communal rather than authorial), festive (pleasure takes precedence over fidelity), and site-bound (one returns *to the site*). It does not claim to reproduce a past event: it celebrates a heritage by producing the new. It is the olive tree of the Acropolis transposed into the field of contemporary art — with the same ambiguities. The typographic braces around the "Re" — *{Re}HAPPENING* — isolate the prefix as though to interrogate its meaning: what does it mean to *re*-do a happening? The works presented each year oscillate between *tribute* and experimentation proper: in 2024, *names, erased* by John Popham traces on two cellos the gestures of past musicians in explicit homage to Rauschenberg's *Erased de Kooning Drawing*, while *Destruction is desirable* by Kiran Jandu and Michael Medeiros is a participatory happening with clay, movement, and improvised sound. Without wishing to erase the singularity of each proposition presented over more than a decade, a certain consistency can be observed in the programming's broad range: the *{Re}HAPPENING* avoids strict reenactment — no historic BMC work is reconstructed identically — in favor of an atmosphere of creative community where interdisciplinarity, site-specificity, and participation function as markers of filiation. The transmitted spirit belongs more to a neo-communitarian sensibility — where somatic practices, ecology, arts and crafts, and improvisation converge — than to a critical examination of BMC's pedagogical legacy.

But the memorial festival raises questions that the festival itself does not answer. What does it produce beyond celebration? The *{Re}HAPPENING* possesses an undeniable force: it is the only project that takes place *on the historic campus*, and anyone who performs or attends at Lake Eden experiences a place whose charge cannot be reduced to its documentation. The encounter with the territory — the lake, the Studies Building, the Dining Hall — constitutes in itself a form of transmission. On the other hand, the event does not necessarily produce new knowledge about BMC: the festival does not interrogate the archive, does not generate an autonomous corpus, does not subject the legacy to critical examination. The risk, inherent to every cyclical commemoration, is that of emptied-out repetition — the festive reconstruction of a glorious past without interrogation of the conditions that made it possible — what one might call, with some bluntness, the Puy du Fou trap of the avant-garde.

In Eliadean terms, the *{Re}HAPPENING* is a secularized form of ritual reactualization: the community gathers annually at the sacred site to reactivate the mythic time of origins. But unlike archaic ritual, there is no substantialization: the *{Re}HAPPENING* does not claim to *be* *Theater Piece No. 1* — it knows it is an echo. This awareness of the gap saves it from first-degree literalism, but does not protect it from routinization.

**Arnold Dreyblatt (2017-2022).** Dreyblatt's case is perhaps the richest theoretically. An American artist and composer based in Germany, Dreyblatt assembled from 2015 onward an archive on BMC — documents, photographs, texts, scores, recordings — then devised a precise apparatus for passing it into the present. The project, titled *PERFORMING the Black Mountain ARCHIVE*, was first deployed at the Hamburger Bahnhof in Berlin (2015), in parallel with the exhibition *Black Mountain: An Interdisciplinary Experiment*, before continuing at other institutions and culminating in a 560-page volume, *Black Mountain College as Multiverse* (Verlag Kettler, 2022).

The method is rigorous. Dreyblatt elaborated a *score* in the musical sense: fixed time slots (each morning and afternoon during the exhibition's four months), predetermined locations within the museum space, archival files to extract and render. Ten European art schools — from Kiel to Dresden, from Bonn to Berlin — sent students from varied disciplines (sculpture, painting, media, sound, dance, theater, typography, literature) for one- to two-week residencies in a studio/archive space installed at the heart of the exhibition. When not performing, the students conducted their own research in the archive; visitors encountered the performances by chance during their visit, unannounced. Dreyblatt stated: "I felt instinctively that the involvement of students in the arts would be essential in counteracting a nostalgia for an exemplary pedagogical and social experiment"[^40]: the program sought not to reproduce BMC but to test what its materials produce when activated by contemporary bodies and knowledge.

The title is programmatic. *Multiverse* — not *reproduction*, *reconstruction*, or *homage* — posits that there is no single, original BMC to reconstruct, only multiple parallel actualizations, each as legitimate as the others. Dreyblatt refuses first-degree literalism: he does not claim to redo BMC, he explores what BMC *becomes* when activated by contemporary bodies and institutions. The archive is not a text to be respected but a score to be interpreted — in the musical sense that Dreyblatt, as a composer, commands.

Here is where Goodman's distinction proves operative. Dreyblatt treats BMC's archives as *allographic* material — a score inviting multiple interpretations — whereas BMC itself was an *autographic* event — a singular, irreproducible occurrence. This transformation — from autographic lived experience into allographic material — is the decisive operation of every successful reenactment.

Dreyblatt also assumes the geographical distance — Europe looking at America — and makes this distance the very subject of the work. The *Multiverse* is not an attempt to bring BMC back to Europe, but to explore what BMC becomes when it crosses the Atlantic in the other direction. There is here a return of the Bauhaus in a form that neither Gropius nor Albers would have recognized: not the transfer of a method but the circulation of an *imagination* — that of pedagogical experimentation as an art form.

### The aporia of exhibition

The major BMC retrospectives — *Starting at Zero* (Arnolfini / Kettle's Yard, 2005), *Leap Before You Look* (ICA Boston, 2015-2017, cur. Helen Molesworth and Ruth Erickson), *Black Mountain: An Interdisciplinary Experiment* (Hamburger Bahnhof, Berlin, 2015) — pose a specific problem: how to exhibit a place that was not a site of object production but of experience? BMC certainly produced artworks — and major ones — but its originality did not reside in the works: it resided in the *conditions* of their production. The communal life, the farm, the shared meals, the ungraded courses, the alternation between intellectual and manual labor, the direct democracy of the faculty — none of this can be displayed in a vitrine. The BMCM+AC in Asheville, ostensibly the place closest to BMC's heritage, has a modest exhibition space (about 2,500 sq. ft.) and programs temporary exhibitions that, despite their quality — *The Farm at Black Mountain College* (2024), *Vera B. Williams / Stories* (2024), the chapbooks on Cage and the *Quiet House* — derive their principal merit from highlighting individual trajectories rather than the collective experience that constituted the institution's singularity. Every attempt at exhibition seems to stumble on the same aporia: how to show a *process* through *objects*? The Asheville Art Museum, which holds more than 1,500 pieces linked to BMC, displays only a handful in a section of its permanent collection. As for the town of Black Mountain itself, it seems not to know how to promote — or to be embarrassed to promote — its own heritage: a commemorative plaque at the town's edge, mentioning only Buckminster Fuller, between a diner and a gas station.

International retrospectives have tried other strategies. *Leap Before You Look* sought to solve this problem by integrating a piano and a dance space into the exhibition circuit — activatable devices that invited performance rather than contemplation. The gesture was Huyghean in a sense: it created conditions for activation rather than representation. But a piano in a museum remains a piano in a museum: the conditions of its activation are radically different from those of a piano in a BMC dining hall where students and faculty eat, work, and live together. The museum retrospective is, structurally, a *museum* reenactment — that is, a reenactment that, through the very apparatus that renders it visible (the museum, the catalogue, the wall labels), betrays what it seeks to show. It is Benjamin's paradox pushed to its limit: "exhibition value" definitively triumphs over "cult value," and the very fact of showing BMC in a museum confirms that what was most precious about BMC — lived experience — is irretrievably lost.

Of all these enterprises, *Leap Before You Look* (ICA Boston, 2015-2017) is the most ambitious and the most lucid about its own contradictions. Helen Molesworth, in her introductory essay "Imaginary Landscape," organizes the exhibition around the concept of the *haptic* — what she calls the "extra-optical": BMC's art engages the body beyond the gaze, intertwining the visual and the tactile to the point of making them inseparable. The curatorial gesture is political: by refusing the hierarchy of mediums (painting, weaving, pottery, poetry treated on equal footing), Molesworth structurally reconstructs the disciplinary non-hierarchy that was the foundation of BMC's pedagogical project. Karen Karnes's ceramics neighbor Kline's paintings, Anni Albers's weavings neighbor Callahan's photographs — and it is this very proximity that constitutes the argument. But Frieze's review strikes a just note when it observes that the exhibition is "remarkably light on historical information" in the galleries themselves — the conflicts, the debts, the "internal power struggles" are "vaguely mentioned" at the end of the circuit, with detail reserved for the catalogue. BMC's death is dispatched as quickly as it was experienced. The exhibition selects, like all the reenactments examined in this section: it shows the haptic community, not the community in crisis. And Virginia Reynolds Badgett's review in *Panorama* identifies an additional paradox: *Leap Before You Look* actively works to "dethrone the canonical figures" — the Rauschenbergs, the Cages — in order to bring to light under-recognized artists (Asawa, Jess, Karnes), but in doing so risks undermining the very foundation of BMC's celebrity. Dismantling the canon can weaken the reason the institution merits a retrospective. This is a characteristic double bind: the exhibition can only exist thanks to the "big names," but it wants to show that BMC cannot be reduced to them.

### Archives as site of reconfiguration

There exists a form of encounter with BMC that retrospectives and festivals cannot produce, one that belongs to an entirely different epistemic register: the consultation of primary archives. The Black Mountain College holdings at the Western Regional Archives of the State of North Carolina (Asheville) — where they were transferred from Raleigh in 2012 — constitute the most important documentary corpus on the college: Board of Fellows and faculty meeting minutes, general correspondence, financial records, student files (subject to FERPA), college bulletins and publications, hundreds of photographs. These are supplemented by the BMC Research Project papers (1970-1972, dir. Mary Emma Harris), with filmed interviews, transcriptions, slides, and file cards on objects produced at the college. The Theodore and Barbara Loines Dreier Collection, donated by the treasurer's children, contains Albers's speeches, Dewey's correspondence (54 letters), and notes on institutional reorganization.

Consulting these archives — physically, in the Asheville reading room, folder by folder — is not a reenactment of BMC in the sense we have defined. No one "replays" anything, no assembled bodies are summoned, no substantialization is at stake. Yet the experience reconfigures understanding of the object in a way that no retrospective, festival, or catalogue can produce. The Board of Fellows minutes do not describe the "utopian community" of the mythographies — they describe close votes, alliances forming and dissolving, personality conflicts escalating to lawsuits (Rice against the "conservatives" in 1940, the 1949 split that drove out half the faculty), unresolved heating problems, unpaid bills at the Bank of Black Mountain. Dewey's letters do not formulate an abstract philosophy of education: they negotiate, encourage, moderate — Dewey appears as a pragmatic and cautious adviser, not as the philosopher-totem invoked by filiation projects. The financial records reveal that the college operated, for nearly its entire existence, on the brink of insolvency.

What the archive produces is not transparency to the past — it is the past's resistance. The researcher who opens Box 506.1.12 ("Correspondence and Papers of Board of Fellows; Reports and Memoranda of Faculty Committees; and Material on Finances and Reorganization, 1934-1956") does not recover BMC: she discovers the gap between the BMC of the mythographies and the BMC of the papers — and this gap is the very site of knowledge. The archive does not confirm the inherited narrative: it complicates, contradicts, and densifies it. It is a process of reconfiguration rather than revelation: the documents do not "speak" for themselves — they respond to the researcher's questions, and the questions change with the era, the concerns, the theoretical framework. Mary Emma Harris, in 1987, was looking for the arts. Diaz, in 2015, was looking for the devices of chance and design. Jonathan B. Fisher looks for Deweyan pragmatism in the 54 letters of the Dewey-Dreier correspondence. Molesworth looks for the haptic. Each return to the same boxes produces a different BMC — not through infidelity but through a displacement of the gaze. This is perhaps the most silent but most effective form of what we have called "reproduction is an imagination": the researcher in the archives reproduces nothing, but her work of interpretation is an act of historical imagination in the exact sense that Collingwood gave to reenactment — "re-thinking past thought in one's own mind," not to confirm it but to reconfigure it from the present.
### Filiation as repetition

In France, BMC long remained poorly known — accessible mainly through art historians working on the Bauhaus, and for anglophones through the foundational works of Martin Duberman (*Black Mountain: An Exploration in Community*, 1972) and Mary Emma Harris (*The Arts at Black Mountain College*, 1987), still untranslated into French. An academic turning point came in the anglophone world in the early 2000s: the collective volume *Black Mountain College: Experiment in Art* (ed. Vincent Katz, MIT Press, 2002), the catalogue *Starting at Zero* (Arnolfini / Kettle's Yard, 2005, with essays by Harris, Eva Diaz, and Edmund de Waal), then Eva Diaz's monograph *The Experimenters: Chance and Design at Black Mountain College* (University of Chicago Press, 2015), shifted the study of BMC from testimony and chronicle to the analysis of pedagogical devices and their political implications. The symposium "Black Mountain — Educational Turn and the Avantgarde" (Hamburger Bahnhof, September 2015) consecrated this inflection by bringing together for the first time art historians, philosophers of education, and practitioners around the question of the contemporary relevance of the BMC model.

France was roughly a decade behind in French-language scholarship. It was not until 2014 that the first two French-language books on the subject appeared: Alan Speller, *Le Black Mountain College, Enseignement artistique et avant-garde* (La Lettre Volee) and the collective volume edited by Jean-Pierre Cometti and Eric Giraud, *Black Mountain College, Art, Democratie, Utopie* (PUR / Centre International de Poesie Marseille). In the latter, Arnaud Labelle-Rojoux (Villa Arson) describes BMC as "a multi-faceted myth" and underscores that "the idea of rethinking today how art schools function and the stakes of their existence in the current normative context has never been more urgent." The coincidence is no accident: BMC enters the francophone debate at the precise moment when French art schools are undergoing the most acute crisis in their recent history — the Bologna Process reforms (applied to art schools from 2006) and the prescriptive evaluations of AERES (2009), which became HCERES in 2013, conditioning the recognition of the DNSEP at Master's level. BMC does not arrive in the French landscape as an art-historical object but as an argument in the struggle against the normalization of art schools by university criteria.

It is in this context that several projects of explicit filiation emerge — not reducible to rebuilding a geodesic dome in the courtyard of an art school (though that happens too), but attempting, with varying degrees of success, to take up something of BMC's conditions: duration, communal living, relationship to landscape. The White Mountain College (Beaux-Arts de Marseille, 2020), labeled by the biennial Manifesta 13, inverts the color — "in echo of the black hills of Black Mountain, the school of Luminy with its white hills" — and places itself in the lineage of BMC's *Summer Institutes*: a month of collective residency in the Luminy studios, facing Mont Puget, without a brief or "rhetoric of the project." The Ecole du Domaine du Possible (Arles, 2015-), founded by Francoise Nyssen and Patrick Bouchain on a 120-hectare agricultural estate, reprises the communitarian structure and Deweyan pragmatism of BMC without always explicitly claiming it. The webdocumentary *Re-faire ecole* (Ecole Fructidor, Chalon-sur-Saone) devotes an entire section to BMC under the title "Where is the Black Mountain College?" And in Quebec, the Ecole de la Montagne Rouge (Montreal, 2012), born during the "Maple Spring," inverts the color with a political charge that the Marseille White Mountain College does not carry.

What strikes one in these projects is the recurring motif of *translation* — geographical (North Carolina to Luminy, Arles, Chalon-sur-Saone, Montreal), chromatic (Black to White, Black to Red), and temporal (1933-1957 to 2010-2020). BMC is *displaced* rather than *replayed*. These are not reenactments in the strict sense: none of these projects claims to reproduce an event or situation from BMC. They are claimed filiations, borrowed methods, homages by name. They belong less to reconstruction than to what Kierkegaard called *repetition* (*Gjentagelsen*): not reproducing the past identically, but attempting to make it return *otherwise* in the present. Yet this very elasticity — the fact that "Black Mountain College" can simultaneously signify interdisciplinarity, community, experimentation, self-governance, relationship to landscape — authorizes a semantic appropriation whose contours vary with each project's needs. BMC functions here as a *brand*: a signifier rich enough and vague enough to legitimize very different undertakings. From the inside, it is difficult to situate these projects as genuine continuations of Black Mountain College — the material, geographical, political, and institutional conditions are so different that the filiation amounts more to invocation than to continuation.

Where homage exhibits distance, reenactment claims to abolish it — and it is in the gap between these two gestures that the question running through this section is at stake: how to continue BMC without repeating it?

Dreyblatt's answer — the *Multiverse* — is the most convincing: not to reproduce BMC but to treat it as an open score, material for activation rather than a model to imitate. This is also, as we will see, the answer that the Eden Summer Institute attempted — by installing itself not *on* the BMC site but a few kilometers away, in the same region, under conditions of fragility, uncertainty, and material precariousness that resembled less a homage than a *proof* — in the quasi-printmaking sense: the passage through the press that reveals, in negative, what the matrix has retained.

### What a reenactment can do

The accumulation of cases — artistic, memorial, pedagogical, institutional — calls less for an evaluative grid than for an examination of the operations at stake. Hal Foster, in *The Return of the Real* (1996), proposed a framework for thinking these returns: the neo-avant-gardes of the 1950s-60s are not simple repetitions of the historical avant-gardes of the 1910s-20s — they are their *afterwardness* (*Nachtraglichkeit*), in the Freudian sense. The first event (Dada, Constructivism) takes on its meaning only retroactively, through the repetition that reconfigures it. It is not the Duchamp of 1917 who explains the Rauschenberg of 1953 — it is Rauschenberg who, by "returning" to Duchamp, makes visible in the original possibilities that no one had seen. The return is not an impoverishment but an unfolding. Foster thus opposes Peter Burger (*Theory of the Avant-Garde*, 1974), for whom the neo-avant-garde is necessarily a farce — the emptied-out repetition of a gesture that was only valid in its first occurrence. Foster's framework is directly applicable to BMC: the attempts to "redo" the college are not degraded copies of a lost original — they are the aftereffects that reveal, or betray, its latent potentialities. The question is then: what does each return *make visible* in the original?

Homages to BMC are moreover innumerable and largely undocumented: how many performances have claimed Cage's *4'33"*? How many workshops have invoked Albers's *color studies*? How many rural residencies have presented themselves as "new Black Mountains"? The point is not to catalog them but to understand what a reenactment *does* — what it produces that did not exist before it. And above all — since our thesis is that reproduction is always an imagination — what *kind* of imagination does it deploy? The confirmative imagination of ritual (Eliade), which reactualizes the same to refound order? Or the displacing imagination of repetition (Kierkegaard, Deleuze), which produces difference in the return? The spectrum of reenactments is distributed between these two poles — from the ritual commemoration that confirms, to the artistic intervention that displaces. Five operations, intertwined rather than hierarchized, allow us to think what a reenactment can do — and what it cannot.

To produce knowledge that the document alone does not contain. This is what Collingwood aimed at: not the simple repetition of a past event, but understanding *from the inside* of a historical thought, through the gesture of re-thinking it. The distinction is clear between Deller, whose *Battle of Orgreave* makes one experience the spatial and affective apparatus of police violence — embodied knowledge that the 1984 archives do not deliver — and the commemorative reconstruction that stages what is already known, in expected forms. Dreyblatt's project belongs to the same register: the confrontation of present-day students with archival materials generates performative knowledge that the archive alone does not contain — the archive is a score, the reenactment is its interpretation. But this distinction is more unstable than it appears: the line between "new knowledge" and "convincing illustration" is often a matter of reception context. The {Re}HAPPENING, for instance, does not claim to produce knowledge about BMC — but does the experience of the place (the lake, the Studies Building, the Appalachian light) constitute knowledge of another order, bodily and spatial, irreducible to documentation? The question remains open — and that is perhaps its virtue: a reenactment that leaves no doubt about what it has produced is probably one that has merely confirmed.

To transform those who engage in it. The stake is pedagogical in the strong sense — Dewey's: learning through experience. BMC was not a place of transmission but of transformation: communal life — shared meals, farm work, no separation between class time and free time — *was* the pedagogical apparatus. To replay BMC without this transformative dimension is to replay the surface while forgetting the mechanism. Transformation can also be that of the witness become actor: Bruguera's gesture with Mendieta is transformative because it implicates a body that puts itself at risk in the name of a claimed filiation. Abramovic's with *Seven Easy Pieces* is more ambiguous: the body suffers, certainly, but at the Guggenheim, in a framework that domesticates risk into a spectacle of endurance. The question is not whether participants "enjoyed" the reenactment, but whether they come out displaced — whether something in their categories or habits has shifted.

To leave a corpus that is not reducible to its documentation. Lutticken, in his 2022 essay, identifies the risk with precision: under post-Fordist conditions of the attention economy, history is increasingly transformed into a "deposit of potentially valuable materials to be mined by artists and other post-Fordist workers" — a "productivist mobilization of the archives" where the past becomes raw material for content production.[^19] This is the trap of Bourriaud's *Postproduction* reduced to its poorest version: no longer the invention of new forms from existing materials, but the *mining* of the archive as a resource for the creative economy. Diana Taylor (*The Archive and the Repertoire*, 2003) opposes the archive (documents, texts, objects) to the repertoire (embodied practices, gestures transmitted from body to body). A productive reenactment is not one that goes to the archive to *display* it — it is one that, through the activation of the repertoire, *enriches* the archive. Dreyblatt's *Multiverse* (560 pages) is itself a new archive; Raad's *Atlas Group* pushes the logic to its extreme by generating false archives that tell more truth than the "authentic" ones. The {Re}HAPPENING is documented (photos, videos), but is this documentation anything more than a report? This is the same question that the *Retcon Black Mountain* project must confront.

To engage a relationship to place rather than functioning as an abstract model. BMC is not merely a pedagogy: it is a pedagogy *in a place*, and that place — a rural, segregationist, poor South — is not a backdrop but a condition. The college was constituted within a network of structural tensions: between the avant-gardism of its program and the conservatism of its rural Appalachian environment; between its communitarian ideal and the reality of racial segregation in Buncombe County, where Black students were not admitted until 1944 and under conditions that remained conflictual; between the internationalism of its exiled faculty — the Albers fleeing Nazi Germany, Jalowetz fleeing occupied Czechoslovakia — and the deep Americanness of its poets and essayists, from Olson and Creeley to the transcendentalist tradition of Emerson and Thoreau. The cases examined above illustrate the spectrum of responses: the {Re}HAPPENING takes place on the Lake Eden site itself but in a heritage context bearing no relation to BMC's material conditions; the White Mountain College assumes its deterritorialization but raises the question of what remains specifically *Black Mountain* in a model transposable anywhere; Dreyblatt, by making the Europe-America distance the very subject of the work, is perhaps the most lucid.

To constitute a collective that did not exist before it. Reenactment inherits from ritual its collective dimension: the myth is reactualized only *together*, the presence of assembled bodies is the condition of substantialization. For BMC, this operation is decisive: the college's originality did not reside in a method or a corpus of works but in a *form of collective life*. The farm, the shared meals, the communal chores were not peripheral to the pedagogical project but its conditions. Albers did not merely teach color: he lunched with his students. Cage did not conceive *Theater Piece No. 1* in a studio: he activated it in a dining hall, with people who knew each other, who lived together. A BMC reenactment that does not constitute a collective — a museum retrospective, a chatbot, an academic publication — may be excellent in other respects, but it misses the layer in which BMC existed. Conversely, the {Re}HAPPENING succeeds here where it falls short elsewhere: it gathers, once a year, a community of artists at the Lake Eden site. Dreyblatt is ambivalent: the workshops constitute a temporary collective, but within the prescribed framework of the academic institution.

These five operations do not constitute a grid to be filled — none of the projects examined satisfies all of them, and this incompleteness may be a structural property of BMC reenactment: a place defined by experimentation resists, by design, any evaluation that would claim to fix the conditions of success. But they share an unthought that must now be formulated.

### Recursive reenactment

Industrialized societies — the United States, China, Europe, and by contagion the rest of the world — have entered a technical regime that might be called, borrowing Hui's term, a *recursive neural society*. The artificial neural networks that govern algorithmic recommendation, text and image generation, behavioral prediction, financial modeling, surveillance, and logistics are not tools *in the service of* society: they have become its cognitive infrastructure. And the operative mode of these systems is, structurally, a reenactment. Every inference of a language model is a statistical putting-back-into-action of its training corpus. The algorithmic feed reenacts the user's past preferences in the form of prediction. The generative model reenacts human language at each token produced. Recursivity — in the sense Hui gives the term in *Recursivity and Contingency* (2019): an operation that takes itself as input, modifying itself in the process — is not a metaphor for repetition: it is the technical regime in which repetition now takes place.

If this is correct, then "putting back into action" is no longer a choice — artistic, memorial, political. It is the default technical condition of the contemporary world. The question shifts: no longer "why reenact?" but "how to reenact *otherwise* than the machine already does?" This hypothesis does not disqualify the theoretical frameworks mobilized in the preceding sections — Eliade on ritual, Kierkegaard on repetition, Deleuze on difference, Benjamin on the aura, Collingwood on re-thinking — but it reframes them. These authors thought the return in a world where reproduction was either mechanical (Benjamin's photography) or psychic (Freud's repetition compulsion). We live in a world where reproduction is *computational and recursive* — and this difference is not one of degree but of kind, because the machine does not reproduce an original: it generates from statistical distributions. There is no longer an original in Benjamin's sense. The question of fidelity (Collingwood) and that of difference (Deleuze) are simultaneously obsolete — the machine seeks neither — and radicalized, since it is now the human practitioner who must reintroduce both into a system that, left to itself, produces only statistical convergence.

The five operations are directly reconfigured by this regime. Produce knowledge? The machine does — but tautologically: it retrieves in its outputs what was put in its inputs. Transform? The machine personalizes, it does not transform — it confirms the user in their preferences, it does not displace them. Leave a corpus? The machine documents everything, but its documentation is undifferentiated — everything is archived, nothing is retained in the strong sense. Engage a place? The machine is deterritorialized by construction — it treats BMC and any other corpus with the same topological indifference. Constitute a collective? The machine produces user communities, not collectives — aggregates of individual behaviors, not shared forms of life. Each of the five operations is thus defined in the negative of what the machine does: human reenactment is what *resists* computational recursivity, not by ignoring it but by reintroducing into the loop what it cannot produce — opacity, material resistance, the presence of bodies, conflict, and above all failure.

### Failure as operation

BMC reenactments almost never reenact failure — and the machine, by construction, does not integrate it either. The algorithm optimizes; it does not fail productively. Yet BMC is not only the story of a pedagogical success: it is also, and perhaps primarily, the story of a failure. The college ended in 1957 in debt, attrition, personal conflicts, and student flight. It was never accredited. Its economic model was never viable. The reenactments reenact the *aura*: the Cage *Happening*, the Albers teachings, the interdisciplinarity, the utopian community — everything that, according to Benjamin, does not survive reproduction. The {Re}HAPPENING is a *festival* — it celebrates BMC, it does not explore its fragility. The White Mountain College borrows the pedagogical model but not the insolvency. The retrospectives transform into heritage what was, in daily life, a precarious enterprise. Even Dreyblatt's *Multiverse* tends to constitute BMC as a stabilized object of knowledge rather than an unfinished process. The selection is structural: one wants the return of the glorious without the return of the terrible. One replays the *Summer Institutes* but not Rice's defamation lawsuits, not the conflicts that drove out half the faculty in 1949, not the empty classrooms of the final years. A BMC reenactment that does not take on precariousness, debt, and dissolution reenacts only half of its object — and perhaps the less instructive half. Failure is not the opposite of the operation — it is its sixth modality: the one that most sharply distinguishes human reenactment from machinic recursivity, because productive failure — the kind that teaches, destabilizes, forces one to begin again differently — is what the machine does not know how to do.

## Conclusion

The author of this essay is not merely an observer of BMC reenactments: he is a practitioner of them. The Eden Summer Institute (2024-2025) is part of a larger project — the Eden project — that I initiated in 2015 with the purchase of a thousand-dollar house in Eden, North Carolina, and developed over ten years as an artistic and territorial laboratory. The questions I pose, I first tested on myself. What follows is the essay's turning back on its own conditions.

The development of "Theodore" — a chatbot trained on BMC archives — confronted me for several years with the aporias described above. Simulating by artificial intelligence the conversations between Albers and Fuller is technically feasible — but literalism is rarely the sign of intellectual effectiveness. Reconstructing what Albers "would have said" through a chatbot whose training and biases one does not control belongs to the same fantasy as the costume-era reconstruction: the illusion of transparency to the past, where productive reenactment assumes opacity and distance. The irony is that AI, presented as a tool of disclosure, is itself a black box. But long-term experimentation shifted the question. Theodore was for a long time a mirror before becoming a window: the point is no longer to produce a learned parrot capable of ventriloquizing the archives, but a *troublemaker* that destabilizes the researcher's certainties, generates unexpected connections, and reveals, through its very errors, the blind spots of the corpus. What Theodore taught me is not what Albers *would have said* — it is what *I wanted him to say*, which archives were missing, which ghosts I was summoning, what I was seeking in this impossible conversation. This is the difference between reenactment as *consumption of the specter* and reenactment as *diagnosis of the haunting*.

The Eden project raises another question — that of *memorial gentrification*. A French artist who buys a thousand-dollar house in a deindustrialized county to make contemporary art in the name of BMC — there is in this gesture a form of gentrification, however microscopic. Rockingham County did not ask to become a reenactment site. The thousand-dollar house is not an act of resistance — it is also the sign of a real estate collapse linked to deindustrialization. The paradox is structural: the more the BMC reenactment "succeeds" (attracts audiences, secures funding, gains visibility), the further it moves from the conditions of the original (precariousness, marginality, invisibility). It is the same paradox as Abramovic at the Guggenheim: "exhibition value" triumphing over "cult value." Nora formulated the mechanism: "realms of memory" (*lieux de memoire*) exist only because "environments of memory" (*milieux de memoire*) have disappeared. I have tried to take on this contradiction rather than ignore it: to work *with* the territory rather than *on* the territory. But the contradiction remains — and naming it is perhaps more honest than claiming to have resolved it.

An essay on the reenactment of BMC, written by someone who has spent ten years reenacting BMC, is a reenactment of another order — a reenactment through writing, which claims to analyze what it has practiced. The risk is twofold: complacency and blindness. But the criterion the essay has developed applies to itself: *self-reflexive capacity* — a reenactment whose *form* betrays an awareness of the impossibility of what it attempts. This is what the best works traversed in this essay have in common: Huyghe, in *Remake*, shows *what it is to redo*. Deller, at Orgreave, fabricates a third object that is neither the battle nor a film about the battle. Perry exhibits AI's inability to simulate Black bodies, and this inability *is* the work. This essay is an *essai* — in the same Montaignian sense as BMC and the ESI: an attempt, a trial of thought whose outcome is not guaranteed.

The question that opens this essay — "What is a *good* reenactment?" — has shifted along the way. It does not call for a normative answer but for a reformulation: no longer *how to judge* a reenactment, but *under what conditions does reproduction become an imagination?* Jennifer Allen had proposed a neighboring displacement: the question is no longer "Is this reenactment faithful to the past?" but "Is this reenactment faithful to our present?" This reversal — from archaeology to politics — is the lesson of all the works we have traversed. But the recursive neural society demands an additional turn of the screw: the "present" to which reenactment must be faithful is a present in which the machine already reenacts everything, permanently, without intention or memory. To be faithful to *this* present is to reenact while assuming that the gesture occurs *within* and *against* the recursive loop — that the artist, the researcher, the collective operate inside a system that converts all memory into prediction and all archives into training corpora. Kierkegaardian repetition, not recollection. Deleuzian difference, not the same. Bourriaud's *Postproduction*, not the Puy du Fou reconstruction. And Foster's afterwardness, not Burger's farce.

But this must be nuanced. If *symbolic* substantialization is dead — if no reenactment can bring back the past *as such*, in its full presence — there exists a place where something *does* happen nonetheless, a place the machine cannot occupy: the body. Anwar Congo, replaying his murders in *The Act of Killing*, ends up vomiting. This retching is neither representation nor symbol: it is an irruption of the real into the play, a symptom in the Freudian sense — the return, through the body, of what consciousness had repressed. Similarly, Horwitz's *hardcores* who lose weight to resemble the starving soldiers of 1864 no longer *play* hunger — they experience it. And the participants in the *ta'ziyeh* who flagellate themselves do not *represent* Hussein's suffering — they bleed. There is in reenactment a residual substantialization, which is not symbolic (the bread does not become the body) but physiological: the body does not pretend. This may be the only site of truth in reenactment — and the only site that computational recursivity cannot reach. The point where the distance between past and present is abolished not by belief (Eliade) nor by imagination (Simondon) nor by computation (the machine) but by the symptom. The reenactment that "succeeds" is the one where reproduction ceases to be reproduction and tips into experience — where something unforeseen occurs that neither the archive nor the project nor the algorithm had anticipated.

It is no longer a matter of knowing whether the olive tree is the "real one," but of knowing what grows when you replant it — and what city is built around it, however fragile and immaterial.

[^31]: Bourriaud, Nicolas. *Relational Aesthetics* [*Esthétique relationnelle*, 1998]. Trans. Simon Pleasance and Fronza Woods. Dijon: Les Presses du réel, 2002. Bourriaud defines relational aesthetics as "an aesthetic theory consisting in judging artworks on the basis of the inter-human relations which they represent, produce, or prompt" (p. 112).

[^32]: Bishop, Claire. "Antagonism and Relational Aesthetics." *October*, no. 110, Fall 2004, pp. 51–79. Bishop opposes to Bourriaud's "micro-utopias" a model grounded in dissensus (via Laclau and Mouffe): political art is not that which produces conviviality but that which makes antagonisms visible.

[^33]: David Claerbout's *Bordeaux Piece* (2004) consists of 70 films shot at 10-minute intervals in a house designed by Rem Koolhaas in Bordeaux. The actors repeat the same dialogue at each take, but the natural light, the time of day, and micro-variations in performance transform each iteration. The work lasts 14 hours — the duration of a day of light. One might also cite his *Vietnam, 1967, near Duc Pho (reconstruction after Hiromichi Mine)* (2001), in which the artist returns to the site of a plane crash photographed during the Vietnam War, films the landscape 33 years later, and superimposes the still image of the burning plane on the video of the contemporary landscape. The resulting image, "temporally hybrid and medially unclassifiable" (Ingrid Hölzl, *Intermédialités*, no. 17, 2011), embodies reenactment as temporal collision: the past refuses to fall, the present refuses to forget it.

[^34]: Jean-Christophe Royoux, "Remaking Cinema," in *Cinéma?-Cinéma. Contemporary Art and the Cinematic Experience*, Jaap Guldemond (ed.), Stedelijk van Abbemuseum Eindhoven / NAi Publishers, Rotterdam, 1999, p. 22.

[^35]: The proximity between Benjamin and the Huyghean "aesthetics of reconstruction" was suggested by Jean-Charles Masséra (2000), who structures his essay on *The Third Memory* as a remake of Debord's *The Society of the Spectacle* — which was itself, in many respects, a critical extension of the Benjaminian thesis. Craig Owens (1980) explicitly mobilizes Benjamin's *Habilitationsschrift* on the *Trauerspiel* (*The Origin of German Tragic Drama*, 1928) to define the "allegorical impulse" of contemporary art — an impulse of which reenactment is a direct manifestation.

[^36]: Fried, Michael. *Why Photography Matters as Art as Never Before*. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2008. Fried develops the concept of "absorbedness" — inherited from Diderot and the painting of Chardin and Greuze — to describe how Wall's tableaux exclude the spectator's consciousness: the figures never look at the camera, the world of the image is closed upon itself. This "closure" is, for Fried, what makes Wall an artist of the pictorial tradition rather than the documentary tradition — and thus, paradoxically, what removes him from the interpellative dimension of political reenactment.

[^37]: Crimp, Douglas. "The Photographic Activity of Postmodernism." *October*, vol. 15, Winter 1980, pp. 91–101. Crimp mobilizes the Benjaminian notion of the aura to analyze postmodern photography (Sherman, Sherrie Levine, Richard Prince) as a practice of "decreation" of the original.

[^38]: Freud, Sigmund. *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* [*Jenseits des Lustprinzips*, 1920]. Trans. James Strachey. In *The Standard Edition of the Complete Psychological Works of Sigmund Freud*, vol. 18. London: Hogarth Press, 1955. The concept of *Wiederholungszwang* appears in chapter III, beginning with the observation of the child's *Fort-Da* game — compulsively replaying the mother's departure — and of traumatic war neuroses, in which the dream ceaselessly returns the subject to the scene of the traumatic event.

[^39]: On the link between reenactment and trauma therapy, see van der Kolk, Bessel. *The Body Keeps the Score: Brain, Mind, and Body in the Healing of Trauma*. New York: Viking, 2014. Van der Kolk shows that trauma is "stored" in the body rather than in narrative memory, and that the most effective therapeutic approaches involve bodily reenactment rather than speech alone.

[^40]: Arnold Dreyblatt, interview with Verena Kittel, "Making-Of: Interview with Arnold Dreyblatt," *Black Mountain Research*, 2015. Online: black-mountain-research.com. See also Dreyblatt, Arnold and Meyer, Petra Maria (eds.). *Black Mountain College as Multiverse*. Dortmund: Verlag Kettler, 2022.
