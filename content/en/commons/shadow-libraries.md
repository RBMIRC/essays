## Abstract

This essay argues that shadow libraries — informal digital infrastructures for sharing knowledge outside commercial and legal frameworks — share deep narratives with the place of the library in literature and contemporary art, and can accordingly be analyzed as a distinct practice at the intersection of conceptual art, institutional critique, and social sculpture. Drawing on three decades of shadow library development (1996–2025), this research positions these platforms not simply as piracy or activism, but as aesthetic projects that fundamentally reimagine the relationship between knowledge, access, and the commons. Through the analysis of major initiatives — UbuWeb, AAAAARG, Monoskop, Memory of the World, Bibliotecha, and Sci-Hub — as well as emerging Francophone practices such as "Les Nouveaux Habits du Colportage," this essay develops a critical vocabulary for understanding infrastructure as artistic medium. The central thesis is that *custodianship* — the ongoing acts of care, maintenance, and circulation that sustain these libraries — represents a form of artistic practice that challenges conventional notions of authorship, originality, and the artwork as discrete object.

**Keywords:** shadow libraries, digital commons, infrastructure aesthetics, custodianship, knowledge access, Francophone ecosystem, UbuWeb, AAAAARG, Memory of the World, Bibliotecha, Monoskop, Sci-Hub, Les Nouveaux Habits du Colportage, pirate care, feminist servers

## I. INTRODUCTION — "Share this letter"

On November 30, 2015, a group of scholars, artists, and activists published an open letter entitled "In Solidarity with Library Genesis and Sci-Hub" [1]. The letter, hosted on custodians.online, was a response to the lawsuit filed by academic publisher Elsevier against two of the world's largest shadow libraries. What made this document remarkable was not simply its political stance, but its reconceptualization of what it means to care for knowledge. The signatories — among them Kenneth Goldsmith, Sean Dockray, Marcell Mars, Tomislav Medak, Dušan Barok, and Femke Snelting — positioned themselves not simply as activists or critics, but as *custodians*: guardians of a public good that institutions had failed to protect.

The letter's closing exhortation reads like an artistic manifesto: "Share this letter — read it in public — leave it in the printer. Share your writing — digitize a book — upload your files. Don't let our knowledge be crushed. Care for the libraries — care for the metadata — care for the backup." [2] The dedication — "In loving memory of Aaron Swartz" — invokes the young programmer who took his own life in 2013 while facing federal prosecution for downloading academic articles from JSTOR. Swartz had written the "Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto" in 2008, declaring: "Sharing isn't immoral — it's a moral imperative. Only those blinded by greed would refuse to let a friend make a copy." [3] His death transformed him into a martyr of the open access movement and established the stakes of shadow library practice: this was not simply about convenience or cost, but about fundamental questions of the right to know.

This essay takes seriously the proposition that shadow libraries are not merely tools for accessing texts, but aesthetic projects worthy of attention in art history and criticism. The artists, programmers, and researchers who build and maintain these infrastructures make choices that are simultaneously technical, political, and aesthetic. The selection of texts, the design of interfaces, the architecture of networks, the cultivation of communities — all constitute creative acts that shape how knowledge circulates and who can participate in its circulation. To understand shadow libraries as art is to recognize that infrastructure itself can be a medium, that maintenance can be a form of creation, and that the boundaries between artwork, archive, and institution have become productively unstable in digital culture.

### Defining the shadow library

The term "shadow library" requires careful definition, as it covers a range of practices with distinct characteristics. In the broadest sense, a shadow library is any digital collection that operates outside the legal frameworks of copyright and commercial publishing. This includes massive aggregators such as Library Genesis (LibGen), which hosts over 2.4 million books and 80 million scientific articles, as well as small curatorial collections running on a single Raspberry Pi computer. The "shadow" in shadow library suggests not simply illegality, but a parallel existence: these libraries double the official infrastructures of knowledge, providing access to the same texts through alternative channels.

What distinguishes shadow libraries from mere piracy is their institutional character. Unlike file-sharing networks oriented toward entertainment media, shadow libraries typically focus on scholarly and cultural materials. They develop cataloging systems, maintain metadata, and cultivate communities of users. As Jacopo Rasmi observes: "The existence of shadow libraries belongs neither to the universe of public institutions, nor to the sphere of private enterprise: if one had to assign them a model of governance and reproduction, it would rather be that of commons founded on self-organization by a group of concerned individuals." [4] Many operate with explicit ideological commitments — to open access, knowledge commons, cognitive justice — that position them as interventions in debates over the future of research and education.

The Monoskop wiki, maintained by Dušan Barok since 2004, offers a useful taxonomy distinguishing "large shadow libraries" from "small shadow libraries" or "parallel libraries." Large shadow libraries — LibGen, Sci-Hub, Z-Library — operate at scale through automated harvesting, serving millions of users worldwide. Small shadow libraries — AAAAARG, Memory of the World, Monoskop itself, Bibliotecha — emphasize curation, community, and specific intellectual orientations. This distinction is crucial for understanding shadow libraries as art: while the large libraries function primarily as infrastructure (albeit with their own aesthetic dimensions), the small libraries operate more clearly as cultural projects whose formal and social characteristics call for critical art analysis.

This essay does not claim to be exhaustive. It would be impossible to enumerate the projects — associative, pedagogical, artistic, or simply improvised — that participate today in the practice of shadow libraries. For every initiative documented here, dozens of others operate in the shadows, with no fixed domain name, no manifesto, sometimes no trace other than a shared hard drive in a workshop or a server left running in a living room. Proliferation mirrors radiation: it is precisely because these practices spread through imitation, contagion, and necessity that they resist census. A corpus bias must also be acknowledged: the projects analyzed here are predominantly Euro-American and Anglophone, not because shadow library practices are absent from the Global South — Sci-Hub is massively used in Iran, India, and China; the Palestine Shadow Library responds to urgencies that the West ignores — but because the available documentation reproduces the asymmetries it claims to critique. The case studies that follow therefore constitute not a panorama but entry points — projects whose visibility, documentation, or theoretical density allow for the articulation of questions that traverse the entire field.

### The expanded field of library art

Positioning shadow libraries within art history requires expanding our conception of what art can be and do. Rosalind Krauss's influential essay "Sculpture in the Expanded Field" (1979) [5] provides a useful model. Krauss argued that sculpture had extended beyond its traditional definition to encompass practices that could only be defined negatively — as "not-landscape" and "not-architecture" — opening a field of possibilities including site constructions, marked sites, axiomatic structures, and sculpture proper. The shadow library analogously occupies an expanded field defined by its relations to neighboring categories: it is non-institution (in that it lacks official sanction) while being institutional in its functions; non-archive (lacking the authority of official memory) while being archival in its practices; non-artwork (lacking the signature of individual authorship) while being aesthetic in its form and effects.

Marcel Broodthaers's *Musée d'Art Moderne, Département des Aigles* (1968–1972) established the fictive institution as artistic medium. Operating through twelve "sections" in various locations, Broodthaers's museum accumulated images of eagles from multiple sources, questioning the museum's classificatory authority while creating a parallel institutional structure. The project demonstrated that the institution itself — its systems of classification, exhibition, and legitimation — could be the medium of the work. Shadow libraries extend this logic into the digital domain, creating parallel institutions that both critique and reproduce the functions of official knowledge infrastructures.

Hal Foster's essay "An Archival Impulse" (2004) [6] identified a tendency in contemporary art to collect, store, and present cultural materials in ways that contest official historical narratives. Shadow libraries participate in this archival impulse, but with a crucial difference: rather than producing discrete works that represent archival practices, they build actually functioning archives that serve real scholarly and cultural needs. The work is not the representation of an archive but the archive itself.

### Historical coordinates

Shadow libraries did not emerge ex nihilo from digital culture, but draw on long traditions of alternative knowledge distribution. The Soviet samizdat system — forbidden texts typed, hand-copied, and circulated through networks of trust — established many of the logics that animate contemporary shadow libraries, examined in detail in the section devoted to the aesthetics of piracy. Library Genesis, founded in Russia around 2008, explicitly traces its lineage to this culture.

The figure of the *colporteur* — the itinerant book peddler who distributed cheap popular literature across early modern Europe — offers another historical precedent. Colporteurs carried the *bibliothèque bleue*, small blue-covered booklets containing almanacs, romances, religious texts, and practical guides. Operating outside the official book trade, they brought texts to rural populations who lacked access to urban bookshops and libraries. The research project "Les Nouveaux Habits du Colportage," developed since 2023 at the ERG in Brussels, explicitly reclaims this figure as a model for contemporary digital distribution.

Artist-run spaces and alternative publishing networks also inform shadow library practice. Fluxus mail art networks, artists' books, and edition multiples established precedents for using publication and distribution as artistic media. Martha Rosler's *If You Lived Here...* (1989) created a functioning archive and reading space devoted to housing and homelessness, demonstrating that the library form could serve as a platform for artistic and political intervention.

### Stakes of the argument

Positioning shadow libraries as art is not merely an academic exercise but carries implications for how we understand contemporary creative practice. If infrastructure can be a medium, then the architects of digital systems deserve recognition as cultural producers alongside painters, sculptors, and performers. If maintenance and care constitute creative acts, then conventional notions of authorship founded on originary creation must be revised. If libraries can be artworks, then the boundaries between cultural production and cultural preservation become productively unstable.

This argument also matters for understanding the future of knowledge in digital culture. As academic publishing concentrates into ever-larger corporate conglomerates, and as access to research is increasingly mediated by platform capitalism, shadow libraries represent alternative possibilities for the circulation of knowledge. Their artistic dimensions — their attention to form, their cultivation of communities, their refusal of purely instrumental logics — model ways of relating to knowledge that differ from both commercial publishing and state-managed institutions.

A caveat is in order, however, regarding the status of the argument itself. This essay oscillates, at times deliberately, between two distinct positions: the first holds that shadow libraries *are* works of art; the second, more modest, holds that the tools of art criticism *illuminate* these practices in productive ways. The difference is not trivial. The first position risks subjecting practices born of necessity to a regime of valorization that is not their own — that of the art institution, with its hierarchies, circuits of recognition, and markets. The second recognizes that the categories of art history — expanded field, social sculpture, institutional critique — function here as heuristic tools rather than ontological verdicts: they serve to *make visible* dimensions of these practices (formal choices, production of publics, tension with institutions) that other disciplinary frameworks — legal, computational, sociological — leave in shadow, without claiming to settle the question of whether these practices *are* art in the sense that a painting or a performance is. Sci-Hub does not need to be called an artwork to be worthy of critical attention; requalifying it as such might even diminish it by subjecting it to criteria that are not its own. This essay does not intend to definitively resolve this tension. It argues instead that the tension itself is productive — that it compels us to rethink simultaneously what we mean by "art" and what we mean by "library."

## II. Infrastructure

## Toward an aesthetics of infrastructure

### From object to system

The twentieth century witnessed a progressive expansion of art beyond the bounded object. Clement Greenberg's modernism, with its insistence on medium specificity and optical experience, gave way to practices integrating duration, process, context, and system. Donald Judd's "specific objects" — the term comes from his 1965 essay, though Judd always refused the "Minimalist" label that critical reception imposed on him — already troubled the distinction between painting and sculpture by producing three-dimensional objects whose physical presence in the viewer's space became constitutive of the work. Process Art made the transformation of materials over time an element of the work itself — anticipating the open temporality of shadow libraries, which are never "finished" but continually updated, migrated, backed up. Conceptual Art subordinated material execution to idea and documentation, opening the way for practices where infrastructure — the protocol, the catalog, the network — takes precedence over the individual object.

Jack Burnham's essay "Systems Esthetics" (1968) [7] articulated this shift in terms borrowed from cybernetics and general systems theory. Burnham argued that the art object was giving way to the "art system," in which "the components are the activities of people in various relationships, the procedures for realizing and maintaining these relationships, and the activities that result from their maintenance" [8]. This systemic perspective anticipated the relational turn that Nicolas Bourriaud would theorize in *Relational Aesthetics* (1998): art as the production of intersubjective encounters and social forms rather than discrete objects. Shadow libraries exemplify systems aesthetics, with their distributed architectures, collaborative maintenance, and ongoing processes of accumulation and curation.

The emergence of net.art in the 1990s brought these concerns directly into the digital domain. Artists such as Olia Lialina, Vuk Ćosić, Martine Neddam (whose site mouchette.org, in 1996, created the fictive identity of a teenage girl as a networked work), Annie Abrahams (a pioneer of online performance from Montpellier), and Heath Bunting created works existing as websites, email exchanges, and network interventions. These practices established that the internet itself could be an artistic medium, with its specific affordances and constraints shaping the possibilities of creative work. The initial utopian moment of net.art — when the web seemed to offer a space beyond commercial and institutional capture — gave way to a more ambivalent "post-internet" condition, but the fundamental intuition remains: digital networks are not merely tools for distributing art but media in which art can be made.

Shadow libraries inherit this history while transforming it. Unlike net.art works that typically foreground their art status, shadow libraries present themselves first as functional tools. Their artistic dimensions emerge not from explicit aesthetic claims but from the formal choices embedded in their design and operation. The question becomes not "is this art?" but rather "what aesthetic principles inform these systems, and what do they reveal about the possible relations between knowledge, technology, and community?"

This reframing has methodological implications. Rather than seeking to justify shadow libraries as art by showing they satisfy a preexisting definition, one might ask what shadow libraries reveal about the possibilities of art in networked environments. The shadow library becomes not an object of classification but a site of inquiry — a place where questions of infrastructure, aesthetics, and politics intersect productively.

The artist's book tradition provides another crucial genealogy. Since Ed Ruscha's *Twentysix Gasoline Stations* (1963), artists have explored the book as a medium with its own aesthetic possibilities. Ruscha deliberately avoided the preciousness associated with fine art editions, producing a book one could buy for a few dollars — and distributing it at gas stations rather than galleries. This democratizing gesture — making art accessible through cheap print — anticipates the logic of shadow libraries. The Fluxus movement similarly placed the multiple at the heart of its practice; George Maciunas's Fluxkits were distributed by mail at affordable prices. The emphasis on circulation and access, realized through cheap multiples and postal distribution, directly anticipates shadow library values.

Zine culture extends this lineage in a more radical direction. Born in science fiction circles in the 1930s (*fanzines*), it exploded with punk in the late 1970s: *Sniffin' Glue* in London (1976–1977), *Maximum Rocknroll* in San Francisco, V. Vale's *Search & Destroy* — photocopied, stapled publications distributed hand to hand or by mail, whose "homemade" aesthetic was inseparable from the message. The punk zine established a principle that shadow libraries would take up in the digital age: anyone who possesses a tool of reproduction can become a publisher, and the poverty of the medium is not a defect but a condition of its accessibility. Punk's "anyone can do it," often summed up by the famous three-guitar-chord diagram published in the fanzine *Sideburns* (1977) — "Now form a band" — finds its direct echo in shadow library install parties: "Now start a server."

In France, this tradition of guerrilla publishing has its own roots. The Situationists distributed tracts and *détournements* outside any editorial circuit; the counterculture editions of the 1970s — *Actuel*, the publications of the bookshop Parallèles — practiced a gift economy and network logic avant la lettre. More recently, the micro-publishing and risograph scene — with structures such as Lézard Graphique, Papier Machine, or fairs like Offprint — perpetuates the idea that the form of publication is a political act as much as an aesthetic one. DIY publishing, whether punk, feminist (Bikini Kill and Bratmobile's *riot grrrl zines* in the 1990s), or from queer communities (zines like Bruce LaBruce and G.B. Jones's *J.D.s*), has always linked self-publishing to the constitution of communities excluded from dominant channels. Shadow libraries inherit this dual function: they do not merely distribute content — they produce publics.

### The library as artistic form

Libraries have long attracted artistic interest, from Jorge Luis Borges's metaphysical fictions to conceptual artists' engagements with cataloging and classification. The library offers rich material for artistic exploration: its organizational systems impose order on heterogeneous materials; its architecture manifests beliefs about knowledge and public space; its institutional authority to preserve and provide access makes it a crucial site in the politics of memory. Before even examining shadow libraries, it is worth mapping the uses that the visual arts and literature have made of the library as form, device, and metaphor.

Literature has thematized the library as architecture of power. In *The Name of the Rose* (1980) [38], Umberto Eco makes the library a physical labyrinth whose plan reproduces a map of the known world; knowledge is guarded by traps, mirrors, and a poisoning monk. Eco's library is the narrative ancestor of the *gatekeeping* that shadow libraries seek to break: knowledge as dangerous power, access to which is reserved for those who control the keys. Georges Perec, in "Brief Notes on the Art and Manner of Arranging One's Books" (1978, reprinted in *Thoughts of Sorts*, 1985) [39], explores the very impossibility of classification: arranging by color, date, affinity, or size produces a different library each time — which is to say that every classification system is an active fiction, a creative act as much as an archival one. Perec's problem is the problem of all shadow libraries: how to organize abundance without betraying it?

In the visual arts, the library has become a medium in its own right. Rachel Whiteread's *Untitled (Library)* (1999) [40] is a white resin cast of an entire bookcase: what one sees is not the books but the imprint of the space *between* the books and the shelves — a library in negative that renders knowledge spectral and inaccessible. Claudio Parmiggiani's soot imprints (*Delocazione*, since 1970) [41] proceed from an analogous gesture: by burning objects against a wall, the artist preserves only their shadow, their residual trace — libraries of which nothing remains but the memory of combustion. These works raise the question of what endures when the material support disappears — a question that shadow libraries confront in technical form: server migration, data backup, the preservation of files whose formats become obsolete.

Thomas Hirschhorn's *Bataille Monument* (2002) created a temporary library and meeting space in a housing estate in Kassel during Documenta 11, making theoretical texts accessible to a community that official institutions neglected. The work functioned simultaneously as sculpture, library, community center, and social experiment, collapsing the categories that conventional art institutions keep separate. This library dimension is a constant in Hirschhorn's practice — the *Gramsci Monument* (Bronx, 2013), the *Musée Précaire Albinet* (Aubervilliers, 2004) all integrated a resource space made of photocopies, cardboard, tape, and conversation. The makeshift library, in these works, is not an adjunct to the installation: it is its engine, the place where the audience ceases to be spectators and becomes readers, interlocutors, participants. The poor material — the photocopy rather than the catalog, tape rather than the frame — is a formal choice as much as an economic one: it signals that knowledge circulates better when it is not precious. *Martha Rosler Library* (2005–2007) [42] proceeds from a neighboring but distinct gesture: Rosler moved her personal library of more than 7,000 volumes into galleries and art centers (MoMA, Antwerp, Liverpool), making it accessible to the public for the duration of the exhibition. The work is not the book — it is the act of sitting next to a stranger to read the same engaged text. The library becomes a device for producing publics, exactly as AAAAARG would a decade later in digital space.

Other artists have made the library an instrument of explicit institutional critique. Meschac Gaba, in his *Museum of Contemporary African Art* (1997–2002) [43] — a nomadic installation in twelve rooms presented from the Witte de With to Documenta 11 and subsequently acquired by Tate Modern — devoted one room to a Library containing works on African and international art. By placing the library at the heart of a fictive museum born from the observation that no space existed in Europe for contemporary African art, Gaba turns the book into a tool of epistemic repair. As he put it: "My museum has no walls. [...] I want to show artists that you can show work everywhere, you can do it on your own. You don't need four walls to determine your place, to decide who you are." [62] The library here is a piece of a fictive but operative institutional puzzle — it produces real effects in real institutions.

Theaster Gates's *Stony Island Arts Bank* (Chicago, since 2015) [44] radicalizes this approach by anchoring it in urban space. Gates acquired for one dollar an abandoned bank building on Chicago's South Side and transformed it into a cultural center housing, among other things, the 12,000 volumes of the Johnson Publishing Company library, publisher of *Ebony* and *Jet* — seven decades of African American cultural memory. Gates does not represent a library; he physically saves one from destruction and reinstalls it in a neighborhood whose institutions have been systematically disinvested. The gesture is simultaneously archival, architectural, and political: "How do we start to imagine ourselves as deeper caretakers of the things that exist in the world?" [63] Gates's *custodianship* — his very vocabulary is that of shadow libraries — operates at the intersection of social sculpture and reparative justice.

Shadow libraries extend these precedents in a crucial way. Unlike Broodthaers's fictive museum or Hirschhorn's temporary intervention, shadow libraries are actually functioning institutions that serve real needs over extended periods. UbuWeb has operated since 1996, AAAAARG since 2005, Memory of the World since 2012. These are not representations of libraries or commentaries on library functions, but actual libraries whose artistic dimensions emerge from their formal characteristics and social effects.

### Infrastructure as aesthetic practice

Recent research in media studies and geography has drawn attention to infrastructure as a crucial site of cultural and political meaning. Shannon Mattern's work on the "deep time of media infrastructure" [48] emphasizes that communication systems have long histories that shape their present forms. Keller Easterling's concept of "extrastatecraft" [49] identifies infrastructural space as "a medium of information" that organizes populations and resources according to logics that often escape public scrutiny.

The feminist server movement, centered on the Brussels-based collective Constant, has been particularly attentive to the aesthetic and political dimensions of infrastructure. The "Feminist Server Manifesto" from the "Are You Being Served?" workshop in 2013 [35] — whose principles will be examined in detail in the fourth part of this essay — articulates a language of ecology, situation, care, and politics that provides a framework for understanding shadow libraries as aesthetic practices embedded in social relations rather than as autonomous technical systems.

The concept of "network topology as composition" offers another angle on the aesthetics of infrastructure. Just as a musical composition arranges sounds in time and a visual composition arranges forms in space, a network architecture arranges nodes and connections in a relational structure. The choice between centralized and distributed architectures, between open and closed systems, between hierarchical and horizontal organizations — these are compositional choices with aesthetic as well as technical implications.

## Knowledge commons and the gift economy

### Theoretical frameworks of the commons

Elinor Ostrom's Nobel Prize–winning work on the governance of common-pool resources demonstrated that communities can sustainably manage shared resources through institutional arrangements that avoid both state control and privatization. Digital commons differ from Ostrom's paradigmatic cases on important points. Informational goods are non-rival: my use of a text does not diminish your ability to use the same text. This means that the "tragedy of the commons" narrative does not directly apply.

Charlotte Hess and Elinor Ostrom's *Understanding Knowledge as a Commons* (2007) [9] extended commons analysis to informational resources, identifying threats of both underproduction (insufficient incentives for creation) and enclosure (excessive restriction of access). Shadow libraries address enclosure by restoring the non-rival character of digital texts, making them available as common resources rather than commodities.

Hervé Le Crosnier, the French information science researcher [50], has been particularly influential in theorizing digital commons in Francophone contexts. His work emphasizes *biens communs informationnels* (informational commons) and their vulnerability to both market enclosure and state surveillance. Le Crosnier's framework helps explain what is distinctive about Francophone shadow library practice, with its insistence on self-hosting, data sovereignty, and collective governance.

### The gift economy in digital culture

Marcel Mauss's classic study *The Gift* (1925) [10] analyzed gift exchange in archaic societies as a "total social fact" involving economic, juridical, moral, aesthetic, and religious dimensions. The gift creates obligations that bind giver and receiver in ongoing relationships, in contrast to commodity exchange, which concludes with the transaction. Lewis Hyde's *The Gift: Creativity and the Artist in the Modern World* (1983) [11] extended Mauss's analysis to artistic production, arguing that works of art circulate (or should circulate) according to the logic of the gift rather than the commodity.

Shadow libraries instantiate gift economy principles in several ways. Contributors upload texts without expecting direct compensation, participating in a generalized exchange that benefits the community as a whole. Users who download texts incur an implicit obligation to contribute in return — through uploading, improving metadata, or simply maintaining the network through continued participation. This operates in stark contrast to capitalist platforms: "The technical and economic ecosystems of these platforms, based on the attraction and retention of attention, market profitability (via subscriptions, data sales, or advertising), and algorithmic automation, are light-years from the functioning of these clandestine libraries founded on discretion, the gift economy, and curious, studious engagement with their labyrinthine repertories." [12]

Kenneth Goldsmith's UbuWeb exemplifies the aesthetics of the gift economy. Since 1996, artists and researchers have contributed materials to the site without formal agreements or compensation. Goldsmith describes this as operating according to the logic of the gift: "The gift is to the commodity as the tribe is to the market, as the church is to the auction house, as the garage sale is to Sotheby's." [13]

### Counter-enclosure practices

The concept of "enclosure" derives from the historical process by which common lands in England were privatized between the fifteenth and nineteenth centuries. Scholars such as James Boyle [51] have applied this concept to intellectual property, arguing that extensions of copyright represent a "second enclosure movement" that privatizes the cultural commons. Lawrence Lessig's [52] work on "free culture" similarly identifies excessive copyright as restricting creativity and concentrating cultural power.

Shadow libraries can be understood as counter-enclosure practices, reclaiming for common use what has been enclosed by intellectual property law. This framing positions them within a long history of resistance to privatization.

Florence Piron's work on "cognitive justice" [53] extends this analysis to postcolonial contexts. Piron argues that dominant knowledge systems systematically exclude non-Western epistemologies, and that open access to research is a necessary but insufficient response. True cognitive justice requires not only access to existing knowledge but recognition of the diversity of ways of knowing. Projects such as the Palestine Shadow Library (2025) represent attempts to address this by creating counter-archives that preserve materials that dominant archives neglect.

## The aesthetics of piracy

### Piracy as cultural practice

The figure of the pirate carries complex cultural associations: criminality and freedom, theft and redistribution, individualism and communalism. Historical pirates operated outside the law but often with informal codes of governance more democratic than the societies they plundered. This ambivalence persists in contemporary usage, where "piracy" designates both a crime and a form of resistance.

The samizdat tradition provides a crucial precedent for understanding piracy as cultural practice. In the Soviet Union, forbidden texts were typed, hand-copied or carbon-copied, and circulated through networks of trust. As the Public Library project notes: "The dissemination of knowledge before the digital depended crucially on photocopying and pirate re-prints. Photocopied books and articles passed down from generation to generation and from scientist to scientist have enabled many to finish their studies, to become scientists and to teach younger generations." [14] Indeed, "the 'knowledge society' three decades ago would not have been conceivable without the illegal sharing of knowledge, just as the 'knowledge society' today isn't conceivable without the illegal sharing of knowledge." [15]

The Chinese concept of *shanzhai* offers another perspective on creative copying. Originally designating mountain bandit fortresses, *shanzhai* has come to name the practice of manufacturing copies of consumer goods that often improve upon or adapt originals for local markets. The research project "Les Nouveaux Habits du Colportage" integrates *shanzhai* into its conceptual vocabulary, suggesting that creative copying has multiple cultural genealogies beyond the Euro-American frame.

### The poor image and democratic circulation

Hito Steyerl's influential essay "In Defense of the Poor Image" (2009) [16] provides a crucial theoretical framework for understanding the material aesthetics of shadow libraries. Steyerl argues that the degraded, compressed, copied images that circulate through digital networks do not represent a fall from some originary plenitude but a distinct condition with its own characteristics and possibilities. The poor image is "a copy in motion" that "transforms quality into accessibility," sacrificing resolution for speed and ease of circulation.

This framework applies directly to shadow library texts, which often exist as imperfect scans, OCR-processed PDFs with errors, or files lacking metadata. From the standpoint of preservation, they are inferior to professionally produced editions. But from the standpoint of access, they are superior: a mediocre scan of a rare book is infinitely more accessible than an immaculate copy in a distant library. Moreover, as a peer-to-peer platform manifesto argues: "The collection benefits from exchange and circulation. Its digital artifacts do not deteriorate with use. On the contrary, thanks to the proliferation and dissemination of material, we obtain a (modest) measure of document preservation." [17]

Steyerl's analysis positions the poor image within a "class society of appearances," where high-resolution originals are reserved for the wealthy while degraded copies circulate among the masses. Shadow libraries intervene in this class society, redistributing cultural goods that official systems hoard.

However, the risk of aestheticizing precarity must be measured. There is a fine line between celebrating the poor image and rendering invisible the material constraints endured by those who produce and maintain these libraries. AAAAARG's .fail domain is not primarily an ironic gesture — it is the result of repeated lawsuits and forced migrations. UbuWeb's "dormancy" is not primarily an interesting temporal figure — it is the expression of real exhaustion in the face of unpaid labor. Aaron Swartz did not kill himself in an expanded field. The critical apparatus of art history, by transforming constraint into aesthetic category, risks anesthetizing precisely what it claims to reveal. The art-historical framing is not wrong — the formal choices are real, the aesthetic dimensions exist — but it remains partial if it does not keep in view the legal violence and attrition that constitute the ground of these practices. The aesthetics of the poor image should not allow us to forget that poverty, here, is not a stylistic choice but an imposed condition.

This is why contemporary thinking about shadow libraries shifts from the poor image to the labor that produces it. Scanning, OCR processing, metadata creation, and uploading require labor, typically performed by unpaid volunteers. The poor image does not circulate by spontaneous generation but through the efforts of *folk archivists* whose labor often remains invisible. The vocabulary of *custodianship* names this labor; events such as scanning parties make it collective and social; publications such as Barok, Mars, Medak, and Thurston's *Careful: A Repertorium* (2025) theorize it. More broadly, attention shifts from the isolated object — the file, the scan, the copy — to the library as infrastructure: the networks, metadata, interfaces, and communities that enable discovery and access.

### Piracy and authorship

Roland Barthes's essay "The Death of the Author" (1967) [18] announced the dispersal of authorial authority into the "multidimensional space" of texts and readings. Michel Foucault's 1969 response, "What Is an Author?" [19], analyzed the "author function" as a principle of classification and valorization rather than a natural property of texts.

Shadow libraries complicate authorship on multiple levels. The individual texts they contain have authors in the conventional sense, but the library as a whole is a collective production assembled by many hands over time. Rasmi designates as "*folk archiving*" the logic of spontaneous, collaborative cataloging induced by the web. *Folk archivists* are non-professional practitioners who preserve and circulate cultural materials according to their own logics, outside official institutional frameworks. This perspective suggests that "what makes the value of an intellectual or artistic object is the possibility of its dissemination and use by diverse publics: this means that any obstacle to these operations — such as the right to restrict copies exercised by an author or, more often, a distributor — hinders its valorization." [20]

Andrea Francke and Eva Weinmayr's The Piracy Project (since 2010) [45] has explicitly explored this troubled zone between pirated copy and artistic original. The project consists of a traveling collection of pirated, modified, annotated, or détourned books — objects that are neither the originals nor simple reproductions, but transformations that interrogate the very status of authorship. At what point does a pirated copy acquire an identity of its own? Francke and Weinmayr's project directly prefigures the aesthetics of shadow libraries as institutional critique in action: the traveling library does not contain works — it *is* the work, and what it exhibits are the very operations of copying, transformation, and circulation that copyright law seeks to control. In a complementary mode, *The Art of Google Books* project (a Tumblr active since 2012) [46] documents the involuntary traces left by Google Books' mass digitization process: technicians' fingers caught by the scanner, crumpled pages, shadows of gloved hands. These artifacts — the human imprints within the industrial machine of digitization — remind us that behind every digital library, even the most automated, there is bodily, situated, fallible labor.

## III. Typologies

## The curatorial library — UbuWeb

### Kenneth Goldsmith, artist-archivist

Kenneth Goldsmith founded UbuWeb in 1996 as an online resource for avant-garde poetry, gradually expanding it to include film, sound, and other experimental cultural materials. As Monoskop documents: "UbuWeb is the most significant and largest online archive of avant-garde art; it was initiated and is led by conceptual artist Kenneth Goldsmith. UbuWeb has grown into a relevant and recognized critical institution of contemporary art. Artists want to see their work in its catalog and thus agree to a relationship with UbuWeb that has no formal contractual obligations." [21] Goldsmith's background as a conceptual poet — author of works such as *Day* (a transcription of an entire edition of the New York Times) and *Traffic* (a transcription of 24 hours of traffic reports) — informs UbuWeb's aesthetics: like his writing, the archive performs an act of selection and recontextualization that transforms its source materials.

Goldsmith describes UbuWeb as existing in a "legal gray zone," hosting materials without formal permission from rights holders. The site's motto — "If We Had to Ask Permission, We Wouldn't Exist" [22] — articulates the pragmatic logic that enables its operation. Rather than seeking licenses that would be impossible to obtain for thousands of works, UbuWeb proceeds on the assumption that most of its materials are "historically valuable, commercially worthless" [23].

In 2013, Goldsmith was named the first Poet Laureate of the Museum of Modern Art, a position that brought institutional recognition to his practice while potentially compromising his outsider status. His 2020 book, *Duchamp Is My Lawyer: The Polemics, Pragmatics, and Poetics of UbuWeb*, articulates the site's philosophy, arguing that UbuWeb operates in the tradition of the readymade: selecting, framing, and presenting materials that acquire new meaning through their inclusion in the archive. The invocation of Duchamp in the title positions UbuWeb within the historiographic lineages of appropriation and institutional critique.

The relationship between UbuWeb and its source materials parallels that between Duchamp's readymades and their industrial origins. Just as Duchamp's urinal acquired art status through its placement in an exhibition context, materials on UbuWeb acquire new significance through their placement within Goldsmith's curatorial framework. The archive transforms its contents not by altering them but by recontextualizing them — gathering marginalized works into a coherent collection that affirms their importance.

### Curatorial vision and canon formation

UbuWeb's contents reflect a coherent curatorial vision centered on the historical avant-gardes and their contemporary extensions. The site's sections — concrete poetry, sound poetry, conceptual writing, film, music, texts — map a particular understanding of experimental culture that privileges certain movements and figures while necessarily excluding others. To browse UbuWeb is to encounter a canon: not the official canon of dominant institutions, but an alternative canon of marginalized and forgotten experimenters.

This canon-forming function raises questions about the politics of inclusion and exclusion. Who decides what belongs on UbuWeb? What does inclusion signify, and what are the consequences of exclusion? Goldsmith has been transparent about his curatorial role, describing the site as reflecting his personal tastes and knowledge. This transparency differs from the supposedly neutral or exhaustive claims of official archives, but it also concentrates authority in a single individual.

The tension between alternative and dominant canons is productive rather than simply oppositional. UbuWeb has helped rescue figures from oblivion: concrete poets like Augusto de Campos and Décio Pignatari, sound poets like Henri Chopin and Bernard Heidsieck, filmmakers like Stan Brakhage and Maya Deren. By making their work accessible, UbuWeb has influenced research, teaching, and contemporary practice. The archive does not merely preserve a past canon but actively constructs a usable history that shapes the present.

UbuWeb's aesthetic philosophy can be characterized by several principles. First, an ethics of access: making materials available takes priority over formal perfection. Many of UbuWeb's files are imperfect VHS transfers, low-resolution scans, or audio recordings of variable quality. This aligns with Steyerl's "poor image" aesthetic, accepting degradation as the price of circulation. Second, an anti-commercial stance: UbuWeb hosts materials that are "out-of-print, hard to find, or simply encyclopedic" rather than current commercial products. Third, an institutional critique that operates through construction rather than opposition — building an alternative archive that demonstrates other possibilities.

A tension runs through this argument, however. This essay — like most studies of shadow libraries — celebrates the distribution of authorship while organizing each case study around a singular figure: Goldsmith for UbuWeb, Dockray for AAAAARG, Mars and Medak for Memory of the World, Elbakyan for Sci-Hub, Barok for Monoskop. The narrative structure contradicts the theoretical thesis. Power does not disappear when it migrates from the commercial publisher to the pirate-curator; it changes form. Institutional authority (the publisher who decides what to publish) gives way to charismatic authority (the individual whose taste and network determine what enters the archive). Goldsmith himself acknowledges this by describing UbuWeb as a reflection of his personal tastes. That this authority is exercised with generosity rather than for profit does not change its structure: one individual decides, others benefit. The question of whether shadow libraries create new forms of power even as they contest old ones deserves to be kept open rather than resolved in advance.

### UbuWeb's trajectory: dormancy and reactivation

In January 2024, Goldsmith announced that UbuWeb would become "dormant" after nearly three decades of operation. The announcement suggested exhaustion with the demands of maintaining the project and uncertainty about its future. For practitioners and researchers who had come to depend on UbuWeb's resources, the announcement was alarming — a reminder of the precarity that characterizes shadow library infrastructure.

In February 2025, however, Goldsmith announced the site's reactivation, citing political developments — presumably the return of the Trump administration — as motivation. This political framing explicitly positions UbuWeb as cultural resistance, suggesting that the archive's significance derives not only from its contents but from its posture of defiance toward restrictive cultural policies. The dormancy-and-return narrative also demonstrates something important about the temporality of shadow libraries: these projects can suspend and resume, going quiet when energy wanes and returning when circumstances demand.

The "JSTOR Pirate Headquarters" project of 2014 at the Kunsthalle Düsseldorf offers another angle on UbuWeb's institutional positioning. For this project, Goldsmith installed a station where visitors could download academic articles from JSTOR — not through illegal means but using the Kunsthalle's legitimate institutional access. The project made visible the unequal access that institutional subscriptions create while operating entirely within legal bounds. This kind of institutional manipulation — using legitimate access to critique access restrictions — represents a different strategy from UbuWeb's gray-zone operation.

## The social library — AAAAARG and The Public School

### Sean Dockray's expanded practice

Sean Dockray, an Australian-American artist and researcher, founded AAAAARG in 2005 while involved with the Telic Arts Exchange in Los Angeles. The name, originally standing for "Artists, Architects, and Activists Reading Group," has since become a cry of frustration — "aaaaargh!" — that captures the site's conflicted relationship with legal authority. Dockray's practice extends beyond AAAAARG to include The Public School (a platform for organizing free courses), research on automation and machine learning, and academic work including a doctoral thesis entitled "Performing Algorithms: Automation and Accident."

Unlike Goldsmith's individual authorship over UbuWeb, Dockray designed AAAAARG as a social infrastructure. Users can request texts, upload materials, create "collections" (thematic groupings), and participate in discussions. The result is a library shaped by collective contribution rather than individual curation, reflecting the reading practices of artistic and theoretical communities worldwide. To browse AAAAARG is to encounter a record of what these communities were reading and discussing over the past two decades.

Dockray's concept of the "ludic library" emphasizes play and experimentation in library practice. Rather than the seriousness of official research, AAAAARG invites users to follow their curiosity, make unexpected connections, and treat reading as a form of exploration. This ludic orientation connects to Dockray's broader interest in autodidactic practice — learning outside formal educational institutions — and informs The Public School's model of user-generated curricula.

The theoretical sophistication of AAAAARG's community distinguishes it from more general-purpose shadow libraries. The texts that circulate reflect the reading lists of art schools, philosophy departments, and activist study groups: critical theory, continental philosophy, political economy, media studies, art criticism. This intellectual specificity creates a particular kind of community — readers who share not only access but orientations, concerns, and vocabularies. Mars and Medak designate these formations as *studious publics*: communities that constitute themselves through the very act of shared study, where collective reading produces a public rather than the public pre-existing the reading. The concept shifts attention from the library as service (providing texts to a given public) to the library as process of subjectivation (producing a public through shared texts).

### The library as social sculpture

Joseph Beuys's concept of "social sculpture" (*Soziale Plastik*) proposed that social relations themselves could be the medium of artistic practice. Beuys extended the concept of sculpture from the shaping of physical materials to the shaping of social forms, arguing that "everyone is an artist" capable of participating in this expanded practice. Shadow libraries, particularly those with strong community dimensions like AAAAARG, can be understood as social sculptures in Beuys's sense: their primary medium is not the texts they contain but the relationships they enable between readers.

AAAAARG's community orientation distinguishes it from both individual curatorial projects (like UbuWeb) and massive automated aggregators (like LibGen). The site's users know each other, at least virtually; they share references, debate interpretations, and form intellectual connections that extend beyond the platform. Texts function as nodes in a social network, occasions for encounter and exchange rather than mere commodities to be consumed.

The reading group format that AAAAARG originally instantiated has a long history in left-wing and artistic communities. Consciousness-raising groups, study circles, and reading groups have been vehicles of political education and community formation. By creating digital infrastructure for reading groups to share materials, AAAAARG extends this tradition into networked space, enabling connections between geographically dispersed readers who would otherwise remain isolated.

The collections feature exemplifies AAAAARG's social orientation. Users can create thematic groupings of texts — a collection on post-operaismo, speculative realism, feminist geography — that reflect their intellectual interests and guide others toward relevant materials. These collections are curatorial acts that share the curator's perspective, creating paths through the library that others can follow or modify.

### Precarity and persistence

AAAAARG has faced repeated legal challenges that have forced domain changes and threatened the site's very existence. In 2010, publisher Macmillan sent cease-and-desist letters targeting the site. In 2016, a legal action in Quebec forced another migration. An ongoing lawsuit from André Bazin's translator has imposed financial pressure. The site has operated at various URLs, most recently aaaaarg.fail — a domain choice that transforms legal vulnerability into defiant humor. The ".fail" extension acknowledges the possibility of collapse while refusing to capitulate in advance.

This precarity is constitutive of AAAAARG's aesthetics and politics. Unlike official libraries that operate with the blessing of legal and institutional authority, AAAAARG exists in a permanent state of emergency, never certain of its survival. This condition produces a particular relationship to time: the site could disappear tomorrow, so materials must be downloaded, shared, and preserved elsewhere. Users become co-custodians, responsible for maintaining access not only to the site but to the networks of trust and care that enable its persistence.

The contrast with commercial platforms is instructive. When commercial services shut down, their contents often disappear entirely, locked in proprietary formats or deleted from servers. Shadow libraries cultivate practices of redundancy and migration that enable continuity across platforms. AAAAARG's history of domain changes demonstrates that the library is not identical to any particular instantiation but persists through the ongoing efforts of its community. The work, in this sense, is not the website but the social practice of maintenance and reproduction that sustains it.

### The Public School model

The Public School, founded by Dockray in 2007, represents an extension of AAAAARG's principles into educational practice. The platform allows anyone to propose courses on any subject; when enough people express interest, the course is organized. There are no prerequisites, no required credentials, no grades or certificates. Learning happens through collective organization rather than institutional provision.

The Public School has operated in multiple cities — Los Angeles, New York, Brussels, Helsinki, and others — each instance adapting to local conditions while sharing the underlying platform. This federated model creates space for local variation within a shared framework, similar to the distributed architecture of some shadow library projects.

The relationship between AAAAARG and The Public School illustrates how shadow library practice can extend into adjacent domains. Providing access to texts is necessary but insufficient for meaningful engagement with knowledge; texts need contexts, conversations, and communities to become meaningful. The Public School provides these contexts, organizing the social infrastructure that makes textual access educationally productive.

## The networked library — Memory of the World / Public Library

### Marcell Mars and Tomislav Medak: artist-activists

Marcell Mars is a Croatian programmer and artist associated with the Multimedia Institute (mi2) in Zagreb and the hacker collective Calafou in Catalonia. Tomislav Medak is a philosopher and cultural theorist who has written extensively on intellectual property, automation, and knowledge commons. Together, they founded Memory of the World / Public Library in 2012, developing both the theoretical framework and the technical infrastructure for a distributed network of personal libraries.

Their partnership exemplifies the productive combination of technical competence and theoretical sophistication that characterizes the most significant shadow library projects. Mars brings programming expertise and hacker culture sensibilities; Medak brings philosophical rigor and connections to broader critical discourse. Neither dimension alone would suffice — the project's significance lies in their integration.

The name "Memory of the World" invokes the UNESCO program for the preservation of documentary heritage, while "Public Library" asserts the project's connection to the institution that guarantees universal access to knowledge. By adopting these institutionally resonant names, Mars and Medak claim the authority of official knowledge infrastructure while operating outside its sanctioned channels. The gesture is simultaneously appropriative and aspirational: claiming what the public library should be rather than simply describing what it is.

The project's philosophy is encapsulated in a foundational formulation: "A public library is: (1) free access to books for every member of society, (2) a librarian, (3) a library catalog. With books ready to be shared, meticulously cataloged, everyone is a librarian. When everyone is a librarian, the library is everywhere." [24] This slogan asserts that librarianship is not a professional monopoly but a practice open to all; that libraries need not be buildings or institutions but can be distributed across homes and devices; and that universal access requires not massive centralization but distributed participation.

### Distributed architecture as aesthetic choice

Memory of the World's technical architecture reflects its political and aesthetic commitments. Rather than centralizing materials in a single repository, the project consists of a network of personal libraries maintained by individuals using the Let's Share Books plugin for Calibre. Each library is autonomous — its owner decides what to include and how to organize it — while the network creates connections that allow users to discover and access materials across multiple collections.

This distributed architecture differs aesthetically from centralized repositories. Where LibGen presents a unified catalog that obscures the heterogeneity of its sources, Memory of the World foregrounds the individuality of its constituent libraries. Each collection reflects the interests and judgments of the person who maintains it, creating a landscape of distinct voices rather than a homogenized mass.

The network's topology itself becomes a compositional choice, arranging relationships between nodes in ways that have aesthetic as well as technical implications. A distributed network produces different aesthetic effects from a centralized repository: the experience of navigating between personal libraries, encountering different organizational schemes and different emphases, differs fundamentally from searching a unified database. The form of the infrastructure is expressive.

The political implications of distributed architecture are also significant. Centralized repositories create single points of failure: if the server goes down or the domain is seized, access is lost. Distributed networks are more resilient, since no single failure can eliminate the entire system. This resilience is not merely technical but political: it reflects a commitment to the principle that knowledge infrastructure should not depend on a single authority.

The Let's Share Books plugin for Calibre makes this distributed architecture accessible to non-technical users. Calibre, the open-source e-book management software, already enables individuals to organize personal digital libraries; Let's Share Books adds the ability to make these libraries discoverable and accessible to others. The tool transforms isolated collections into network nodes, enabling participation without requiring specialized technical expertise.

### The Pirate Care framework

Mars, Medak, and Valeria Graziano have developed "Pirate Care" as a broader framework connecting shadow library practice to other forms of solidarity. Their 2025 book, *Pirate Care: Acts Against the Criminalization of Solidarity* (Pluto Press) [25], examines practices that provide essential services — health, housing, food, knowledge — outside or against the legal frameworks that restrict them.

The Pirate Care framework positions shadow libraries within a broader ecology of mutual aid practices. Just as volunteers provide medical care to undocumented migrants despite legal restrictions, shadow library operators provide access to knowledge despite copyright restrictions. Both practices prioritize human need over legal compliance; both face criminalization as authorities seek to restrict solidarity.

This framing has strategic implications. By connecting shadow libraries to other care practices, Pirate Care builds alliances across different domains of activism. The refugee aid worker and the server administrator share commitments that might not be obvious; the Pirate Care framework makes these connections visible and actionable.

The framework also emphasizes the labor dimension of shadow library practice. Care work is systematically undervalued in capitalist economies; shadow library maintenance shares this characteristic. The server administrator who keeps libraries running performs care work that is essential, ongoing, and typically unpaid. Recognizing this labor as care connects it to feminist critiques of economic invisibility.

### Exhibition and performance

Memory of the World has been presented in numerous institutional art contexts, including exhibitions at the Württembergischer Kunstverein Stuttgart (2014), the Museo Reina Sofía in Madrid, Transmediale Berlin, the Galerija Nova in Zagreb, the Impakt Festival in Utrecht, and The New School in New York. These presentations raise questions about the relationship between shadow libraries and the art world: what does it mean to exhibit an infrastructure that exists primarily online?

The exhibitions have typically combined documentation, installation, and activation. Visitors could encounter scanning stations for digitizing books, public catalogs displaying available materials, or workshops where participants learn to set up their own local libraries. The exhibition becomes a site of production rather than mere consumption: rather than passively viewing representations of the project, visitors participate in extending its infrastructure.

This performative dimension distinguishes Memory of the World's institutional presentations from conventional documentation-based conceptual art. The work is not completed in advance of the exhibition but is constituted through the activities it enables. Scanning parties, workshops, and collective reading sessions extend the project rather than merely representing it. The exhibition becomes a node in the network, contributing materials and cultivating participants who can continue the practice beyond the institutional framework.

The 2019 presentation at Paper Struggles (Raven Row, London), alongside Eva Weinmayr and Andrea Francke's The Piracy Project, placed Memory of the World within a broader field of practices engaging piracy, copying, and alternative distribution. Such juxtapositions help clarify what distinguishes different approaches: The Piracy Project collects pirated books as artifacts, while Memory of the World distributes digital texts as functional resources. Both engage piracy, but through different strategies and with different effects.

## The local library — Bibliotecha and offline networks

### The Raspberry Pi as artistic medium

Bibliotecha is an open-source framework for creating local, offline digital libraries using the Raspberry Pi single-board computer. Developed collaboratively by artists and programmers including Andre Castro, Roelof Roscam Abbing, and Max Dovey, the project creates wireless networks that allow users to browse and download e-books without internet connectivity.

The Raspberry Pi, a credit card–sized computer originally developed for educational purposes, has become a medium for artistic projects exploring low-tech and DIY approaches to digital infrastructure. Its affordability (approximately $35), energy efficiency, and flexibility make it accessible to artists and communities who might be excluded from more expensive or complex technologies.

Bibliotecha's offline operation represents a political and aesthetic stance against the assumptions of permanent connectivity in contemporary digital culture. The constraint of offline operation becomes a generative limitation, producing forms of sharing that differ from the ubiquitous connectivity of contemporary platforms.

### Situated knowledge and community curation

Donna Haraway's concept of "situated knowledges" emphasizes the partial and positioned character of all knowledge. Bibliotecha instantiates this principle by enabling communities to create collections that reflect their specific interests and needs rather than aspiring to comprehensive coverage.

This situated approach inverts the logic of large shadow libraries. Where LibGen aspires to comprehensive coverage — everything for everyone — Bibliotecha embraces limitation — this, for us.

### Contemporary colportage

The *colporteur* — the itinerant peddler who, from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, carried books, almanacs, and the *bibliothèque bleue* from village to village, his trunk on his back — is a decisive figure in the history of knowledge access in France. Colporteurs were the distribution infrastructures of the Ancien Régime: they circumvented the official channels of the book trade, circulated forbidden texts and Protestant bibles in the false bottoms of barrels, and made print accessible to populations served by neither urban booksellers nor institutional libraries. In 1757, a royal edict punished them with death for clandestine distribution of books without privilege — a criminalization of access that finds its echoes in contemporary prosecutions of shadow library administrators. What Daniel Roche called "peddlers of culture" were also, structurally, pirates: they distributed without authorization, outside legitimate channels, to publics deemed unworthy of reading.

Several contemporary projects explicitly reactivate this figure. Gaëlle Clarck and Joëlle Slacmeulder's TOUK TOUK BOOK TOUR transports curatorial collections of books from place to place, reconstituting the colporteur's gesture at the scale of a cultural network. Leo Leyens's Pl4tform radicalizes the principle by distributing digital libraries via servers carried in a backpack — the library literally borne on the body through urban and rural spaces, like the trunks of Alpine colporteurs who covered twenty kilometers a day. In both cases, the infrastructure is inseparable from the body that moves it: the server only functions where the bearer stands, the collection exists only in the encounter.

These itinerant practices suggest that shadow libraries need not be fixed infrastructures. They can take mobile, temporary, and situated forms — and it is precisely this mobility that makes them resistant to seizure, just as the colporteurs of the Ancien Régime escaped controls by moving between jurisdictions.

The federated model of the CHATONS network (Collectif des Hébergeurs Alternatifs, Transparents, Ouverts, Neutres et Solidaires), initiated by Framasoft, extends this logic to the scale of digital infrastructure. The CHATONS charter articulates principles that connect to shadow library values: transparency in data handling, respect for privacy, non-commercial orientation, and commitment to popular education. Each member operates independently, choosing which services to offer and how to organize, while the network creates connections and mutual support — a counter-geography of servers and communities that doubles the dominant platforms. This model offers direct lessons: rather than one massive library serving everyone, a network of local libraries could serve specific communities with materials relevant to their needs.

With the sections that follow, we change scale. The artistic projects, associative servers, and portable libraries examined so far operate at human dimensions — a few thousand titles, communities of a few hundred people, curatorial collections where each entry results from a deliberate choice. Monoskop, Sci-Hub, Library Genesis, and Z-Library belong to a different order of magnitude: millions of titles, tens of millions of users, technical architectures capable of withstanding judicial seizures and state blockades. The aesthetic question does not disappear, however — it shifts. It is no longer the curator's gesture that constitutes the work, but the very form of the infrastructure: its protocols, its interfaces, its strategies of resilience.

## The scholarly library — Monoskop and Sci-Hub

### Dušan Barok's wiki practice

Dušan Barok founded Monoskop in 2004 as a wiki documenting media art in Bratislava, Slovakia. Over the following decades, the site expanded to encompass arts, media, and humanities research more broadly, combining collaborative wiki-style documentation with a digital library of full-text resources.

The wiki format distinguishes Monoskop from other shadow libraries. Rather than simply hosting files, Monoskop embeds texts within a network of contextual information: artist biographies, institutional histories, bibliographies, and thematic articles. The library is not a mere repository but a knowledge environment. Monoskop's distinctive aesthetics lie in this integration: each PDF is embedded in a wiki page that situates it historically, geographically, and intellectually. The act of downloading is never isolated — it is always preceded and accompanied by an act of contextual reading. In this sense, Monoskop is the most explicitly pedagogical shadow library: it does not merely distribute texts but models a way of approaching them. The rigor of its cataloging — each entry verified, each link maintained — constitutes a form of care that belongs as much to curatorial practice as to database administration.

Barok's 2025 publication, *Careful: A Repertorium on Shadow Library Practice* [29], co-written with Marcell Mars, Tomislav Medak, and Nick Thurston, consolidates years of theoretical and practical engagement with shadow libraries. The very title — *Careful* — echoes the ethics of care that traverses the entire field.

### Alexandra Elbakyan and Sci-Hub

Alexandra Elbakyan, a Kazakh computer scientist, founded Sci-Hub in 2011 while a master's student working on brain-computer interfaces. As John Bohannon reported in *Science*: "Publisher paywalls are the bane of scientists and students in Kazakhstan. [...] What was needed, she decided, was a system that allowed that paper to be shared — with absolutely everyone. She had the computer skills — and contacts with other pirate websites — to make that happen, and so Sci-Hub was born." [26]

Elbakyan defended her project in terms that invert the legal framing: "The project is legal, while restricting access to information and knowledge is not. The current operation of the academic publishing industry is a massive violation of human rights." [27] The scale of Sci-Hub's impact is staggering: "Over the 6 months leading up to March [2016], Sci-Hub served up 28 million documents. More than 2.6 million download requests came from Iran, 3.4 million from India, and 4.4 million from China." [28]

In 2016, *Nature* magazine named Elbakyan among the ten most influential people in science. In 2023, the Electronic Frontier Foundation awarded Elbakyan a prize for her contributions to knowledge access.

### Anna's Archive: the meta-library

Anna's Archive, launched in 2022 by an anonymous figure known as "Anna," represents a new phase in shadow library development: the aggregation of shadow libraries themselves. In October 2023, Anna's Archive completed a harvest of WorldCat, the world's largest library catalog, adding metadata for over 700 million records.

Anna's Archive exemplifies the preservation activism that has intensified in the wake of platform seizures. When Z-Library's domains were seized by US authorities in November 2022, Anna's Archive's aggregation ensured that materials remained accessible.

# INTERLUDE — Political economy and legal frameworks

To understand shadow libraries, one must understand the system they double. Academic publishing has concentrated considerably over the past three decades: five companies — Elsevier, Wiley, Springer Nature, Taylor & Francis, and SAGE — control a majority of the scholarly journal market, with profit margins exceeding 30%, generated primarily by acquiring copyright on publicly funded research and reselling access through institutional subscriptions. The model creates artificial scarcity for inherently non-rival digital goods: once an article exists, its distribution costs essentially nothing, but publishers charge thousands of dollars for institutional access and thirty dollars or more for individual articles. As the George Washington University library put it: "Scholarly resources are not luxury goods. But they are priced as though they were." [30] The "serials crisis," which began in the 1990s, forced libraries to cut subscriptions and reduce other services, producing a two-tier system in which well-funded institutions maintain broad access while others lose access to essential literature.

The open access movement has contested this model through several avenues — "gold" open access (authors or funders pay publication fees), "green" (repository deposit), or "diamond" (neither authors nor readers pay). But the transformation remains partial: gold can transfer costs from readers to authors, excluding researchers without funding; green depends on author compliance and faces embargo restrictions; diamond requires sustained institutional support. The majority of current research remains behind paywalls, and even optimistic projections place universal open access decades away. In the meantime, shadow libraries provide de facto open access outside official channels. The geographic data is revealing: in 2016, a study by John Bohannon in *Science* [31] showed that the United States was the fifth-largest user of Sci-Hub after Russia, and that a quarter of requests came from OECD countries — the wealthiest nations, supposedly with the best access [32]. Even researchers with institutional access prefer the convenience: the multiple clicks required to verify access rights through the university library cannot compete with the immediacy of a DOI entered into Sci-Hub.

This system is buttressed by international treaties establishing minimum standards of protection. The Berne Convention (1886) establishes automatic copyright protection without registration; the TRIPS Agreement (1995) integrates intellectual property into the World Trade Organization framework with enforcement mechanisms including trade sanctions; the WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996) extends reproduction rights to digital copies and prohibits the circumvention of technological protection measures. National implementations vary considerably: US law offers fair use and its four-factor test but also the DMCA prohibiting technical circumvention; the EU Directive of 2019 harmonizes certain aspects while leaving others to national transposition; Russia's relatively weak copyright enforcement long allowed Library Genesis to operate, though legal frameworks have tightened.

Judicial proceedings illustrate both the legal vulnerability of shadow libraries and their resilience. Elsevier brought suit against Sci-Hub and Library Genesis in a US federal court in 2015, obtaining default judgments against defendants who did not appear — without, however, interrupting operations. As Peter Suber observed: "A lawsuit isn't going to stop Sci-Hub, nor is there any obvious technical means. Everyone should be thinking about the fact that this is here to stay." [33] Elbakyan herself framed the stakes in the starkest terms: "If Elsevier manages to shut down our projects or force them into the darknet, that will demonstrate an important idea: that the public does not have the right to knowledge." [34] In 2022, US authorities seized more than 240 Z-Library domains and arrested its alleged operators in Argentina, marking an escalation toward criminal prosecution. In 2024, a court ordered $30 million in damages against Library Genesis, though collection remains unlikely given the operators' jurisdictional distance. These cases demonstrate a constant: law enforcement disrupts but does not eliminate access, just as royal edicts against colporteurs never stopped the circulation of forbidden books.

## IV. Care

## Feminist servers and situated infrastructure

### Constant and the Feminist Server Manifesto

Constant, the Brussels-based nonprofit for art and media founded in 1997, has played a central role in developing feminist approaches to digital infrastructure. Femke Snelting, a major figure at Constant and signatory of the "In Solidarity with Library Genesis and Sci-Hub" letter [37], embodies the continuity between shadow library practice and that of feminist servers. Laurence Rassel, who forged her practice there at the intersection of cyberfeminism, free software, and self-education, contributed decisively to this orientation — work she continues today as director of the École de Recherche Graphique (ERG) in Brussels, where open source principles inform a pedagogy founded on the collective and on transmission. Working at the intersection of art, feminism, and free software, Constant produces projects, publications, and events that explore how technology can support rather than undermine feminist values.

The 2013 workshop "Are You Being Served?" brought practitioners together to reflect on what a feminist approach to server infrastructure might mean. The resulting manifesto articulates several principles that have durably influenced shadow library practice. The first is ecological: a feminist server is not an isolated technical system but an element caught within a network of relationships — to the people who use it, to other servers, to the material and social environments that sustain it. The second is situatedness: rather than claiming universality, a feminist server makes its political choices explicit and acknowledges its particular context — a server in Brussels does not operate under the same legal conditions, surveillance regimes, or community relationships as a server in Nairobi. The third concerns the balance between care and security: the point is not to succumb to paranoia but nor to neglect protection, which characterizes a reflective practice of infrastructure. The fourth, finally, concerns transparency: feminist servers strive to be comprehensible to those who use them, in contrast to the opacity that characterizes commercial platforms.

These principles provide a framework for understanding shadow libraries as aesthetic practices embedded in social relations rather than as autonomous technical systems.

### ATNOFS: a traversal network

"A Traversal Network of Feminist Servers" (ATNOFS), developed from 2022, extends feminist server practice into a reticular form. The project creates connections between feminist server operators across geographic locations, enabling knowledge sharing, mutual support, and collective reflection.

ATNOFS has produced events, publications, and a traveling server named "rosa" that visits different locations, materializing the network through physical presence. The publication *Infrables* documents ATNOFS activities and theorizations, making visible the conceptual work that accompanies technical practice.

The combination of material infrastructure (servers, networks) and discursive production (publications, workshops) reflects the understanding that feminist technological practice requires ongoing reflection and articulation. Mara Karagianni, a practitioner involved in ATNOFS, conducts server administration workshops that combine technical instruction with political discussion. Participants learn to install software while reflecting on the power relations inscribed in technical choices.

### Varia and collaborative practice

Varia, based in Rotterdam, is a collective of artists, designers, and programmers developing collaborative digital practices. The collective hosts the documentation for Bibliotecha and has developed related projects exploring local, offline, and peer-to-peer networks.

The publication *Networks of One's Own 2: Three Takes on Taking Care* (2019) [36] explores themes of maintenance, care, and infrastructure through artistic and theoretical contributions. The reference to Virginia Woolf in the title (*A Room of One's Own*) positions network infrastructure as a feminist concern, connecting digital practice to longer histories of women's struggles for creative space and autonomy.

Varia's location in Rotterdam positions it within a distinct cultural and linguistic context while maintaining connections with Brussels, Berlin, and other nodes in the European network of feminist technological practice. This distributed geography creates a landscape of related but distinct practices, each shaped by local context while participating in shared conversations.

## Pedagogy and transmission

### Teaching shadow libraries

Shadow library practice depends on the transmission of knowledge: teaching people to install servers, digitize books, manage metadata, and navigate the legal and ethical complexities of alternative distribution. This pedagogical dimension extends the work into educational practice, making teaching itself a component of the project.

The skills taught in shadow library workshops have applications beyond shadow library practice itself. Learning to set up a YunoHost server or manage a Calibre library produces transferable competencies in self-hosting, privacy protection, and digital autonomy. The workshop thus functions as an introduction to broader practices of technological self-determination.

Workshops and "install parties" — events where participants learn together to install software — have become characteristic pedagogical forms. The format combines technical instruction with community building: participants acquire skills while also building relationships with others who share their interests. The workshop becomes a site of encounter, producing not only technical knowledge but also social networks that can sustain ongoing practice.

The install party format positions learning as pleasure rather than obligation. Participants bring their devices, help each other, and celebrate successful installations. Mutual aid — participants teaching each other rather than depending solely on expert instructors — distributes pedagogical authority and develops the capacity to teach alongside technical competence.

### Art schools and experimental publishing

Francophone art and design schools have become important sites for shadow library–adjacent practices. ÉSAD Grenoble-Valence, through its "Biennale Exemplaires" and its network of partner schools (including institutions in Lyon, Rennes, Strasbourg, Toulouse, Brussels, Geneva, Le Havre, and Metz), has fostered experimental publishing that explores the territory between official and alternative distribution.

These institutional contexts provide resources — time, space, equipment, expertise — that support experimentation. Students can explore shadow library practice within the framework of their education, developing projects that would be impossible to sustain independently. Faculty research, such as "Les Nouveaux Habits du Colportage," receives institutional support that enables sustained investigation.

The relationship between art schools and shadow libraries is complex. Schools that train students for careers in creative industries may be ambivalent about practices that challenge intellectual property frameworks. Yet the pedagogical imperative to provide students with access to materials often produces a pragmatic accommodation: teachers sharing PDFs, libraries maintaining informal collections, a tacit acceptance of practices that official policy might prohibit.

### Beyond the academy: autodidactic communities

Shadow libraries support autodidactic practice — learning outside formal educational institutions. The Public School, Sean Dockray's platform for organizing free courses, exemplifies this autodidactic orientation. Users propose courses on any subject, and others sign up to participate; when enough interest accumulates, the course takes place. The platform creates a learning infrastructure that bypasses institutional gatekeeping.

The "Pirate Care" project extends this framework through its syllabus for self-organized study groups. The syllabus provides reading materials organized around themes of care, solidarity, and resistance. Students — self-organized or formally enrolled — work through the readings together, discussing, debating, and applying what they learn. The syllabus format makes shadow library materials educationally actionable, guiding students through an otherwise overwhelming abundance.

These autodidactic practices challenge the monopoly of accredited institutions over the production and certification of knowledge. If anyone can learn anything using freely available materials and collectively organized instruction, what is the function of universities and schools? Shadow libraries do not answer this question, but they pose it, demonstrating alternatives that make visible as choices the dominant system's claims to necessity.

## V. CONCLUSION {#conclusion}

In 1959, the Xerox Corporation introduced the Model 914, the first automatic photocopier accessible to the general public. In under seven seconds, the machine produced a copy of any printed document. The effect was immediate and massive: in the United States, the number of annual copies rose from 20 million at the beginning of the decade to 14 billion in 1966 [54]. The publishers' reaction was swift. Scholarly journals, whose business model rested on the controlled scarcity of the subscription, saw in the photocopier a machine for destroying intellectual property. The legal scholar Melville Nimmer, professor at UCLA, predicted that "the day may not be far off when no one need purchase a book." The case of *Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States* (1973) crystallized the conflict: the medical journal publisher sued the National Library of Medicine and the National Institutes of Health for large-scale photocopying of articles — 85,744 requests processed in 1970 alone, amounting to nearly one million pages [55]. The Court of Claims ruled in the libraries' favor, invoking fair use. The Supreme Court, on appeal in 1975, affirmed by an equally divided vote (4-4) — a "non-decision" that left the question open and precipitated the revision of the Copyright Act of 1976 [56].

The episode is instructive not for its legal outcome but for its structure. Every reproduction technology — the printing press, colportage, the photocopier, the digital file — provokes the same sequence: panic among rights holders, test cases, legislative adjustment, normalization of use. Publishers in the 1960s spoke of the photocopier exactly as those of the 2010s speak of Sci-Hub: an instrument of piracy that threatens to "destroy publishing." And in both cases, actual use is above all scholarly. Mars and Medak put it precisely: "The 'knowledge society' three decades ago would not have been conceivable without the illegal sharing of knowledge among students, academics, and researchers the world over." [15] Downloading PDFs from LibGen or Sci-Hub is not counterfeiting in the ordinary sense — it is not the commercial reproduction of a competing good. It is the ordinary practice of contemporary research, that of millions of scholars, students, and readers doing what their predecessors did at the photocopier: accessing a text they need, now.

This scholarly use does not exhaust the significance of shadow libraries. As this essay has sought to show, they participate in a broader ecology of alternative education and self-organization projects with a long history: activist reading groups, popular universities, punk zine libraries, scanning parties, feminist servers, digital colportage workshops. In each of these cases, the library is not a mere tool of dissemination but a device of *empowerment* linked to the collective mastery of reproduction technologies. Learning to scan, to catalog, to maintain a server, to organize a syllabus from shared resources — these technical gestures are also pedagogical and political gestures. The Pirate Care Syllabus of Mars, Medak, and Graziano [25], Constant's workshops in Brussels [35], de Valk's XPUB program at the Piet Zwart Institute [47], the digital colporteurs of TOUK TOUK BOOK TOUR and Pl4tform are all variations on a single intuition: the circulation of knowledge is inseparable from the social forms that organize it, and these forms cannot be delegated to commercial platforms without loss.

Yet it is precisely this intuition that the most recent developments have thrown into crisis. Since 2023, lawsuits filed by authors against artificial intelligence companies have revealed that the training corpora of large language models — OpenAI's ChatGPT, Meta's LLaMA, Nvidia's NeMo, Anthropic's Claude — rely massively on shadow library collections [57]. The Books3 dataset, composed of nearly 197,000 pirated books extracted from the shadow library Bibliotik, served as training material for multiple models before being removed from the Hugging Face platform in October 2023. Court documents filed in *Bartz v. Anthropic* reveal that Anthropic downloaded at least 5 million copies from Library Genesis and 2 million from the Pirate Library Mirror [58]. Internal communications at Meta show that employees were aware that mass downloading from LibGen violated copyright — one of them explaining to a colleague that he used servers rented from Amazon rather than company servers to "avoid the risk of traceability" [59]. At Nvidia, internal emails from August 2023 — revealed by the amended complaint of January 2026 — document direct contacts with Anna's Archive to acquire approximately 500 terabytes of pirated data, despite the shadow library's explicit warning about the illegal nature of its collections [60]. The settlement of the Anthropic case, in September 2025, amounted to $1.5 billion — approximately $3,000 per work, a sum that subsequent plaintiffs consider derisory relative to a global artificial intelligence market estimated at $391 billion in 2025 by Grand View Research, and projected by UNCTAD to reach $4.8 trillion by 2033 [61].

The reversal is dizzying. Shadow libraries constituted themselves as knowledge commons, maintained by the volunteer labor of *folk archivists* driven by an ethos of *custodianship* and *pirate care* — care for the circulation of texts despite legal restrictions. AI companies treated these commons as deposits of raw material, extractable without compensation or even acknowledgment. What was *folk archiving* became *training data*. What was a collective practice of counter-enclosure became a free resource for the most capitalized industry in recent economic history. The arc from scholarly use to collective *empowerment* to neoliberal knowledge extractivism traces a trajectory that shadow library practitioners did not anticipate — and that the ongoing lawsuits are only beginning to make visible.

It is in this space that art has something to do. Not to celebrate shadow libraries as finished works — this essay has insisted enough on the risks of aestheticization — but to make perceptible the operations that transform a commons into a commodity. The artists who work with and on shadow libraries — from Kenneth Goldsmith to Marcell Mars, from Constant to the digital colporteurs, from Theaster Gates to Andrea Francke — do not produce aesthetic objects in the conventional sense. They make infrastructures visible, name practices, create spaces where the question of knowledge circulation can be posed otherwise than in the terms of property law or platform capitalism. Making visible that the training of language models relies on the invisibilized labor of thousands of *folk archivists* — this is a critical gesture that only art, with its capacity to short-circuit between registers, can accomplish with the required efficacy.

The stakes are not nostalgic. It is not a matter of returning to a prior state of shadow libraries, before their capture by industry. It is a matter of reactivating the imaginary of the commons that gave rise to them, confronting it with the material conditions that are now its own. The words are already there: *custodianship*, *counter-enclosure*, *studious publics*, *pirate care*. The practices too: scanning, cataloging, sharing, maintaining, teaching. What is missing is the articulation between these practices and the awareness of the extraction to which they are subjected. That is exactly the work of art — not as decoration of struggles, but as an instrument of thought capable of holding together the joy of sharing and the anger of dispossession.
