## Introduction — The War of Imaginaries

> "Education, I argue, is not a 'stilling in' but a 'leading out', which opens paths of intellectual growth and discovery without predetermined outcomes or fixed end-points. It is about attending to things, rather than acquiring the knowledge that absolves us of the need to do so; about *exposure* rather than *immunisation*." [^1]

[^1]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, Abingdon, Routledge, 2018, p. ix.

Teaching art means confronting a contradiction: transmitting something that, for a century now, has defined itself by putting every transmissible norm in crisis. Since Duchamp, contemporary art can no longer be confined within rules, canons, or stabilized techniques. There is no longer a corpus to transmit, no method to apply. Art has become that which questions the very possibility of art.

This contradiction was long able to remain latent. For a century and a half, art schools operated on an implicit contract: the "master" holds an embodied knowledge — technical at the Academy, formal at the Bauhaus, ideological at the VKhUTEMAS — that is transmitted through proximity rather than through explication. James Elkins has shown that this opacity is not an accident but a structure: since no one knows exactly what art is or how one becomes an artist, the teacher who has "succeeded" possesses a secret — a personal alchemy — that the student can only hope to capture through imitation [^2]. Talent, it was said, cannot be taught; it reveals itself. This mythology suited everyone: it protected the teacher's authority and spared the institution from being held to account.

[^2]: Cf. James ELKINS, *Why Art Cannot Be Taught: A Handbook for Art Students*, Urbana, University of Illinois Press, 2001, esp. ch. 1–3.

This contract is now broken on both sides. On one side, the Bologna process has compelled art schools to make explicit what, in their teaching, qualifies as "research" — a condition for access to doctoral-level programs. The opacity of the "master" becomes an institutional handicap: one must now render accounts, publish, document. On the other side, generative artificial intelligences produce images, texts, and analyses with an efficiency that rivals the traditional function of the teacher. The machine appears to know more, faster, without fatigue. What remains for the human teacher? For the risk, as Gert Biesta reminds us, is constitutive of education itself: it "is there because education is not an interaction between robots but an encounter between human beings. [...] Students are not to be seen as objects to be molded and disciplined, but as subjects of action and responsibility." [^3]

[^3]: Gert J.J. BIESTA, *The Beautiful Risk of Education*, Boulder, Paradigm Publishers, 2013, p. 1.

We are therefore no longer simply facing a crisis of transmission, but a war of imaginaries. Hannah Arendt had already named the malaise: the "crisis of transmission" designates the rupture of the intergenerational bond through which the "old" introduce the "new" into a pre-existing common world. Yet with technological acceleration, the world of the "old" (teachers) appears obsolete in the eyes of the "new" (students born into the digital age). AI deepens this gap: the student has the impression that ChatGPT "knows" more than their professor. Ivan Illich had anticipated this confusion as early as 1971: the student is "*schooled to confuse teaching with learning*, grade advancement with education, a diploma with competence, and fluency with the ability to say something new." [^4] AI inherits this confusion and amplifies it: it conflates information with knowledge, linguistic fluency with thought.

[^4]: Ivan ILLICH, *Deschooling Society*, New York, Harper & Row, 1971, ch. 1.

But the problem runs deeper: AI does not merely move fast — it steals the future by projecting biased pasts forward. Predictive algorithms close down the future by calculating the probable; art education must reopen the possible. The decisive question is no longer "what does the teacher transmit?" but "what is the teacher, and what does the teacher put at stake?"

The thesis of this text can be stated in a single sentence: the artist-researcher who teaches must be visible at work. They must show their research in progress — the gropings, the revisions, the dead ends — and share it as common material. Their legitimacy no longer resides in what they know, but in what they do under the gaze of others and what they build with them. In this context, the teacher can no longer be a mere transmitter. They must become, in Thomas Hirschhorn's terms, a "beauty warrior" engaged in continuous "presence and production," or, in Rasheedah Phillips's terms, a "chrononaut" capable of deprogramming imposed futures.

This demand for visibility of work in progress radically distinguishes the exposed teacher from the traditional "master." The "master" showed results; the exposed teacher shows a process. The "master" taught from a position of finished knowledge; the exposed teacher teaches from a position of research in action. The "master" stood above; the exposed teacher works in the midst of students — visible, vulnerable, verifiable.

This proposition rests on a single opposition: the "old master" (I deliberately retain the masculine) treats knowledge as a possession (which he holds and distributes); the exposed teacher treats experience as a resource (which they contribute to the commons and elaborate with others). This distinction will run throughout the text — but it must be handled with caution. The term "resource" is not innocent: it also belongs to the vocabulary of management ("human resources") and can serve a logic of exploitation. The challenge will be to conceive of a resource that is not a commodity — a commons that resists appropriation. The model of the Indonesian *lumbung* — the communal rice barn — offers a concrete alternative to this loaded vocabulary.

The most radical contemporary experiments show that equality is wrested through friction, chaos, and the intensity of conflict. The stake is not only to be equal before knowledge, but to constitute an assembly — a political entity capable of enduring and producing worlds. The exposed teacher does not seek perfect symmetry, which remains a liberal utopia; they seek to hold the space open so that assembly can take place.

A note on the form of this text. It would be contradictory to defend the visibility of work in a text that presented itself as a closed, finished, indisputable system. This text is itself a working tool — a proposition to be discussed, criticized, transformed. It cites its sources not as definitive authorities but as companions on the road, interlocutors with whom to think. It acknowledges its biases, its simplifications, its blind spots — and invites the reader to identify them, to contest them, to propose alternatives. In this sense, the form of the text attempts to mimic its content: exposing a thought in progress rather than a finished knowledge. It is a performative writing — one that does what it says — with all the risks that entails. Eli Meyerhoff has named the enemy: "the romance of education," that construction by which "education has been presented as the best and only option for study," to the point of making "the possibilities of alternative modes of study [...] almost unthinkable." [^5] The exposed teacher works against this romance.

[^5]: Eli MEYERHOFF, *Beyond Education: Radical Studying for Another World*, Minneapolis, University of Minnesota Press, 2019, p. 4–5.

## I. From Inherited Asymmetry to the Pursuit of Assembly

The history of art education can be read as a succession of attempts to resolve a problem that never stops reformulating itself: how to transmit what, by definition, resists transmission? Each era has proposed an answer — and each answer has stumbled on its own contradictions. A single question will serve as our guiding thread: does the teacher show their work in progress? This question is the decisive test of pedagogical asymmetry. The "master" who hides their process maintains an unbridgeable gap; the one who exposes it opens the possibility of verification — and therefore of equality. But this equality is not enough: it must still crystallize into a form — an assembly, a dispositif, an infrastructure capable of making it last. A methodological caveat is in order. Every genealogy risks teleology: reconstructing the past to make the present appear as its necessary outcome. The panorama that follows does not entirely escape this risk. It selects, from historical complexity, the elements that prefigure the final proposition. The reader is invited to keep this reservation in mind: the pedagogical regimes described here are ideal types, not exhaustive descriptions.

### Archaeology of Pedagogical Vocabulary

Vocabulary retains the trace of asymmetries. The "master" (from the Latin *magister*, the one who is "greater") embodies a knowledge possessed and completed. The apprentice is defined by their lack; the disciple by their deference. The pupil (from the Latin *elevare*, "to raise") is the one pulled upward — toward a position they never quite reach. The professor (the one who "declares publicly") "professes" a unidirectional knowledge: they speak, the other listens. Other terms open other possibilities. The student (from the Latin *studere*, "to apply oneself") is defined by an activity — study — that can be shared. The researcher supposes an investigation whose outcome is not known in advance. The peer designates someone equal. The assembly (from the Latin *assimulare*, "to bring together") supposes a plurality that constitutes itself as a political body. These terms make it possible to think a horizontality that the older terms excluded by definition. Masschelein and Simons remind us that this horizontality is inscribed in the very origin of the school: "From its inception in the Greek city states, school time has been time in which 'capital' (knowledge, skills, culture) is *expropriated*, released as a 'common good' for public use, thus existing independent of talent, ability or income." [^6]

[^6]: Jan MASSCHELEIN and Maarten SIMONS, *In Defence of the School: A Public Issue*, Leuven, E-ducation, Culture & Society Publishers, 2013, p. 16–17.

### The Academic Regime: Three Centuries of Possessed Rules

The Royal Academy of Painting and Sculpture, founded in Paris in 1648, established the first formal regime of art education. Its postulate: art obeys rational rules. The proportions of the human body, the laws of perspective, the hierarchy of genres — all of this could be formulated, taught, applied. The "master" possesses these rules; the apprentice is ignorant of them and must learn them. It would, however, be reductive to present the Academy as a site of silent, dogmatic transmission. The Academy's "Conférences," where the king's paintings were publicly analyzed, constituted a space for debate — admittedly framed, but real. The quarrel of colorists versus draughtsmen, which pitted the Poussinists against the Rubenists in the late seventeenth century, demonstrates that academic knowledge was not a monolithic bloc. Academic asymmetry rested less on technical secrecy than on the rhetorical mastery of aesthetic justification: the "master" was the one who knew how to argue the rule, not merely apply it. Evaluation remained one-directional. The pedagogical progression ran from simple to complex: copying engravings, drawing from antique casts, then from the live model, and finally, composition. The Prix de Rome crowned the one who best applied the criteria. The master, for his part, was never evaluated — at least not within the pedagogical framework. His status attested that he had already satisfied the criteria. Knowledge was embodied in the person of the "master" — it was inseparable from their physical presence, their gaze, their judgment. This embodiment produced a structure of dependence: the "master" possessed what the apprentice desired, and this possession could not be shared without being lost. What the academic "master" never did: show their own work in progress. They taught from the finished work — their own or that of the old masters. The process remained invisible; only the result was given to be seen. The apprentice had to guess, by observing finished works, what the "master" had gone through to arrive there. This regime would dominate European art education for nearly three centuries.

### The Collapse of Academicism and the Birth of Laboratories

But at the turn of the twentieth century, a double crisis weakened the academic edifice. An aesthetic crisis first: Impressionism (1870s) contested the hierarchy of genres; Post-Impressionism (1880s–1900s) dynamited perspective; the avant-gardes — Cubism, Futurism, Expressionism, Abstraction (1905–1914) — rejected the very idea of universal rules. Young artists no longer wanted to learn to draw "like the old masters"; they wanted to invent new languages.

The Academy, guardian of the rules, became the enemy to be destroyed. Then a political crisis: the First World War discredited the institutions that had trained the officers and engineers of industrial slaughter. European "civilization" had committed suicide in the trenches; why continue teaching according to its norms? The war also produced ruins — material and symbolic — upon which one had to rebuild. Art was no longer an ornament; it became a vital necessity. It was in this context of ruin and reconstruction that two pedagogical laboratories were born, almost simultaneously, each attempting to refound art education on radically new bases: the Bauhaus in Weimar (1919) and the VKhUTEMAS in Moscow (1920). They shared a common rejection of academicism, a faith in industry and technology, an ambition to transform everyday life through design. But they diverged on a decisive point: the nature of the authority that should replace that of the academic "master."

### The Laboratories of the Interwar Period

The Bauhaus (1919–1933) enacted a decisive break with academicism. The *Vorkurs* — the preliminary course successively designed by Itten, Moholy-Nagy, and Albers — freed the student from academic imitation: one no longer copied models but experimented with the elementary properties of form, color, and materials. But the teacher remained a *Meister*. The title was not a lexical accident: Itten led breathing and gymnastic exercises whose dramaturgy he controlled; Albers devised formal problems whose internal logic he knew in advance. Discovery was programmed — the student explored, but within a territory the "master" had already mapped. Moholy-Nagy, in *Vision in Motion* (1947), formulated a decisive intuition — "Teaching is not a profession but an attitude" — but his own practice remained that of a designer-engineer who projected forms onto matter rather than searching with it.

Teaching was no longer the transmission of content but a disposition toward investigation. Something decisive was introduced: the idea of a commons. Tools, materials, discoveries began to be shared. Yet asymmetry persisted in a new form. The *Meister* — Kandinsky, Klee, Moholy-Nagy — taught from a position of accomplished mastery. They had already discovered the laws of visual language; the students retraced this path under their direction. Johannes Itten, with his mysticism, or Kandinsky, with his theory of correspondences between forms and colors, did not so much invite a co-construction of knowledge as a conversion of the student to a perceptual truth deemed universal. The universality of modernist visual language functioned as a paradoxical injunction: "Be free, but according to the objective laws of form." This pedagogy of the *tabula rasa* demanded that the student strip away their prior cultural baggage to access the purity of formal language. "Discovery" was often a guided recognition of what the "master" had already found. The commons of means did not extend to a commons of evaluation.

What the modernist "master" did not show: their research in progress. Kandinsky did not write *Concerning the Spiritual in Art* in front of his students; he arrived with a finished theory. The process of discovery remained private; only the doctrine was made public. Asymmetry had changed in content (from academic rules to laws of form), but not in structure.

In Moscow, the VKhUTEMAS (Higher Art and Technical Studios, 1920–1930) proposed a radicalization of the Bauhaus project in the context of the Soviet revolution. El Lissitzky shuttled between Moscow and Berlin; Moholy-Nagy drew inspiration from Constructivism. The two experiments were in dialogue — but their political contexts diverged, and this divergence produced two different conceptions of pedagogical horizontality. Productivist Constructivism, theorized by Boris Arvatov and Alexei Gan, refused the distinction between artist and engineer, between creation and production. The artist became a "constructor" whose task was to organize the material life of the masses. Within this framework, the teacher/student distinction tended to dissolve in favor of the category of "producer." Alexander Rodchenko and Varvara Stepanova conceived the studio as a site of collective production where academic hierarchies were abolished. This abolition was, however, largely rhetorical. Asymmetry shifted from the aesthetic register to the ideological one. The revolutionary "cadre" who guided production maintained a position of authority that escaped any contestation — since it was grounded not in artistic knowledge but in political consciousness. To criticize the "master" would amount to criticizing the revolution itself. Aesthetic authority was replaced by a political authority equally vertical. What the two laboratories had in common: in both cases, the "master" did not show their work in progress. They taught from a position of certainty — aesthetic certainty at the Bauhaus, political certainty at the VKhUTEMAS. Doubt, groping, failure remained private. And in both cases, a vertical authority (the formal universal, revolutionary ideology) replaced academic authority without eliminating it. The VKhUTEMAS and the Bauhaus illustrate a danger that threatens any attempt to move beyond pedagogical asymmetry: the displacement of authority rather than its transformation. The exposed teacher must guard against replacing the authority of knowledge with another form of authority — moral, political, procedural, or technological — equally uncontestable.

### Black Mountain College: The Legacy and Its Limits

In 1933, the year the Bauhaus closed under Nazi pressure, another experiment began on the other side of the Atlantic. Josef and Anni Albers, forced into exile, took a boat to America. The couple joined Black Mountain College, founded that same year by John Andrew Rice in the Appalachian mountains of North Carolina. Josef Albers brought with him the legacy of the Bauhaus — but in a radically different context: no totalitarian state to serve or flee, no revolution to accomplish, no industry to transform. Only a small rural community, isolated, experimental. Black Mountain College (1933–1957) represents the most radical attempt to challenge pedagogical asymmetry. For the school, as Masschelein and Simons put it, is above all "a society that provides time and space to renew itself, thus offering itself up in all its vulnerability." [^7] BMC took this vulnerability seriously.

[^7]: Jan MASSCHELEIN and Maarten SIMONS, *In Defence of the School*, op. cit., p. 10–11.

The college functioned without grades, without formal diplomas, without separate departments. Art was not one discipline among others but the organizing principle of the entire community life. Decisions were made collectively; teachers and students shared domestic tasks; the boundary between learning and living blurred. M.C. Richards testified: "We were all students, all teachers. The question was not who knows, but what are we discovering together." John Cage recounted that his first courses on chance and indeterminacy were explorations for himself as much as for the participants. The formulation goes beyond simple reciprocity: it designates a shared state of investigation. It is here that the idea of the teacher who searches in front of and with students finds a particularly explicit formulation — and a documented practice. Cage did not merely come to teach a theory of chance; he came to experiment with it, in real time, with the participants. The research was visible; the process was shared. Black Mountain realized — partially, provisionally, and unevenly depending on the teacher — what the Bauhaus and the VKhUTEMAS had promised without fulfilling.

But the absence of formal structure did not guarantee equality. Black Mountain was shot through with authority conflicts — tensions between Albers (who remained a *Meister* at heart) and the younger members, forced departures of certain teachers, informal power struggles. Evaluation existed in fact; it had simply become informal, diffuse, potentially more arbitrary. Jo Freeman, in her essay "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1970), showed that in groups without official hierarchy, informal elites inevitably reconstitute themselves, based on charisma, friendship, or cultural capital — and that these hierarchies are all the more tyrannical for being unavowed and therefore uncriticizable. Black Mountain illustrates this paradox: the abolition of formal structures does not eliminate power; it makes power invisible and therefore uncontrollable. What Black Mountain lacked — or more precisely, what failed to hold — were explicit dispositifs capable of guaranteeing equality over time. For there were dispositifs. John Andrew Rice, the college's founder, had been shaped by Dewey's thought; he believed in participatory democracy and had established community meetings where teachers and students decided together.

Paul Goodman, who taught there in the summer of 1950, attempted to apply his anarchist principles of decentralization and self-management — principles he would later theorize in *Growing Up Absurd* (1960). Group dynamics techniques inspired by Kurt Lewin's T-groups (*training groups*), developed from 1946, were beginning to circulate in progressive American educational milieus; BMC was an early experimental ground for these methods of collective *feedback*, even if their formalization would come later. These experiments were pioneering — perhaps too pioneering. They ran up against structural limits. Community meetings became interminable and exhausting; personal conflicts (notably around Albers, who left BMC in 1949 after tensions with the administration) paralyzed decision-making; feedback dispositifs, without a sufficiently formalized framework, could devolve into destructive confrontation.

Horizontality was an ethos, but the architecture remained fragile. It depended on the goodwill of individuals — and when that goodwill was lacking, the dispositifs were not enough to compensate. The commons of investigation had difficulty sustaining itself without co-produced tools to materialize and perpetuate it beyond individual persons. This is the decisive lesson of Black Mountain: horizontality is not decreed; it is built through explicit dispositifs, through infrastructure — but these dispositifs must be robust enough to survive conflicts, and flexible enough not to bureaucratize the vital impulse of experimentation. BMC had the dispositifs; what it perhaps lacked was the maintenance of those dispositifs — the capacity to repair them, adjust them, evolve them when they ceased to function. This double reading of Black Mountain — model of emancipation and warning about its limits — is not a contradiction but a productive tension. Black Mountain did not "fail" to realize assembly; it experimented with the conditions of its possibility and the obstacles to its realization. It is precisely because BMC stumbled on the fragility of its own dispositifs that we can today think about what they lacked: not more structure, but better maintenance of structure.

### Laboratory Thinking: An Alternative Genealogy

Before synthesizing the aporias of prior models, we should trace a transversal genealogy: that of "laboratory thinking," which runs through these regimes without being reducible to any one of them. The classical scientific laboratory — that of Claude Bernard or Pasteur — is a closed, sterile space where variables are controlled and facts purified. This model has often served as a foil or, more clumsily, as a reference of "rigor" for artistic research. But other genealogies are possible. Aby Warburg, with his "library-laboratory" and his *Mnemosyne* project (1924–1929), invented another relationship to knowledge. The black panels on which images were pinned without explanatory text constituted a space of montage where knowledge arose from visual shock, from the "good neighborliness" between heterogeneous images. It is a thought by atlas, by constellation — which prefigures our digital interfaces and moodboards, but with a heuristic power we have often lost. What is decisive in Warburg: the work of montage was visible. The panels of Mnemosyne were not a conclusion but a process — a thought in the making, modifiable, reconfigurable. Warburg received the scholars of his circle — Fritz Saxl, Edgar Wind, Gertrud Bing — in front of his panels, reconfigured them during discussion, tested visual hypotheses in real time. They discussed the juxtaposition of images; Warburg moved a reproduction; meaning transformed before the participants' eyes.

But a nuance is needed: the Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg was not a school. It was a circle of peer-researchers, not a "master"-student relationship. Warburg did not "descend" toward the ignorant; he worked with collaborators who contributed to the project. Warburg's model prefigures less the classroom than the research seminar — or the dissertation workshop. It is a horizontality among confirmed researchers, not between teacher and students. Transposing it to the pedagogical context therefore requires additional work: how to create the conditions for students in training to participate in this type of collective inquiry? What is decisive in Warburg: the work of montage was visible. The panels of Mnemosyne were not a conclusion but a process — a thought in the making, modifiable, reconfigurable. Visitors to the Warburg library saw the research in progress, not just its results. The material infrastructure (the panels, the pins, the spatial arrangement) made the process tangible.

John Dewey, with his "laboratory school" (*Laboratory School*, Chicago, 1896), proposed another path: education as a process of life, ideas validated by social experience. This is the foundation of project-based pedagogy and learning by doing. For Dewey, the laboratory is not a site for verifying pre-established hypotheses but a space where thought takes form in action. A concrete example: at the Laboratory School, children learned mathematics by cooking — measuring ingredients, calculating proportions, adjusting quantities. The teacher did not transmit abstract knowledge; they accompanied an activity whose problems arose in situation. Dewey had articulated the principle as early as 1897: "The teacher is not in the school to impose certain ideas or to form certain habits in the child, but is there *as a member of the community* to select the influences which shall affect the child and to assist him in properly responding to these influences." [^8]

[^8]: John DEWEY, "My Pedagogic Creed," *The School Journal*, vol. LIV, no. 3, January 16, 1897, p. 9.

But above all: the teachers at the Laboratory School were themselves researchers. They documented their practices, tested pedagogical hypotheses, met to analyze what had worked or failed. Dewey himself observed classes and adjusted the dispositif. The school was a laboratory for teachers as much as for students. An important nuance: in Dewey, the teacher exposes their pedagogical work (how to teach), not necessarily their personal artistic or intellectual work. It is research on teaching, not research within the discipline being taught. Transposing this to art education requires going further: the teacher-artist exposes not only their pedagogy but their artistic practice itself. These alternative genealogies — Warburg, Dewey — make it possible to think the laboratory otherwise than as a closed space of purification. The laboratory of research-creation is a space of montage (Warburg) and of social experience (Dewey) — a site where heterogeneous knowledges rub against each other, where thought takes shape in doing. And above all: a site where work is visible, where the process is exposed, where infrastructure makes assembly possible.

But these historical genealogies are no longer sufficient. In the recent period (2020–2025), the function of the laboratory has mutated. Faced with generative AIs that excel at producing probable results — the statistical average of everything already said or shown — the research-creation laboratory becomes a factory of improbables. Bernard Stiegler gave this mutation its conceptual framework. The current era is no longer merely the Anthropocene but the "Entropocene": an epoch in which automation destroys knowledges — know-how, know-how-to-live, know-how-to-think — faster than societies manage to produce new ones. Entropy, for Stiegler, is not only thermodynamic; it is noetic: the flattening of singularities, the homogenization of forms of life and thought. Against this tendency, only knowledges — "as practices of negentropy" [^stiegler_neguentropie] — can produce difference, bifurcation, novelty.

[^stiegler_neguentropie]: Cf. Bernard STIEGLER, *La Société automatique, 1. L'avenir du travail*, Paris, Fayard, 2015, esp. ch. 1 and the postface. Stiegler defines knowledges as "practices of negentropy" capable of producing "bifurcations" against the entropic tendency of automation. Cf. also *Qu'appelle-t-on panser? 1. L'immense régression*, Paris, Les Liens qui Libèrent, 2018.

The research-creation laboratory, in this perspective, is a site of negentropic production: where generative AI flattens toward the statistical mean, the laboratory produces deviation, accident, the unforeseen.

Vilém Flusser had anticipated this configuration as early as 1983. His distinction between "redundant" images — those that merely confirm the apparatus's program — and "informative" images — those that explore still-untried possibilities — describes exactly the contemporary situation. Flusser wrote: photographers worthy of the name "are in pursuit of possibilities that are still unexplored in the camera's program, in pursuit of informative, improbable images that have not been seen before." [^flusser_photographe]

[^flusser_photographe]: Vilém FLUSSER, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography*, London, Reaktion Books, 1983, p. 37.

Generative AI, in this sense, is the Flusserian apparatus par excellence: it industrially produces the redundant (the average of everything already seen) and presents this redundancy as creation. The laboratory, by contrast, is the site where one plays against the program — where one deautomatizes protocols to produce the improbable. It is a space of friction where one opposes the resistance of the material — the body, the clay, the buggy code — to the fluidity of calculation.

The anthropologist Tim Ingold offers a decisive theoretical framework for thinking this friction. In *Making* (2013), he opposes two models of making. The hylomorphic model — inherited from Aristotle and dominant in Western thought — saw "form [...] as imposed by an agent with a particular design in mind, while matter, thus rendered passive and inert," [^ingold_hylemorphism] is no more than a receptacle.

[^ingold_hylemorphism]: Tim INGOLD, *Making: Anthropology, Archaeology, Art and Architecture*, Abingdon, Routledge, 2013, p. 20–21.

The craftsperson would first have an idea, then execute it. This model is that of the engineer, the industrial designer — and, we might add, of generative AI, which "applies" a prompt to a statistical material. Ingold opposes to it a model of correspondence: "The forms of things arise within fields of force and flows of material. It is by intervening in these force-fields and following the lines of flow that practitioners make things." [^ingold_textility]

[^ingold_textility]: Tim INGOLD, "The Textility of Making," *Cambridge Journal of Economics*, vol. 34, no. 1, 2010, p. 91; argument reprised in *Making*, op. cit., ch. 2.

The woodworker follows the grain of the wood; the potter follows the plasticity of the clay; the draughtsperson follows the grain of the paper. Form is not imposed from above; it emerges from the encounter between gesture and material. Thought does not precede making; it takes shape in making.

This distinction has major pedagogical implications. If thought takes shape in the gesture, then teaching cannot be limited to transmitting ideas that the student will then apply. The teacher must show the gesture — and above all, show how the gesture responds to the material. Ingold speaks of an "education of attention": to learn is to learn to perceive what the material tells us, to adjust our gesture to its responses. AI has no hands. It has no body that resists, that tires, that adjusts. It does not "follow" materials; it calculates probabilities. The research-creation laboratory is the site of the "thinking hand" — the space where the friction between gesture and material produces a knowledge the machine cannot simulate. Here, theory does not precede practice: it springs from the technical accident the machine strives at all costs to avoid. The exposed teacher, in this Ingoldian perspective, is the one who shows how their hand corresponds with the material — how they follow resistances, adjust gestures, let forms emerge that they had not foreseen. They do not show the application of an idea; they show a correspondence in action.

### Infrastructure as Work: *Parliament of Bodies*, the *Navire Avenir*, and the *School of Mutants*

The most radical contemporary experiments have learned the lessons of Black Mountain. They no longer content themselves with proclaiming horizontality; they build the dispositifs that make it possible. The work of art is no longer the finished object; it is the infrastructure that enables encounter. Paul B. Preciado, in the context of Documenta 14 (2017), created the *Parliament of Bodies* — a "parliament of bodies" where teaching takes place not in a standard classroom but in a "soft," reconfigurable architecture designed by Andreas Angelidakis. Foam blocks resembling concrete ruins could be moved, stacked, transformed by participants. Space itself becomes a tool of co-production; the arrangement of bodies in space is part of what is learned. The *Parliament of Bodies* was not a site for transmitting knowledge but for political constitution. Preciado describes it as a "stateless assembly" — a parliament without papers, founded not on national identity but on the shared vulnerability of bodies. The "34 Exercises of Freedom" that took place there did not teach a content; they performed forms of common life.

In another register, the project of the *Navire Avenir* (Ship of the Future), initiated by the collective PEROU (Sébastien Thiéry), brings together artists, architects, rescue sailors, caregivers, and survivors to design the first European vessel dedicated to sea rescue. The work is not merely the ship under construction; it is the constituent assembly that drafts its specifications. It is a pedagogy of extreme design, where art serves a vital function, learning from naval engineering and maritime law to produce a political form.
The *School of Mutants*, founded by Hamedine Kane and Stéphane Verlet-Bottéro in Dakar, pushes the logic even further: the school is not a building but a nomadic structure that "unlearns" the colonial university. It reactivates the archives of post-independence African educational utopias (notably the University of Mutants in Gorée, 1979) to imagine other forms of transmission. The school "mutates" according to context (Dakar, Berlin, Taipei, Leeds), carrying with it a library of anti-colonial knowledges.

These examples show that "making assembly" is not merely a question of posture or intention; it is a question of infrastructure. The exposed teacher cannot merely show their work; they must build the material dispositifs that make co-production possible. Architecture, space, tools, protocols are part of the pedagogical work.
However, the teacher must guard against becoming the owner of the infrastructure. If they remain the only one to define the rules of the *lumbung* or to draw the plans of the Parliament, they replace the authority of knowledge with the authority of the dispositif — they shift from *magister* (master of content) to *governor* (master of the framework). The history of Documenta 15 showed that even the architects of the *lumbung* (ruangrupa) struggled to manage the images and conflicts that emerged from their own structure. The ultimate objective is for the assembly to be able, eventually, to revoke its architect or to redraw its own plans. The teacher puts in place a provisional and sabotable structure — an infrastructure that students can hack, divert, transform.

### Synthesis: From the Aporias of Prior Models to Assembly

This historical traverse allows us to identify the aporias each model encountered: The Academy possessed clear rules but produced dependence on the "master." Knowledge was embodied in a person; evaluation was unilateral. The "master" did not show their work in progress. The Bauhaus proposed a common language but imposed an aesthetic dogmatism disguised as freedom. The formal universal replaced academic authority without eliminating it. The *Meister* arrived with finished theories. The VKhUTEMAS abolished the aesthetic hierarchy but replaced it with a political one. Revolutionary ideology became the new uncontestable authority. The Party cadre did not doubt publicly. Black Mountain College invented pioneering dispositifs (democratic meetings, group dynamics, self-management) but could not maintain them. The structures existed; it was their maintenance that was lacking. Personal conflicts paralyzed decision-making mechanisms; horizontality, for lack of upkeep, allowed informal powers to re-emerge. What all these models lacked — except partially Black Mountain — was the visibility of the teacher's work. The academic "master," the Bauhaus *Meister*, the revolutionary cadre of the VKhUTEMAS all taught from a position of constituted knowledge. Their research process remained private; only results were made public. Yet, as Tim Ingold writes, "the classroom is a place where much of the *real [...] work* is carried on, a site of creative transformation in which we join with the thinking of our predecessors in order to go further, beyond what they would ever have imagined." [^9] It is this "real work" that the prior models kept invisible.

[^9]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, op. cit., p. viii.

But visibility is not enough. Dispositifs that guarantee equality — explicit, negotiable, contestable structures — are also needed. And an infrastructure — a space, tools, protocols — that makes co-production materially possible is needed. And finally, the maintenance of these dispositifs — the collective capacity to repair, adjust, and evolve them — is needed. The proposition of the exposed teacher attempts to resolve these aporias by combining several elements: the explication of rules (against academic opacity), the co-construction of knowledge (against modernist dogmatism), the symmetry of evaluation (against the arbitrariness of informal power).

The figure of the exposed teacher, as it will be developed in the following sections, is constructed precisely from these four aporias. It articulates: explicit structural dispositifs, in response to the tyranny of structurelessness identified by Freeman; the visibility of work in progress, in response to the opacity of process that grounded the authority of both the academic "master" and the *Meister*; shared material infrastructure, in response to the merely declarative horizontality that collapses at the first conflict; rotational maintenance of dispositifs, in response to the exhaustion of the sole maintainer, whose history at Black Mountain — and at Documenta 15 — showed inevitably reconstitutes an informal sovereignty.

This articulation raises considerable theoretical and practical difficulties, which the following sections will examine in turn: the epistemic conditions for the disembodiment of knowledge, the mechanisms of a pedagogy of lack, the construction of common dispositifs, the ethical limits of transparency, the political conditions of assembly, and the reconfigurations imposed by generative AI.

## II. The Artist-Researcher and the Disembodiment of Knowledge

The integration of art schools into the university system and the emergence of the figure of the artist-researcher reconfigure this landscape. The artist is no longer solely a producer of works but a producer of knowledge, required to make explicit what their practice contributes to the field. This transformation has decisive consequences for the pedagogical relationship.

### The Bologna Process and the Conflict of the Faculties

The Bologna process (1999) imposed on European art schools a restructuring of their curricula according to the LMD (Bachelor-Master-Doctorate) framework. The doctoral art student becomes a "young researcher." The teacher becomes a "practitioner-researcher." A decisive shift occurs: knowledge is no longer supposed to be possessed; it is supposed to be produced. And if it is produced, it can be co-produced. This transformation has a logical consequence that many still refuse to draw: if the teacher is a researcher, they must show their research. A researcher who does not publish, who does not present their work, who does not submit it to discussion, is not a researcher -- they are an enlightened amateur. Likewise, a teacher-researcher who does not show their research to their students maintains an unjustifiable asymmetry: they demand of students what they do not require of themselves. Henk Borgdorff, in *The Conflict of the Faculties* (2012), named the resulting structural tension: on one side, the norms of academic research (methodological explication, peer review, communicability of results); on the other, artistic practices (singularity, intuition, the opacity of the work). How does one account for a practice that, by definition, resists explication? Steven Henry Madoff reminds us that "no school is a school without an idea" and that "*an ethics of knowledge is the foundation of any school* in its essential definition as a gathering place." [^10] The art school cannot be reduced to a training dispositif; it is a site where an ethical relationship to knowledge is at stake.

[^10]: Steven Henry MADOFF (ed.), *Art School: Propositions for the 21st Century*, Cambridge, MA, MIT Press, 2009, p. ix.

Research-creation makes practice itself a mode of research, provided it is accompanied by a work of explication, formatting, and archiving. The artist-researcher cannot content themselves with producing a work; they must account for what it contributes to knowledge. This demand for explication performs a gesture of disembodiment: knowledge ceases to be lodged in the sole charismatic person of the teacher and becomes a shareable, documented, verifiable resource. But a critical reading inspired by the sociology of academic labor suggests a darker interpretation. Disembodiment is also the name of an alienation. To transform a singular artistic practice, often opaque to itself, into "explicit methodology" and "communicable results" is to subject it to the norms of quantitative evaluation and audit. The risk exists of confusing art with its documentation, the work with its explanatory label.

### The Archipelagic Medium: Attention, Inter-pollination, and Pedagogical Consequences

The previous section identified a tension: Bologna demands the explication of what, in artistic practice, resists explication. This tension cannot be resolved if one remains within a "continental" conception of university knowledge -- a unified territory, administered from a center, where each discipline occupies a delimited parcel. Yves Citton proposes an alternative with the concept of the "archipelagic medium," developed within the framework of the EUR ArTeC (University Research School supported by the universities Paris 8 and Paris Nanterre). This concept articulates two threads of his thought -- the reworking of Edouard Glissant's "archipelagic thinking" and the "ecology of attention" -- into a tool with direct consequences for our discussion.

The first thread is Glissantian. "Archipelagic thinking" refuses closed systems and root-identities in favor of relations, crossings, creolizations. But where Glissant thought the archipelago as a geographical and cultural condition -- that of the Caribbean -- Citton thinks it as a mediatic condition. Our knowledges, our images, our data circulate between heterogeneous platforms, incompatible formats, dissonant regimes of truth. In *Arts, sciences, technologies. Defis a la recherche-creation* (Les presses du reel, 2025), co-edited with Marc Lenot, Citton and his contributors map these islets: visual arts (criteria of exhibition and market), cognitive sciences (experimental protocols), design (production constraints), digital humanities (databases and algorithms). These islands share neither languages, nor criteria of validation, nor temporalities. But they can enter into resonance -- and it is in these resonances that research-creation finds its space. What distinguishes the "archipelagic medium" from a simple geographical metaphor is that it names a technical condition. The archipelago is not merely a state of knowledge; it is a state of media. The material supports through which we produce, store, and transmit knowledge are themselves archipelagic: a .obj file does not spontaneously dialogue with a .pdf article; a performance recording does not "translate" naturally into quantitative data; a training dataset has no commensurability with a sketchbook. Research-creation does not consist in ignoring these incompatibilities but in working within the gaps they produce. The "challenge" in the book's title is primarily a challenge of translation between formats that resist translation.

The second thread is attentional. In *Pour une ecologie de l'attention* (Seuil, 2014), Citton distinguishes the "economy of attention" -- which treats attention as a scarce resource to capture and monetize -- from the "ecology of attention" -- which treats it as a collective milieu to cultivate. This distinction is decisive for understanding what AI does to teaching. Generative models are the most perfected instruments of the economy of attention: they produce instant responses, calibrated to satisfy the requester, optimized to maintain engagement. Their logic is that of capture -- capturing the user's attention by providing exactly what they expect, at the moment they expect it, in the most fluid format possible. This capture produces what Citton calls "fascinated attention": an attention caught in a flow that leaves no room for bifurcation, fertile distraction, or unforeseen detour. The research-creation workshop, by contrast, should function as a milieu of "joint attention" -- an attention shared among several subjects who are looking together at something that none of them yet quite understands. The exposed teacher, by showing their work in progress, does not capture the student's attention (as an LLM does by generating a fluent response); they direct it toward a third object -- the draft, the dispositif, the impasse -- around which collective attention can coalesce. The difference is not between human attention and machinic attention, but between two attentional regimes: capture (which closes) and conjunction (which opens).

This shift from capture to conjunction has a concrete pedagogical consequence. The archipelagic teacher does not possess a unified territory of knowledge that they distribute from a center. They operate between islands -- between studio practice and critical theory, between code and gesture, between archive and improvisation. Their authority comes not from mastering a continent but from their capacity to produce what Citton, borrowing from ArTeC's vocabulary, calls "inter-pollinations": transfers of pollen between practices that would not fertilize each other spontaneously. The botanical metaphor is more precise than the maritime metaphor of the "navigator": the navigator knows the route; the inter-pollinator does not know in advance what will take. They carry fragments from one register to another -- a Simondonian concept into a ceramics workshop, a video editing technique into a philosophy seminar -- and they wait to see if something germinates. This gesture presupposes a double exposure: the teacher must show both their competence (what they carry) and their incompetence (the fact that they do not master the terrain of arrival). The inter-pollinator is expert in nothing in particular; they are a practitioner of the in-between. And it is precisely this uncomfortable position -- neither quite artist, nor quite theorist, nor quite engineer -- that Citton identifies as the condition of possibility for research-creation.

But the archipelago also has its dangers, which Citton does not underestimate. The principal risk is dispersion -- a research-creation that flutters between islands without ever going deep, that confuses inter-pollination with attentional surfing, that produces decorative hybridity rather than knowledge. The symmetrical risk is co-optation: that archipelagic "creativity" becomes a "transmission belt for the culture industry" or a "humanist decoration for the hard sciences." Citton is aware of this and it is why he summons Flusser -- the artist-researcher as "disciplinary exile" who, from their uncomfortable position, interrogates apparatuses rather than serving them. Disciplinary exile is not a romantic posture; it is the refusal to be assigned to an island. For the exposed teacher, this double vigilance -- against dispersion and against co-optation -- translates into a concrete principle: inter-pollination must be documented. It is not enough to "cross disciplines"; one must show what the crossing produces, what it misses, what resists translation. The visible labor (which we will detail in the section "Visible Labor") is also a labor of visible translation -- the patient, often failed, work of carrying something from one island to another. This demand for documentation connects with the question of the prismatic archive (section "The Archive as Commons"): the archive that does not liquefy provenances but maintains the gaps, the tensions, the fertile incompatibilities between heterogeneous registers.

### The Production of Artistic Subjectivity

Howard Singerman, in *Art Subjects: Making Artists in the American University* (1999), offers an analysis that goes further. According to him, contemporary art education does not transmit rules, language, or even attitudes: it produces a type of subject. The post-MFA university studio is a "linguistic community" where the student learns to speak like an artist, to behave like an artist, to present themselves as an artist. What is transmitted is a form of professional subjectivity. This analysis has decisive implications. If art education produces subjects rather than knowledges, then pedagogical asymmetry takes a new form: the teacher is no longer the one who knows, but the one who is. They embody the model of successful subjectivity -- the accomplished, recognized, legitimate artist -- that the student must imitate. Authority is no longer epistemic (founded on knowledge) but charismatic (founded on being). James Elkins, in *Why Art Cannot Be Taught* (2001), extends this analysis by radicalizing the diagnosis set out in the introduction: the opacity of what is transmitted is not a defect of the system -- it is its operating principle. The less one can define what art is, the more the authority of the one who "embodies" it becomes uncontestable -- and uncontrollable. The studio becomes a site of quasi-mystical initiation. Boris Groys, in his contribution to *Art School* titled "Education by Infection," [^11] reverses this image: what is transmitted in the studio is not knowledge but a *contagion* -- one does not catch art; one is contaminated by a practice. The metaphor of infection presupposes contact, co-presence, a shared vulnerability that remote transmission (including by AI) cannot simulate.

[^11]: Boris GROYS, "Education by Infection," in Steven Henry MADOFF (ed.), *Art School: Propositions for the 21st Century*, op. cit.

It is precisely this opacity that the exposed teacher must break. Not by pretending the secret does not exist, but by showing that there is no secret -- only work. Visible, verifiable, shareable work. The alchemy is not a magic; it is a practice. And this practice can be shown.

### Disembodying Subjectivity: Risks and Limits

The problem is therefore not only to disembody contents, but also to disembody this supposedly successful subjectivity that continues to ground the professor's authority. It is about showing that one is in the process of becoming an artist rather than someone who already is one -- about renouncing the embodiment of the model to become a research companion.

This strategy is illuminated by the Lacanian concept of the "Subject Supposed to Know" (SSK), developed in Seminar XI (1964). For Lacan, the transference -- what leads an analysand to engage in treatment -- does not rest on any real knowledge of the analyst, but on a supposition: the analysand supposes that the analyst holds knowledge about their desire. The analyst does not need to know anything; it suffices that this knowledge is supposed of them. This supposition is the "pivot" of the transference: it is what sets the analytic work in motion.

The transposition to the pedagogical relationship is illuminating. The art student supposes that the teacher holds knowledge about what art is, about how one becomes an artist, about what makes a work "work." This supposition is not an error to be corrected; it is the motor of the pedagogical relationship. Without it, why would the student come to class? Why would they listen to the teacher's advice? The problem is not the supposition itself, but what happens when it solidifies. In the analytic cure, the goal is the "fall" of the SSK: the analysand progressively discovers that the analyst did not hold knowledge about their desire -- that this knowledge had to be constructed, and could only be constructed by the analysand themselves. This discovery is not a disappointment; it is a liberation. The analysand ceases to seek in the other the key to their own desire.

The exposed teacher performs an analogous transformation -- but by different means. By showing their work in progress, their doubts, their revisions, they do not destroy the supposition of knowledge (which would be impossible and counterproductive), but they displace it. The student continues to suppose a knowledge -- but this knowledge is no longer lodged in the person of the teacher; it is projected toward a third object: the work itself, the research process, the commons under construction. Desire does not disappear; it changes object.

This displacement is delicate. If the teacher says too quickly "I don't know," "I'm searching," "I'm wrong," they risk producing not a liberation but a disorientation. The student, deprived of a foothold, may feel abandoned. The fall of the SSK, in analysis, takes years; it cannot be precipitated without violence. Likewise, in pedagogy, the disembodiment of knowledge must be progressive -- accompanied, scaffolded by dispositifs that offer other holds. The answer proposed here is the "displacement of desire" toward co-production: the object of desire is no longer the "Master" (what they know, what they are), but the "Commons" (what we build together). The shared archive, the co-produced tools, the collective research become the new supports of transference. The student no longer desires to be like the teacher; they desire to participate in an inquiry of which the teacher is themselves a stakeholder. This is a demanding transformation, which presupposes considerable psychic and political maturity. Students -- especially at the beginning of their studies -- are often in need of reassurance and strong identificatory models. They need figures with whom to identify, mirrors in which to project themselves. A teacher who refuses all identification can produce an effect of disorientation rather than emancipation. The proposition must therefore be nuanced. It is not about refusing all identification, but about displacing it: the student can identify not with the teacher-who-knows, but with the teacher-who-searches. The model is no longer accomplished mastery but research in action -- visible work. This displacement maintains the identificatory function (necessary for the psychic structuring of learning) while transforming its content. Moreover, the need for models varies according to the phases of training.

In the first year, the student often needs strong reference points; at the end of their studies, they can more readily tolerate -- and desire -- shared uncertainty. The exposed teacher is perhaps not a model for every situation: they are better suited to advanced stages of training, when the student has already constructed an artistic identity solid enough to withstand confrontation with the teacher's own incompleteness.

### The Body That Remains

The disembodiment of knowledge must not be confused with a dematerialization of teaching. If knowledge detaches from the charismatic person of the teacher to become a shareable resource, the teacher's body remains irreducibly present -- and this presence is constitutive of the pedagogical relationship. Two dimensions must be distinguished here: embodied knowledge and situated knowledge. Embodied knowledge designates what can only be learned through the body. In a sculpture studio, the body that manipulates the material is a body that makes mistakes, hesitates, adjusts -- and it is precisely this hesitation that teaches. The student does not learn only by looking at the result; they learn by watching how the teacher's body negotiates with the material. In a performance class, the teacher's physical presence -- their breathing, their positioning in space, their micro-gestures -- is part of what is transmitted.

Mireille Losco-Lena, in *Faire theatre sous le signe de la recherche* (2017), showed that the Anglo-Saxon concept of *Practice-as-Research* is "difficult to assimilate" in the French context, precisely because theatrical research can be reduced neither to knowledge about practice (the external university discourse) nor to knowledge of practice (artisanal know-how). The book identifies four distinct regimes of what "research" means in theater: the laboratory (from Stanislavski to Grotowski, a closed space of systematic experimentation), the creative process (rehearsal as inquiry), institutional research-creation (imposed by the university framework), and the fabrication of spectatorial experiences. This distinction is decisive for our discussion: the exposed teacher does not belong to any one of these four regimes in isolation -- they articulate them by making visible, before students, the passage from one to another.

Situated knowledge designates something else: the fact that all knowledge is produced from a particular position -- a body marked by gender, race, class, history. Donna Haraway, in her foundational article "Situated Knowledges" (1988), critiques what she calls the "god trick": the pretension of science to speak from nowhere, from a position of disembodied objectivity. For Haraway, this pretension is a mystification; all knowledge is partial (incomplete) and partisan (committed). Recognizing this situation is not a weakness; it is the condition of a "strong objectivity" -- an objectivity that assumes its limits and positions rather than masking them. The exposed teacher articulates these two dimensions. On one hand, they show their body at work -- the fatigue, the fluctuating attention, the gesture that searches. On the other, they assume that what they teach is situated: produced from a particular position in the artistic field, marked by a trajectory, by specific privileges or vulnerabilities. They do not pretend to speak from nowhere; they say where they speak from. This double exposure -- of the body and of the position -- radically distinguishes human teaching from machinic transmission. To return to the questions raised by AI: it has neither a body that tires, nor a position to assume; it simulates a disembodied objectivity that Haraway has taught us to suspect.

The exposed teacher does not only expose their intellectual process; they expose their body at work and their position in the world. Technical gestures, working postures, rhythms of attention -- all of this is learned through co-presence, through observation, through osmosis. The disembodiment of propositional knowledge (what can be formulated in statements) must not obscure the embodiment of practical knowledge (what can only be learned through the body) nor the situation of critical knowledge (what can only be understood by knowing where it speaks from).

## III. The Pedagogy of Lack

Traditional pedagogical asymmetry rests on an imaginary contract: the student supposes that the teacher possesses a knowledge -- or an artistic subjectivity -- that the student does not yet have. This supposition organizes the transfer of desire: the student desires what the teacher is supposed to possess, and it is this desire that fuels learning. But this structure has a cost.

### The Logic of Supposition and Its Effects

The logic of supposition works as follows: the student supposes the teacher knows; this supposition creates a transfer of trust; the student desires what the teacher is supposed to possess. The problem is not that this logic is false -- the teacher generally knows a great deal. The problem is that it creates a structure of dependence. The student cannot verify; they must believe. This structure encourages idealization, discourages critique, infantilizes. To question the teacher would be to undermine the supposition that sustains the entire dispositif. Hence the inhibition of critique -- even when it would be legitimate, even when it would be necessary. The student who doubts the teacher doubts their own capacity to learn; they undermine the ground on which they walk. Jacques Ranciere, in *The Ignorant Schoolmaster* (1987), proposed the most radical critique of this logic. The book tells the intellectual adventure of Joseph Jacotot (1770-1840), a French revolutionary exiled in the Netherlands, who was faced with a practical problem: teaching French literature to Flemish students whose language he did not speak. For lack of better options, Jacotot had them procure a bilingual edition of Fenelon's *Telemaque* and asked them to learn the French text with the help of the translation. To his astonishment, the students learned not only the text but French itself -- without his having explained anything. From this experience, Jacotot drew a scandalous conclusion: the magisterial explanation -- far from reducing ignorance -- produces it.

> "To explain something to someone is first of all to show them that they cannot understand it by themselves." [^12]

[^12]: Jacques RANCIERE, *Le Maitre ignorant. Cinq lecons sur l'emancipation intellectuelle*, Paris, Fayard, 1987, p. 15. Eng. trans.: *The Ignorant Schoolmaster: Five Lessons in Intellectual Emancipation*, trans. Kristin Ross, Stanford, Stanford University Press, 1991.

Explanation presupposes an inequality of intelligences that it claims to bridge but endlessly reproduces. The teacher explains because the student cannot understand alone; the student cannot understand alone because they have always been explained to. This is what Ranciere calls the "pedagogical myth": the vicious circle by which the educational institution creates the incapacity it claims to repair. Jacques Derrida, cited as an epigraph by Biesta, gives this reversal its most radical formulation:

> "One has to accept that 'it' (the other, or whatever 'it' may be) is stronger than I am, for something to happen. I have to lack a certain strength, I have to lack it enough, for something to happen. [...] There has to be weakness..." [^13]

[^13]: Jacques DERRIDA, cited as epigraph in Gert J.J. BIESTA, *The Beautiful Risk of Education*, op. cit.

Jacotot opposes to this myth an axiom: the equality of intelligences. Not the equality of knowledges (an obvious absurdity), but the equality of the capacity to learn. Every human intelligence can learn by itself -- provided a will sets it in motion. The role of the "master" is therefore not to transmit knowledge (which presupposes inequality), but to verify that the student exercises their intelligence. Jacotot did not ask his students to repeat what he had told them; he asked them: "And you -- what did you see? What do you think of it? What do you make of it?" The crucial distinction is between teaching and explaining. One can teach what one does not know -- that is the paradox of the "ignorant schoolmaster." Jacotot taught piano, painting -- disciplines he did not master. For teaching, in this perspective, is creating the conditions for the other to learn -- not transmitting a content. The "ignorant schoolmaster" does not say "I will explain it to you"; they say "learn, and tell me what you think." They do not verify that the student has understood what the teacher knows; they verify that the student has searched. Ranciere insists: this method is not one "pedagogy" among others. It is an anti-pedagogy -- a refusal of the very principle of explanation as the foundation of teaching. Progressive pedagogies (Freinet, Montessori, etc.) sought to improve explanation, to make it more active, more playful, more adapted. Jacotot refuses explanation itself. It is not the method that must change, it is the postulate: one must start from equality, not arrive at it.

### Breaking the Contract: From Supposition to Verification

The exposed teacher proposes to break this contract -- but by means other than Jacotot's. Where the "ignorant schoolmaster" withdrew to let the student learn alone ("learn, and tell me what you think"), the exposed teacher shows their own learning process. They no longer ask to be believed, but to be verified. By making visible their work in progress, their hesitations, their failures, they disembody knowledge from their person and present it as a lacking process rather than as a possessed capital. This is the heart of the proposition: moving from supposition to verification. The student must no longer suppose that the teacher knows; they can verify how the teacher searches. Belief gives way to observation; authority founded on mystery gives way to authority founded on visible work. The exposed teacher takes up Jacotot's axiom (the equality of intelligences) but adds something: the visibility of work. Jacotot verified that the student searched; the exposed teacher submits themselves to this same verification. Their labor is visible; the student can observe -- and no longer merely suppose -- what it means to search.

Where Jacotot said "learn this book and tell me what you think," the exposed teacher says "watch how I search, and let us search together." This is a significant difference. Jacotot maintained an asymmetry of position: the "master" verifies, the student is verified. The exposed teacher institutes a symmetry of verification: the student can verify the teacher's work, just as the teacher can verify the student's. The teacher's drafts are accessible; their errors are documented; their revisions are visible. The student can observe that the teacher, too, gropes. They show that they are themselves shot through with doubt, in the midst of searching, never finished. Emmanuel Nal reports the testimony of a fourth-grade student who summarizes this dynamic with disarming clarity: "When I realize that he doesn't know, I want even more to say what I think. It's like a game." [^14] The teacher's avowed non-knowledge does not create a void; it liberates speech.

[^14]: Fourth-grade student, cited in Emmanuel NAL, "Favoriser l'eveil a la demarche philosophique par une pedagogie de l'instant et une reciprocite dans les pratiques reflexives entre enseignants et eleves," *Education et socialisation* [online], 39, 2015, par. 22-23. DOI: https://doi.org/10.4000/edso.1441

The teacher is no longer the full figure facing an empty student, but a subject who also lacks, who is also searching. To expose is to take a risk. To teach is to risk no longer knowing. This is what Tomasz Szkudlarek names, in his preface to Biesta, a teaching "performed *with empty hands, with no instruments of certainty*." [^15] For "the educational way is the slow way, the difficult way, the frustrating way, and, so we might say, *the weak way*, as the outcome of this process can neither be guaranteed nor secured." [^16]

This proposition must, however, be distinguished from what Byung-Chul Han has identified as "the transparency society" -- a contemporary injunction to make everything visible that, far from producing trust, generates exhausting mutual surveillance. In the current attention economy, "vulnerability" has become a social currency: the culture of performed authenticity on social media attests to this. The risk is that the teacher who exposes their process slides from methodological transparency to autobiographical performance. The exposure of doubts and failures can occupy more psychic space than the traditional "master's" silence: it asks students not only to learn, but to become the empathetic witnesses of a process that does not directly concern them -- creating an undue emotional burden, an affective asymmetry that replaces the epistemic one without correcting it.

The exposed teacher, as defined here, does not belong to this confessional logic. The exposure they practice is technical and operational: it bears on gestures, tools, drafts, research protocols -- not on the affects that accompany them. The distinction is the one that Sensevy, in his theory of joint action in didactics, draws between the didactic contract (what is at stake in learning) and the relational contract (what is at stake in the interpersonal relationship). The exposed teacher makes the first visible without instrumentalizing the second.

### Epistemic Symmetry: A Didactic Concept

The concept of "epistemic symmetry," derived from the Theory of Joint Action in Didactics (JATD) developed by Gerard Sensevy and his collaborators, allows us to structure this new posture. Epistemic symmetry does not mean that the teacher and the student know the same thing -- that would be absurd. It means that they are equally responsible for the progression of the inquiry. In the JATD's research, notably that dealing with gender equality in mathematics, epistemic symmetry aims to break the prejudice according to which some students (boys) would be naturally more apt for action and others (girls) for passive listening. Transposed to the research-creation workshop facing generative AIs, this symmetry takes a new form. We often hear that students "master" digital tools better than their teachers -- this is a commonplace that needs nuancing. What students often master better is the fluent use of interfaces; what they master less is the critique of those interfaces, their genealogy, their presuppositions. The older teacher, for their part, may have a critical distance that the "digital native" student lacks -- precisely because they have known a world without these tools. Epistemic symmetry therefore does not consist in "reversing" expertise (the student would know, the teacher would be ignorant), but in recognizing that competences are differently distributed: one knows how to prompt, the other knows how to read biases; one knows the latest models, the other knows the history of image-making machines. Cooperation becomes necessary because no one possesses the whole.

This distribution connects with what Gramsci theorized in his *Prison Notebooks*. Gramsci distinguished two senses of the word "intellectual." In the broad sense, "all men are intellectuals" -- all work, even manual labor, involves an activity of thought, choice, judgment. But in the narrow sense, "not all men have in society the function of intellectuals" -- only some are recognized as such and exercise a function of cultural direction. [^18]

[^18]: Antonio GRAMSCI, *Selections from the Prison Notebooks*, ed. and trans. Q. Hoare and G. Nowell Smith, London, Lawrence and Wishart, 1971, p. 9.

The stakes, for Gramsci, were to bring forth "organic intellectuals" from the working classes, capable of thinking their own condition rather than letting it be thought by others. The research-creation workshop, in the face of AI, can become the site of such an emergence. The student is not merely a user of tools; they can become an intellectual of these tools -- someone who thinks them, critiques them, diverts them. The teacher does not verify whether the student has "learned the lesson"; they verify -- like Jacotot -- whether the student exercises their intelligence upon the objects they manipulate. The shared ignorance before the black box of AI becomes the starting point of a joint inquiry where each contributes from their position. This joint inquiry implies a transformation of the syllabus itself. The concept of "cooperative didactic engineering," from the same theoretical framework, designates "a dispositif that brings together teachers and researchers for the design and implementation of teaching sequences in the field." [^18b] Its principle is iterative: "the sessions produced are jointly conceived, implemented, analyzed, and rethought then re-implemented, analyzed in a process, a movement." [^18c] Co-construction is therefore not an abstract principle of horizontality but a concrete protocol of shared design-test-revision.

[^18b]: Gerard SENSEVY, "Des sciences interventionnelles ancrees sur des alliances entre recherche et terrain? Le cas des ingenieries cooperatives," *Raisons educatives*, no. 25, 2021, p. 163-194.

[^18c]: Gerard SENSEVY, Sophie JOFFREDO-LE BRUN, Mireille MORELLATO, Serge QUILIO, and Jean-Benoit VIGOT, cited in Caroline PERRAUD and Maryvonne MERRI, "Entre recherches en didactique et enseignement/formation: transposition ou enrichissement mutuel?," *Activites*, vol. 21, no. 1, 2024.

Transposed to the research-creation workshop, this protocol means that the course is no longer a fixed program the teacher unfolds, but an open score that adapts to the week's discoveries and dead ends. Students participate in defining questions, evaluating methods, reorienting objectives. The syllabus becomes a living document -- modifiable, negotiated -- not a unilateral contract imposed at the start of the semester.

### The Displacement of Desire

This displacement of the teacher's status entails a displacement of desire. If the teacher no longer presents themselves as the holder of a finished knowledge, the student can no longer simply desire to "have" what the other has. Desire must shift toward the student's own position as researcher and co-producer of knowledge. The student ceases to be a consumer of contents and becomes a co-producer of problems, forms, concepts. The teacher exposes not a "gift" of knowledge -- which would create a symbolic debt and maintain a hierarchy -- but a work object: a text in progress, a dispositif under construction, a still-fragile hypothesis. The student is no longer placed in the position of a grateful debtor but in that of a witness and co-investigator. Paulo Freire opposed "banking" education (the student as an empty receptacle in which the teacher deposits contents) to "problem-posing" education (the student and the teacher as co-investigators of a common world). His formulation is unequivocal: "The teacher is no longer merely the-one-who-teaches, but one who is himself taught in dialogue with the students, who in turn while being taught also teach. They become jointly responsible for a process in which all grow." [^17]

[^17]: Paulo FREIRE, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, New York, Continuum, 1970, p. 80.

The exposed teacher radicalizes this proposition: not only do the teacher and the student investigate the world together, but the teacher themselves becomes an object of investigation -- their process, their choices, their dead ends can be interrogated. The truly ethical space of knowledge then becomes that of shared research: a commons where all intelligences can contribute, critique, verify.

## IV. Dispositifs of the Commons

For symmetry not to remain merely declarative, it must materialize in concrete dispositifs. The visibility of work is not decreed; it is organized. Four dimensions are essential: visible labor, reciprocal evaluation, the distinction between evaluation and certification, and the co-production of tools.

### Visible Labor: Presence and Production

This is the central dispositif, on which all others depend. The teacher works in the presence of students. They show their drafts, their revisions, their dead ends. Knowledge no longer appears as a finished product but as a continuous effort. The artist Thomas Hirschhorn has formulated a concept that radicalizes this demand: "Presence and Production." In his monumental projects -- the *Gramsci Monument* in the Bronx (2013), the *Bataille Monument* in Kassel (2002) -- Hirschhorn commits to being physically present on site 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, for the entire duration of the project (often several months). This presence is not supervision; it is a form of physical solidarity and shared vulnerability. It guarantees that the artist is accessible, accountable, and exposed to the contingencies of the real. Hirschhorn explicitly opposes "presence and production" to "participation." Participation, he says, is often "lazy" and "demagogic": one invites the public to "participate" in an already-conceived work, without real commitment from the artist. Presence, by contrast, implies that the artist is there -- visible, vulnerable, working. It is not simply about being present, but about producing in presence. The monument becomes a factory of events; the artist seeks to create "precarious moments of grace" where thought arises from intense activity.

For teaching, this distinction is decisive. Asking students to "participate" in an already-conceived course is to maintain asymmetry under the guise of openness. Being present and producing in front of them -- showing one's work in the making -- is to transform the relationship. Concretely, visible labor can take several forms: Writing in real time: The teacher writes an article, a text, a research note during the session, with the screen projected. Students see the crossings-out, the reformulations, the moments of blockage. They see that writing is not inspiration but work. Presenting successive versions: The teacher shows the first version of a text, then the second, then the third. They explain what changed and why. Students discover that thought is constructed through revision. Analyzing a failure: The teacher presents a publication rejection, a negative review, a project that did not come to fruition. They analyze what did not work. Students discover that failure is part of the process. Discussing methodological choices: The teacher presents the alternatives they considered and the reasons for their choices.

Students discover that research is made of decisions, not only discoveries. This practice of visible labor belongs to a tradition reaching back at least to the Renaissance workshop, where the apprentice saw the "master" at work. But it differs on an essential point: in the traditional workshop, the "master" showed mastered work, virtuosity in action; here, the teacher shows uncertain work, groping in progress. It is not the demonstration of a competence; it is the exposure of a search. Visible labor enables verification. The student can verify -- and no longer merely suppose -- what it means to search. Supposition gives way to observation; belief gives way to judgment. The student discovers that the teacher, too, crosses out, hesitates, makes mistakes, starts over. This discovery can be destabilizing -- it shatters the image of the infallible "master" -- but it is also liberating: it shows that error is part of the process, that one can search without knowing in advance where one is going.
Visible labor also has a temporal dimension: it exposes the duration of work, against the illusion of instant inspiration. This dimension takes on particular relief today in the face of generative AIs. Not that AI is "the problem" -- it is a tool among others. But it crystallizes a broader transformation: the compression of production time, the effacement of process in favor of result, the disappearance of trace. When a student generates an image in three seconds with Midjourney, they obtain a result -- but they have not traversed the time of research. They have not lived through the thirty failed attempts, the abandoned bifurcations, the revisions after a night's sleep. The teacher who shows their thirty successive drafts does not "resist" AI through slowness -- as if virtue lay in deceleration. They make visible something AI cannot show: the sedimentation of work, the successive layers of decisions, the dialogue with oneself over time. The question is not to choose between speed (the machine) and slowness (the human) -- this opposition is too simple, and it is a class privilege: not everyone has the luxury of the long time. The question is to understand what happens in the time of work, and what is lost when that time is compressed or made invisible. The collective Black Quantum Futurism, to which we will return, calls this "chronopolitics": the way time is distributed, stolen, or rendered inaccessible. The exposed teacher practices a form of temporal transparency: they show that work takes time, and that this time is not an inefficiency to be optimized but a condition of thought.

### Reciprocal Evaluation and the Question of Competence

The teacher's productions are submitted to the structured critique of students. This critique must be formalized -- collective discussion, written evaluation, public commentary -- and not merely tacit or informal. Students become peer-critics whose judgment counts. If the teacher shows their work, that work must be open to criticism. Otherwise, visibility becomes a new form of authority: "Look how hard I work" -- but with no right of reply. Exposure without reciprocal evaluation is an exhibition, not a sharing. An objection arises immediately: the symmetry of rights to speak does not ipso facto create a symmetry of competences. Asking students in the learning phase to evaluate the research of a confirmed teacher raises the question of epistemic legitimacy. Is there not a risk of "pedagogical populism" where the student's immediate opinion would be worth the teacher's constructed expertise? A distinction must be drawn between two types of asymmetry. The asymmetry of power designates the capacity of one to decide the fate of the other (to grade, to certify, to recommend). The asymmetry of competence designates the difference of experience, know-how, familiarity with a field. The first is what we wish to contest; the second is a fact -- and a fact that evolves. Becoming-peer is the goal of training, not its starting point. Reciprocal evaluation does not deny the difference in competence; it refuses that this difference automatically translates into unilateral power. The student can recognize that the teacher knows more while having the right -- and the duty -- to criticize what they see. The student's critique does not need to be "correct" to be useful; it obliges the teacher to make explicit what remained implicit, to justify what seemed obvious. But the student who evaluates their professor's article knows that this same professor will assign them a grade. This situation creates an inevitable distortion: the student may be tempted to spare the teacher, or on the contrary to criticize excessively out of resentment. The habitus of scholastic submission, embodied since childhood, is not erased by decree.

To avoid false symmetry, guarantee dispositifs are needed. But -- and this is a crucial point -- these dispositifs must protect both parties. The teacher is not only in a position of power; they are also, increasingly often, in a position of vulnerability. The precarization of higher education (adjuncts, short-term contracts, non-tenured positions) creates a dependence on the institution that can be exploited. Student evaluations, when transmitted to the administration without mediation, can become instruments of pressure -- even of denunciation. A student unhappy with a grade, a poorly managed personal conflict, a pedagogical misstep, can become a "report" that weakens the teacher's position vis-a-vis an administration eager to avoid conflict.

Two regimes of critique must therefore be distinguished: internal pedagogical critique, which circulates within the group and aims at collective improvement, and institutional evaluation, which reaches the administration and can have consequences for the teacher's career. Epistemic symmetry concerns the first, not the second. The student who critiques the teacher's work within the studio space performs a pedagogical function; the student who "reports" the teacher to the administration performs a policing function. These two gestures have nothing in common.

Three types of dispositifs can be envisioned, with this dual protection in mind:

Internal, nominative critique: Contrary to the initial intuition, anonymity may not be the best solution. In a research community, each person assumes their position. Nominative critique, expressed in the protected space of the studio, makes speech accountable and enables dialogue. It assumes that the teacher has the maturity to receive critique without retaliation -- and that the group has built enough trust for speech to be possible.

Confidentiality vis-a-vis the institution: What is said in the studio stays in the studio. Students' critiques of the teacher's work do not reach the administration. This rule protects the teacher against the instrumentalization of symmetry by the institution (which could use student critiques to weaken the least docile teachers), and it protects the pedagogical space itself: if students know their critiques can be used against the teacher, they will stop critiquing -- or they will critique strategically, which amounts to the same thing.

Temporal separation of formative and certifying spaces: Reciprocal evaluation takes place in a framework distinct from the one in which the teacher assigns grades. This separation protects the student from the teacher's retaliation, but it also protects the teacher against the temptation of complacency -- if they know they will be critiqued, they might be tempted to grade more generously to buy peace.

These dispositifs may seem complex. They are. But this is the price of a symmetry that does not turn against those it claims to emancipate -- whether they are students or teachers. Precarity is not equally distributed; the dispositifs of protection must take this into account.

### Evaluation and Certification: Two Distinct Regimes

A clear distinction must be drawn between two functions that traditional teaching conflates: formative evaluation (which accompanies learning) and certification (which attests to a competence for the outside world). The evaluative symmetry proposed here concerns the first, not the second. Formative evaluation can and should be reciprocal: the student critiques the teacher's work, the teacher critiques the student's, in an exchange that advances both.

Certification, however, belongs to another regime: it commits the institution vis-a-vis the external world (employers, other institutions, the profession). This function cannot be symmetrical -- the student does not certify the teacher. But before proposing solutions, we must acknowledge a structural tension well known to art schools: the dissociation between theory and practice, and the implicit hierarchies it produces. In many institutions, "theoretical" teacher-artists -- those who speak about art, who conceptualize, who write -- are institutionally distinct from technicians -- those who master the machines, the materials, the processes. The former often have status, titles, visibility; the latter have know-how, practical competence, daily contact with students in the studios.

This division reproduces an ancient hierarchy: that of intellectual labor over manual labor, of conception over execution, of the artist over the artisan. It also has a class dimension: technicians, often trained through vocational programs, may perceive teacher-artists who "don't practice" as bourgeois figures -- people who talk about what they don't do, who theorize without getting their hands dirty. This perception is not always unjust; some teachers have indeed long abandoned practice and teach from a position of detachment. But it can also be reductive, for the artist who writes, who exhibits, who conceptualizes their practice -- simply does not do it in the same way as the technician. The exposed teacher, as we define them, can partially defuse this tension -- on the condition of showing that they truly work. If they expose their labor, their drafts, their failures, they can no longer be perceived as the one who "does nothing."

This exposure must include the technical dimension: showing that one knows (or is learning) how to use the tools, that one dialogues with technicians, that one does not disdain manual know-how. The exposed teacher is only credible if they also expose their relationship to technique -- including their technical incompetence, when that is the case, and the way they compensate for or assume it. Evaluation committees should reflect this complexity. The solution is not to abolish certification but to displace it -- and to pluralize it. The model of the research-creation doctorate offers a lead: the student-researcher is evaluated by a jury of external peers, not by their research supervisor. The teacher accompanies but does not certify; they are a research companion, not a judge. This separation could be extended to other levels: mixed committees (teachers from the institution and external ones, professionals, alumni) for certifications, while formative evaluation remains within the space of co-production.

But these committees should also include technicians -- not as auxiliaries but as full evaluators. If we want certification to reflect the reality of artistic work, it cannot be monopolized by those who conceptualize to the detriment of those who fabricate. This is also a question of institutional justice: technicians, often lower-paid and less recognized than teacher-artists, hold indispensable knowledge. Including them in evaluation means recognizing that mastery of a technique is not inferior to mastery of a discourse. Bourdieu and Passeron showed that the explication of the rules of the academic game is a condition for equal opportunity. In this perspective, certification criteria must be explicit, public, debatable -- even if they are not symmetrically applied.

The student must know what they will be judged on, and be able to contest these criteria in the formative space, even if they cannot modify them unilaterally. This distinction between formative evaluation and certification partially resolves the paradox of symmetry: one can be peers in inquiry without being equals before the institution. The exposed teacher does not abolish institutional difference; they create a space -- that of co-production -- where this difference is provisionally suspended.

### The Co-Production of Tools: Assembly as Form

Beyond visible labor, co-production must extend to the very tools of research. The teacher does not merely show their work; they build the instruments of investigation with the students. The pedagogical space becomes an assembly -- a site where rules, methods, objects of inquiry are decided together. The Indonesian collective ruangrupa, curators of Documenta 15 (2022), theorized this dimension through the concept of *lumbung* -- the communal rice barn. The exhibition becomes a space of common life where resources (time, money, attention, knowledges) are pooled and redistributed according to collectively negotiated rules. The invited artists did not produce individual works; they contributed to a relational infrastructure. The key concept of this pedagogy is *nongkrong* -- an Indonesian term meaning "hanging out together," discussing without a set purpose. Unlike Hirschhorn's frenetic schedule (24/7 production), ruangrupa values informal, non-productive time, necessary for building trust. Learning happens through osmosis, through communal cooking, through the collective management of conflicts. These two models -- Hirschhorn's productive intensity, ruangrupa's relational slowness -- are not contradictory; they correspond to different phases of co-production. There is a time for producing together intensely; there is a time for "hanging out" and letting relationships build. The exposed teacher must know how to navigate between these temporalities.

First day of a workshop. The teacher arrives with a list of ten references. They present them briefly, then ask each participant to propose one. Collective discussion: why this reference? How is it relevant to the group's work? Some proposals are retained, others set aside, still others shifted to a "to explore later" list. By the end of the session, the bibliography no longer belongs to the teacher: it has become a common tool, enriched by all.

Second example: building a glossary. A term keeps coming up in discussions -- say, "process." Someone asks: "But what do we actually mean by that?" The teacher could answer; they choose not to. They propose that each person write, in two sentences, their working definition. The definitions are read aloud. They diverge. A debate ensues. Eventually, the group decides to keep two competing definitions in the glossary, with a note on the contexts where each is pertinent. The glossary is not an authoritative dictionary; it is a map of usages.

Third example, more conflictual. A group must decide on the critique protocol. The teacher proposes that each presentation be followed by ten minutes of silence before feedback. A student objects: "Silence is anxiety-inducing. It advantages those already comfortable with waiting." Someone else responds: "That's precisely the point -- silence allows us to move beyond the immediate reaction." The disagreement persists. The group decides to experiment with both formats and to settle the matter at mid-point. The protocol is not imposed; it is negotiated, tested, adjusted. These scenes are not idyllic. They involve tensions, resistances, power dynamics, awkward silences. Co-production does not abolish conflicts; it makes them visible and negotiable. That is precisely its value: it compels the explication of what remained implicit in traditional teaching.

### Interessement: Holding Heterogeneous Knowledges Together

The co-production of tools presupposes holding together heterogeneous knowledges -- technical, theoretical, sensory. Michel Callon, in his sociology of translation, proposed the concept of "interessement" to think this articulation: interessement designates the set of operations by which an actor attempts to stabilize the identity of other actors and to engage them in a network of cooperation. Transposed to the studio, interessement is not seduction; it is the patient construction of the material and intellectual conditions that make cooperation effective -- the reason why each participant has an interest in engaging in the common inquiry. Bruno Latour extended this reflection to the notion of "assembly" itself. In "From Realpolitik to Dingpolitik" (2005), he observes that the word *Ding* (thing) long designated, well before referring to an inert object, "the issue that brings people together because it divides them." "Making assembly," for Latour, means gathering around a matter of concern -- and not a mere matter of fact -- humans, non-human entities, and heterogeneous knowledges. The pedagogical assembly, in this perspective, is not composed solely of teachers and students; it also includes tools, references, materials, institutional constraints -- everything that "counts" in the situation. Now, if tools are part of the assembly, the question of their fabrication becomes political. Ivan Illich had formulated it with radical clarity: "I choose the term 'conviviality' to designate the opposite of industrial productivity. [...] Convivial is the society in which man controls the tool." Illich's criterion of conviviality -- the tool must reinforce the autonomy of its user rather than reduce them to the role of consumer -- takes on particular acuity today in the face of generative AIs, whose proprietary architecture makes any technical appropriation precisely impossible. The user of Midjourney or ChatGPT does not control the tool; they formulate requests to a black box whose functioning they do not understand.

The research-creation laboratory, in this perspective, is also a workshop for technical bricolage: one fabricates, adapts, or diverts one's own tools -- scripts, collection protocols, alternative interfaces, curated datasets -- rather than submitting to pre-established programs. This gesture of fabrication is not an artisanal affectation; it is a condition of intellectual autonomy. A student who learns to write their own scraper, to train a small model on a chosen corpus, or to build an image processing pipeline understands from the inside what the machine does -- and thereby acquires the capacity to critique it.

Isabelle Stengers extends and complicates this perspective with her concept of an "ecology of practices." Where Latour thinks of assembly as gathering around a concern, Stengers insists that each practice engaged in the assembly carries with it requirements and obligations that cannot be negotiated away without betrayal. The "ecology of practices," Stengers writes, "emphasizes positive divergence: practices actively diverge, producing their own particularity in the manner in which each addresses what it addresses." The concept of "reciprocal capture" specifies the mechanism: two heterogeneous practices can mutually reinforce each other -- produce a "symbiotic agreement" -- on the condition that neither reduces itself to the other.

In the research-creation workshop, this ecology imposes a discipline: a visual arts student can "interest" a sociologist in their work on surveillance data -- not by becoming a sociologist, but by showing what their visual approach contributes to the understanding of the phenomenon. The teacher can "interest" their students in a theoretical reference -- not by imposing it as required reading, but by showing what it opens up for their own research. Interessement, in Callon's sense, and reciprocal capture, in Stengers's, describe two sides of the same process: the construction of bonds that enrich the terms without dissolving them.

The exposed teacher practices this interessement constantly: they do not pour out knowledge; they propose connections. This gesture echoes Ingold's formulation: "What makes anthropology educational rather than ethnographic is that we do not so much study others as study with them." The shift from *about* to *with* is the founding gesture of co-production.

### The Archive as Commons: Toward the Prismatic Archive

The different stages of research -- sketches, notes, commentaries, critical feedback -- can be contributed to a shared archive, according to collectively discussed rules. Knowledge detaches from persons to circulate as a common resource. Authority is redefined as the capacity to feed and organize this commons, rather than as the privilege of being its guardian.

The archive is the trace of visible work. It perpetuates what has been shown, allows one to return to the process, authorizes after-the-fact verification. Without an archive, visible labor remains ephemeral -- it disappears with the session. But not all archives are equal. We live through the paradox of an era saturated with digital archives (*Big Data*) yet incapable of sustaining a collective memory. Generative AIs, trained on massive corpora, "hallucinate" the past, blend styles, crush art history into a perpetual present. This is what we might call the liquid archive: a mass of data in which everything mingles, provenances fade, singularities dissolve into the statistical mean.

What I call elsewhere *the liquid archive* is a commons without governance -- a resource shared de facto but not de jure, where no one decides the rules of extraction, contribution, redistribution. The crisis of transmission is not a lack of information, but this liquefaction: the inability to sort, to hierarchize, to maintain differences. When everything is equally accessible, nothing is truly transmitted. Transmission presupposes a selection, an orientation, a friction -- someone who says "this rather than that," "in this order," "for this reason." For, as Ingold reminds us, "in itself, then, the recipe is not knowledge. [...] This is not knowledge that has been *transmitted* to me; it is knowledge that has *grown in me* as I have followed the same paths as my predecessors." [^19] Knowledge is not a deposit but a growth -- it is not stored in a liquid archive; it germinates in a cultivated one.

[^19]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, op. cit., p. 11-12.

Against the liquid archive, we can think a *prismatic archive*. The prism, in optics, does not dissolve light; it diffracts it -- it reveals the spectrum of colors that white light already contained, but latently. The prismatic archive is neither a total archive nor a closed one; it is an archive that maintains differences. It preserves the grain against smoothing, it articulates technical gestures with an explicit political project, it refuses the flattening toward the mean. Three characteristics distinguish the prismatic archive from the liquid archive:

Explicit governance: the liquid archive is a de facto, ungoverned commons; the prismatic archive is a de jure commons, whose rules are discussed and modifiable. Who can deposit? Who can extract? Under what conditions? These questions are not technical; they are political. Elinor Ostrom showed that the commons that endure are those that equip themselves with strict rules accepted by all -- and with mechanisms for conflict resolution.

Traceability of provenances: the liquid archive erases sources; the prismatic archive keeps them visible. When a text is cited, one knows where it comes from, who wrote it, in what context. This traceability is not a fetishism of origin; it is the condition for critique to be possible. One cannot contest what one cannot identify.

Preservation of singularities: the liquid archive tends toward the mean -- this is the nature of statistical models, which learn the probable and forget the improbable. The prismatic archive intentionally preserves what resists the mean: minor works, failed attempts, unfollowed bifurcations, marginal voices. It is negentropic: it produces difference where the system tends toward homogeneity. It is here that the teacher's role must be reinvented: no longer as the holder of knowledge, but as the calibrator of the prism -- the one who determines which spectra become visible, which angles of incidence are institutionally supported, which singularities are preserved against the ambient liquidity.

The question of concrete governance then arises. The co-production of tools is presented as emancipation -- but it is also work. In the knowledge economy, who appropriates the value of this work? If the glossary co-produced by students serves as the basis for the teacher's next article or book, is there not a form of exploitation of student *digital labor*? The governance of commons, as Elinor Ostrom showed, rests on strict rules accepted by all. But these rules are not incompatible with individual rights of withdrawal -- provided these rights are themselves collectively defined and integrated into the rules of the commons.

Concretely, the archiving protocol might work as follows: at the beginning of the workshop, the group collectively defines what will be archived by default (session reports, bibliographies, glossaries) and what will remain private by default (personal notes, unshared drafts). Each participant has a right of withdrawal over what directly concerns them -- their own productions, their recorded contributions -- but this right does not extend to collective productions they helped create. The rule is binding (one cannot unilaterally withdraw a co-produced glossary) but it protects personal space (one can withdraw one's own sketch). This distinction between individual production (right of withdrawal) and collective production (common rule) makes it possible to reconcile the protection of personal opacity with the governance of the commons. It must be explicit from the outset and can be renegotiated along the way -- but any modification of the rules requires collective agreement. A possible answer to the question of appropriation: the common archive should be placed under an open license (Creative Commons, for example), with a rule of reciprocity: everything drawn from it must be returned to it. The teacher who publishes an article drawing on collective work must cite the collective as co-author, or at minimum as contributor. These rules must be explicit from the beginning of the workshop -- they are part of the protocols to be co-produced. At the end of a workshop, the group decides what is contributed to the archive and what remains private. This decision materializes the boundary between opacity and transparency. The archive is not a total capture of the process but a selection -- and this selection is itself a collective act of negotiation. It is here that Ingold's definition of the educational community is confirmed: "a community -- not just a living together but literally a *giving together* (*com-*, 'together', plus *-munus*, 'gift') -- in which everyone has something to give precisely because they have nothing in common." [^20] The commons is not a baseline but an aspiration.

[^20]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, op. cit., p. 6.

## V. The Ethics of Opacity

This program of transparency encounters a paradox: total transparency can become tyrannical. The previous section described dispositifs of visibility -- exposed labor, reciprocal evaluation, shared archive. But these dispositifs carry within them a danger: that of generalized surveillance.

### The Tyranny of Transparency

Michel Foucault analyzed the examination as the central dispositif of disciplinary power: the examiner sees without being seen, judges without being judged. The exposed teacher subverts this dispositif by entering the field of visibility themselves. They transform the gaze of surveillance into a gaze of reciprocal verification. But a permanent recording of the process risks producing a "staging" of acceptable doubt. Students learn to expose presentable doubts while preserving their most vital dead ends in opaque zones. The permanent gaze transforms the space of creation into a space of representation; the genuinely risky fragilities migrate off-stage. Total transparency produces its opposite: a second-degree opacity. One shows what one is willing to show; the rest is concealed all the more carefully because it is supposed to be visible. This is the paradox of the "transparency society" described by Byung-Chul Han: the more visibility is demanded, the more strategic dissimulation is produced. The exposed teacher is not a transparent teacher. They choose what they expose; they protect what must remain in shadow. The visibility of work is not total visibility -- it is a selective, assumed, negotiated visibility.

### Articulating Transparency and Opacity: The Right to Shadow

Edouard Glissant claims the "right to opacity" -- not the right to conceal, but the right not to be entirely translated, entirely explicated, entirely reduced to transparency. In *Poetics of Relation* (1990), he formulates this demand with a clarity that resonates directly with our discussion:

> "Agree not merely to the right to difference but, carrying this further, agree also to the right to opacity that is not enclosure within an impenetrable autarchy but subsistence within an irreducible singularity. [...] Opacities can coexist and converge, weaving fabrics. To understand these truly one must focus on the texture of the weave and not on the nature of its components. [...] We clamor for the right to opacity for everyone." [^glissant_opacite]

Glissantian opacity is not the darkness of one who hides; it is the irreducibility of one who cannot be entirely understood from the outside -- and who does not need to be in order to enter into relation. It is a concept rooted in the history of slavery and colonization, where imposed "transparency" was an instrument of domination: to make the other legible was to control them. The student needs a space of groping that escapes the evaluative gaze. Creation demands zones of non-visibility -- moments when one can make mistakes without witnesses, explore dead ends without having to justify them, play without stakes. The exposed teacher does not demand that everything be visible at all times. They assume the responsibility of delineating, with the students, protected spaces of experimentation. Opacity is not an arbitrary privilege, but a function of the research phase: certain moments of "making" must remain withdrawn, while phases of "rendering account" demand illumination. Emmanuel Nal rightly names this articulation: "The teacher neither abdicates their role nor their responsibility, but *breaks at least for a moment with verticality to show that they too, at their own level, remain on the path*." [^21] The suspension is provisional -- but it is in this provisional space that thought opens.

[^glissant_opacite]: Edouard GLISSANT, *Poetique de la Relation*, Paris, Gallimard, 1990, p. 204 and p. 209. Eng. trans.: *Poetics of Relation*, trans. Betsy Wing, Ann Arbor, University of Michigan Press, 1997, p. 190 and p. 194.

[^21]: Emmanuel NAL, "Favoriser l'eveil a la demarche philosophique...," op. cit., par. 7.

### Opacity as Earned Responsibility -- and Its Limits

The teacher earns the right to provide this protection only by first exposing their own vulnerability. It is through their transparency that they become credible when they say to the student: "Here, you don't have to show." bell hooks insists on this point: "In my classrooms, I do not expect students to take any risks that I would not take, to share in any way that I would not share. [...] Professors who expect students to share confessional narratives but who are themselves unwilling to share are exercising power in a manner that could be coercive." [^hooks_confessional] To ask students to expose themselves while remaining in retreat oneself is to exercise power coercively. Vulnerability is not weakness; it is a condition of legitimacy.

[^hooks_confessional]: bell HOOKS, *Teaching to Transgress: Education as the Practice of Freedom*, New York, Routledge, 1994, p. 21.

This is the central paradox: the teacher earns the right to protect the students' opacity by first exposing their own work. They can only say "you don't have to show" because they have first shown themselves. This articulation between the teacher's transparency and the student's protected opacity extends Glissant's "right to opacity." The exposed teacher does not claim to show everything -- they show what they choose to show, from their singular position. Their exposure is not a total nudity; it is an assumed selection. Nal speaks in this regard of a "*lesson in aporia, which shows that the latter is not necessarily a flaw, but a result* that perhaps simply calls [...] for reflection to be a reflection in time, the time of a maturation [...] which is *humility before what there is to understand*." [^22] Aporia is not a pedagogical failure; it is a result -- the most honest result the exposed teacher can offer.

[^22]: Emmanuel NAL, "Favoriser l'eveil a la demarche philosophique...," op. cit., par. 6.

And it is precisely because they assume this selection -- because they show that they themselves do not show everything -- that they can legitimately offer students the same right to selection. Protected opacity is therefore not a failure of transparency but a condition of singularity. The student who preserves a zone of shadow does not hide out of strategy; they protect the space from which they will later be able to contribute to the commons with their own perspective. Glissant again: opacity is not what one refuses to say; it is what exceeds the sayable -- the irreducible of each being, which cannot and need not be entirely translated. The opacity protected by the exposed teacher is therefore not the opacity of the traditional "master" -- who hides their process to better establish their authority. It is an opacity earned through the teacher's prior transparency, and negotiated collectively with the students. The distinction is not between persons (the transparent teacher, the opaque student) but between phases: the phase of making (opaque) and the phase of rendering account (transparent). However, an objection arises: the power to decide when one passes from shadow to light remains, in practice, largely in the teacher's hands. It is they who "organize" the workshop, who set the calendar, who announce: "Now we move to the restitution phase." This decision -- when to expose, when to protect -- is itself a form of power. How to democratize this power of decision?

Several paths are conceivable. The first consists in explicating and collectively negotiating the calendar of phases: the group decides together when restitution will take place, when archiving will begin. The second consists in giving each participant a veto right over what concerns them: no one can be exposed without their explicit consent. The third consists in institutionalizing guaranteed "free zones" -- moments when opacity is assured by the structure itself, independent of the teacher's decision. These dispositifs do not entirely eliminate the teacher's power -- they remain the one who proposes the framework, even if it can be negotiated. But they limit arbitrariness and make power explicit, and therefore contestable.

### Honneth and Mutual Recognition

Axel Honneth, in *The Struggle for Recognition* (1992), proposes a theoretical framework that anchors pedagogical symmetry in a broader social philosophy. For Honneth, social conflicts are not primarily conflicts of interest but conflicts of recognition. Individuals and groups struggle to be recognized in their dignity, their rights, their contributions. Honneth distinguishes three spheres of recognition: love (affective recognition), rights (juridical recognition), social esteem (recognition of singular contribution). The pedagogical relationship is hybrid: it is not love (family), nor purely juridical (citizenship). It belongs to social esteem founded on contribution. Yet in a class, contributions are unequal. The teacher contributes through their experience, the student through their freshness or their work. Must recognition be symmetrical (equal) or proportional to contribution? The proposition defended here is that symmetry must bear on the right to recognition -- the right to be recognized as a subject capable of contribution -- and not on the equality of contributions themselves. The student can only recognize the teacher as a peer-researcher if they are themselves recognized as a peer-critic. For symmetry to be authentic, it must be structural: the teacher must need the recognition of the students, not out of feigned humility but because this recognition is constitutive of their own identity as researcher.

But mutual recognition does not play out solely on the epistemic plane. The teacher is, in practice, often confronted with another dimension of student life: precarity, fatigue, sometimes psychological distress. Cost of living, unstable housing, part-time work encroaching on studies, chronic exhaustion, anxiety, depression -- these realities traverse the studio without being formally invited. The student who cannot manage to produce is not always experiencing "creative block"; they are sometimes simply exhausted, or unable to concentrate because they don't know how they will pay their rent. The teacher is not trained to respond to these situations. They are not a social worker, nor a psychologist, nor a financial advisor. But they are often the first to perceive them -- because they see the student regularly, because the studio is a space where defenses fall more easily than in a lecture hall, because the artistic working relationship involves a proximity that other pedagogical formats do not have. What to do with this de facto position?

The task is therefore to avoid denial -- acting as though these realities do not exist, evaluating work as though everyone started from the same conditions. Confusion of roles is also a pitfall to avoid: transforming oneself into an improvised therapist, taking on what one lacks the skills to treat. Finally, instrumentalizing and using student precarity as pedagogical material or as a pretext for lowering expectations is clearly a red line.

The exposed teacher recognizes that these realities exist and that they affect work -- they do not pretend not to see them. They adjust, when possible, the modalities of work (deadlines, formats, intensity). They direct toward competent services (social workers, health services, aid programs) without claiming to substitute for them. And above all, they refuse to make the student alone bear responsibility for conditions that exceed them. This attention to the material conditions of student existence is not a bonus; it is a condition of recognition in Honneth's sense. To recognize the student as a subject capable of contribution is also to recognize that this capacity is affected by conditions over which they have no control. Epistemic symmetry presupposes a form of material solidarity -- even if this solidarity, to be effective, must be borne by the institution and not by the teacher alone.

## VI. Political Conditions of Exposure

The previous sections have described a figure — the exposed teacher — and the dispositifs that make it possible: visible labor, reciprocal evaluation, co-production of tools, articulation between transparency and opacity. But this description risks remaining abstract if it does not take into account the concrete conditions in which these dispositifs are inscribed. Higher education is not a neutral space: it is traversed by power relations — statutory, economic, gendered, racial — that determine who can afford to take risks and who cannot. A tenured teacher in a national school does not inhabit the same professional world as an adjunct paid by the hour, without an office, without assurance of renewal. A racialized woman teacher whose competence is structurally called into question does not have the same margin of maneuver as a white male professor whose authority is presumed from the outset. To propose vulnerability as a pedagogical posture without interrogating these material asymmetries is to transform an emancipatory demand into yet another injunction — one more — that weighs on those who are already the most exposed. This section therefore examines the political conditions without which the exposed teacher remains an inaccessible ideal, or even an additional violence disguised as freedom.

### The Unequal Distribution of Vulnerability

A tenured teacher who exposes their failures does not take the same risk as a contract teacher. A recognized professor does not take the same risk as an early-career lecturer. Vulnerability is not equally accessible: it depends on status, on position within the hierarchy, but also on gender, race, class. Those who are already in a position of institutional fragility can hardly afford to add an epistemic fragility.

Showing one's work in progress means taking a risk. This risk is not equally distributed. Adrienne Rich formulated the flip side of this exposure: "When someone with the authority of a teacher, say, describes the world and you are not in it, there is a *moment of psychic disequilibrium*, as if you looked in the mirror and saw nothing. It takes some strength of soul — and not just individual strength, but *collective understanding* — to resist this void, this *non-being*, into which you are thrust, and to stand up, demanding to be seen and heard." [^23] The exposed teacher must also be the one who makes visible those whom the system renders invisible.

[^23]: Adrienne RICH, *Blood, Bread, and Poetry: Selected Prose 1979–1985*, New York, Norton, 1986. Cited in Åke SJÖBERG *et al.*, "Who is in the Classroom?," *TTT Toolbox*, April 25, 2021.

Research on implicit bias in evaluation shows that women teachers, racialized teachers, and precarious teachers are judged more severely than their male, white, tenured counterparts. Exposing one's doubts when one benefits from a presumption of competence is not the same thing as exposing one's doubts when that presumption is already weakened by structural biases. It would be naive — and potentially violent — to make exposure a generalized injunction. The exposed teacher is only possible if the institution genuinely protects those who take this risk. Otherwise, we tip into the neoliberal injunction to transparency: "Be authentic, show your vulnerability" — but without a safety net, and at your own risk. This injunction makes individuals bear the cost of their own exposure, while claiming to liberate them.

### The Student's Double Bind

On the student's side too, symmetry can become a double bind. "Be critical of the teacher — but don't forget they grade you." "Participate in co-production — but know that the teacher remains the guarantor of the framework." "Express your disagreement — but the teacher has the last word on what enters the archive." These contradictory injunctions can produce anxiety rather than emancipation. The student who takes the invitation to critique seriously may find themselves in an impossible position: if they truly critique, they risk displeasing the one who holds the power of certification; if they do not critique, they betray the symmetry contract that was proposed to them. Bourdieu and Passeron, in *The Inheritors* (1964), showed that "invisible pedagogy" — the kind that feigns not exercising authority — is often the most violent, because it masks power relations instead of making them explicit. The student from the working class, who does not master the implicit codes of this "relaxed" pedagogy, is all the more disarmed because they do not know what rules they should conform to. There is a risk that the displayed symmetry of the exposed teacher functions in the same way: a façade equality that masks the persistence of structural inequalities. To avoid this trap, symmetry must be structurally guaranteed by explicit dispositifs — and not merely proclaimed by the teacher's goodwill.

### Institutional Responsibility

This implies an institutional responsibility. Art schools that wish to promote this model must guarantee working conditions that make it possible: job stability, protection against retaliation, recognition of pedagogical risk-taking in career evaluations. Without these guarantees, evaluative symmetry remains a privilege of those who can afford it — that is, precisely those who need it least. For, as Meyerhoff analyzes following Walter Mignolo, "the dominant tendency in university studies has been to present the problems or crises of higher education as analytical and moral questions that could be resolved through rational debate and persuasion. This approach tends to take on an expert position — what Walter Mignolo calls a '*zero-point*' position '*above*' the world. [...] Adopting such a position has *depoliticizing effects*, because it forecloses consideration of how one's own position is implicated in producing the problem." [^24] The exposed teacher refuses precisely this "zero position."

[^24]: Eli MEYERHOFF, *Beyond Education*, op. cit., p. 5–6.

Institutional responsibility cannot remain a moral injunction; it must translate into concrete dispositifs. Several avenues can be envisaged.
Career evaluation criteria: Recruitment and promotion committees could integrate criteria that value pedagogical risk-taking — not only "innovations" (a suspect managerial term) but experiments, including those that did not "work." A candidacy file could include a "formative failures" section where the candidate analyzes what they learned from unsuccessful attempts. This valorization of reflective failure would align with the exposed teacher model.

Statutory protections: Institutions could guarantee that student evaluations (mandatory within the framework of accreditation) will not be used against teachers who practice reciprocal evaluation. A teacher who exposes themselves to structured student critique is taking a risk; this risk must not be turned against them in administrative procedures.

Institutional time: The co-production of tools requires time — time that is not counted in traditional teaching loads. Institutions could recognize this time as an integral part of the pedagogical workload, on the same footing as course preparation or student supervision.

Teacher training: The dispositifs of the exposed teacher are not spontaneous; they must be learned. Specific training — on facilitating collective critique, on the governance of commons, on the articulation of transparency and opacity — could be offered, not as injunctions but as resources.

These proposals do not resolve the structural contradictions of the neoliberal university (quantitative evaluation, competition, precarization). But they open spaces of resistance — "free zones" where other practices become possible. The exposed teacher will not come about by decree; they will be constructed in the interstices that institutions are willing to leave open — or that collectives manage to force.

The co-production of tools is itself politically situated. Who has the time to participate in the elaboration of a common glossary? Who masters the academic codes sufficiently to propose references? The student who works alongside their studies, the one who did not grow up in a cultured milieu, the one whose mother tongue is not the language of instruction — all of them start with a disadvantage in "co-production." Vigilance is necessary to ensure that co-production does not reproduce, under the guise of symmetry, the inequalities it claims to combat. A possible response: explicit compensatory dispositifs. Time reserved for co-production, integrated into the curriculum. Mediations to facilitate the participation of those who master the codes less well. Particular attention to silences — who is not speaking? Why? Authentic co-production is not spontaneous; it is built through deliberate work of inclusion.

## VII. The Exposed Teacher in the Age of Machines

Until now, this text has constructed its proposition — the exposed teacher — in dialogue with the history of art pedagogy, the philosophy of education, and the theory of commons. Artificial intelligence has appeared as a horizon, an accelerating factor, an element of the landscape. It must now be placed at the center.

For what has been at stake since 2022 — the date of the mass diffusion of generative models — is not simply one more crisis in the long history of crises of transmission. It is a change of regime. For the first time, a machine is capable of producing, in a few seconds, structured texts, plausible images, argued analyses, formal propositions that, at first glance, resemble what the teacher produces in hours, weeks, months of work. The result is there — fluid, abundant, immediately available. What is missing is everything else: the doubt, the revision, the dead end, the bifurcation, the dialogue with material that resists.

The dominant discourse on AI in education oscillates between two equally insufficient poles. On one side, panic: AI cheats, AI plagiarizes, AI makes students lazy — we must ban, surveil, detect. This conservative reaction reduces teaching to evaluation, and evaluation to the verification of an authenticity whose criteria were already fragile before AI. On the other, enthusiasm: AI is a formidable tool, it personalizes learning, it frees the teacher from repetitive tasks — we must adopt, integrate, train in its use. This techno-optimist reaction reduces teaching to the transmission of information, and AI to a tutor more patient and more available than any human. Neither panic nor enthusiasm touches the real problem. The problem is not that the machine "knows" more than the teacher — it knows nothing, in the strong sense of the term. The problem is that it produces the appearance of knowledge with an efficiency that renders invisible the difference between a generated result and a searched-for result. It flattens the process onto the product. It erases the thirty drafts, the abandoned dead ends, the nights of doubt, the revisions after a failure — everything that constitutes, precisely, the visible labor that the exposed teacher places at the heart of their pedagogy.

It is here that the proposition of this text takes its full relief. If the function of the teacher is to transmit knowledge — to answer questions, to provide contents, to evaluate productions — then this function can be assumed, perhaps better assumed, by a machine. The teacher-transmitter is structurally outmatched by the LLM: the machine is faster, more available, does not tire, does not grow impatient, does not judge. But if the function of the teacher is to show their work in progress — their gropings, their revisions, their choices, their vulnerability before the unknown — then the machine cannot replace them. Not because it would be "less intelligent," but because it has no work in progress. It has only results. It does not search; it calculates. It does not grope; it optimizes. It does not make mistakes — it hallucinates, which is an entirely different matter: error presupposes a subject who aims at truth and misses it; hallucination is a statistically coherent but semantically empty production, which does not even know it is wrong.

### The Machine That "Knows" and the Subject Who Searches

The claim that "the machine knows more" deserves philosophical critique. Attributing "knowledge" to a large language model (*LLM*) is a misuse of language: an LLM manipulates statistical probabilities between tokens; it has neither intentionality, nor semantic understanding, nor experience of the world. To say it "knows" is to reduce knowledge to stored and retrievable information. However, the argument works on a pragmatic level: for the student, AI seems to know. It provides answers, images, essay outlines with formidable efficiency. Consequently, if the teacher positions themselves as a provider of answers, they are structurally outmatched by the machine. They become obsolete. Ingold, rereading Dewey, reminds us however that ideas do not function as transferable data: "It is simply impossible, Dewey insists, for the beliefs and attitudes that a social group cultivates in its immature members to be '*hammered in*' or '*plastered on*'; they cannot be '*physically extracted and inserted*' [...]. You might be able to do such things with material entities like nails, teeth and germs, but *not with ideas whose very formation depends on experience*." [^25] AI can transfer tokens; it cannot grow knowledge.

[^25]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, op. cit., p. 9.

This objection reinforces the proposition of the exposed teacher. The LLM realizes the fantasy of banking education: it deposits knowledge without body, without doubt, without vulnerability. It cannot be evaluated, cannot fail, cannot be transformed through dialogue. Ingold formulates the danger with precision: "Far from overcoming the presumed inequality of intelligence, *the logic of reason and inheritance reproduces it*." [^26] AI, which "explains" to a user presumed ignorant, structurally reproduces this logic.

[^26]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, op. cit., p. 14–15.

Faced with this machinic "perfection," the value of human teaching resides in what the machine cannot simulate: labor, incompleteness, risk-taking, co-production.

### Vilém Flusser and the Critique of Black Boxes

Vilém Flusser (1920–1991), philosopher of exile and media, offers precious conceptual tools for thinking AI in art education. In his work on photography and the technical image, Flusser defines the "apparatus" (the camera, the computer) as a "black box" whose program is inaccessible to the user. The user — the photographer, the digital artist — merely exhausts the potentialities of the program. They believe they are creating; in reality, they are realizing possibilities foreseen by the apparatus. This analysis, formulated in the 1980s, resonates strangely with the emergence of generative AIs. When Flusser speaks of the automaticity of the technical image, he describes in advance the functioning of Midjourney or DALL-E. The user of these tools "prompts"; but the program determines the space of possibilities. "Creativity" is reduced to the exploration of a space parameterized by others. To truly create, according to Flusser, one must "play against the apparatus" — subvert its program, divert it from its intended purposes, reveal its limits and biases. It is a practice of *hacking* rather than use. The exposed teacher can take up this injunction: faced with AI, the point is not to use it naively or to reject it wholesale, but to put it in crisis — to expose its black boxes. Showing one's work also means showing how one works with — and against — machines. The exposed teacher does not hide that they use digital tools; they show how they use them, what resistances they oppose to them, what limits they discover. Yves Citton, in *Arts, sciences, technologies. Défis à la recherche-création* (2025), reactivates Flusser to think the "surpassing" of institutionalized research-creation. Research-creation, as it has been normalized in universities, risks becoming a "transmission belt for the culture industry" or a "humanist decoration for the hard sciences." Flusser offers an alternative path: the artist-researcher as "disciplinary exile," who is at home nowhere and who, from this uncomfortable position, can interrogate the apparatuses that govern us.

### Chronopolitics: Time as a Stake of Struggle

The collective Black Quantum Futurism (BQF), founded in Philadelphia by the artist Camae Ayewa (Moor Mother) and the lawyer Rasheedah Phillips, proposes a still more radical critique. Their concept of "chronopolitics" designates the way time is distributed, valued, and used as a tool of oppression. Predictive algorithms — used by police, banks, real estate agencies — freeze the future of marginalized communities by projecting biased historical data forward. Phillips calls this "AI fascism": a regimentation of temporality that eliminates contingency in favor of probability. Against this algorithmic capture of time, BQF proposes an "education in temporal literacy": teaching communities to decode the mechanisms by which their time is stolen or predetermined. The Community Futures Lab, opened in 2016 in a Philadelphia neighborhood threatened by gentrification, functioned as a hybrid between community center, art gallery, and time capsule. The "future visioning" workshops did not separate art from survival; participants used concepts from quantum physics and Afrofuturism as practical tools for "escaping the time loops" of poverty. For the exposed teacher, chronopolitics has a direct implication: showing one's work also means showing that work takes time. It means resisting the illusion of instantaneity. It means affirming that thought needs duration to unfold. Faced with AI, which crushes process onto result, the teacher who exposes their thirty successive drafts practices a form of temporal resistance.

This temporal resistance however engages a more profound mutation of the teaching posture. The computational era does not merely modify the rhythm of production; it transforms the very status of the tools through which knowledge is constituted, transmitted, and verified. The language model is not a transparent instrument that one "uses" as one uses a pencil — it is an apparatus in the Flusserian sense, whose program shapes the space of possibilities unbeknownst to the user. The teacher who merely uses AI without interrogating its conditions of production — the training data, the biases of representation, the political economy of computation — reproduces, before computational tools, the alienation that Simondon diagnosed as early as 1958 before industrial machines: "The most powerful cause of alienation in the contemporary world resides in this misunderstanding of the machine, which is not an alienation caused by the machine, but by the non-knowledge of its nature and its essence, by its absence from the world of meanings, and by its omission from the table of values and concepts forming part of culture." [^simondon_alienation] This alienation through misunderstanding is all the more insidious in that AI, unlike the industrial machine, does not present itself as foreign to culture — it speaks our language, it produces our images, it simulates our thought. Its opacity is not that of a mysterious gear mechanism but that of a distorting mirror mistaken for a window.

[^simondon_alienation]: Gilbert SIMONDON, *Du mode d'existence des objets techniques*, Paris, Aubier, 1958; repr. Paris, Flammarion, "Champs," 2012, p. 9–10.

The exposed teacher is therefore called to a double competence that belongs neither to technical expertise nor to simple "digital literacy" — that too-weak term which reduces the question to a user's skill set. They must be at once the one who plays and the one who interrogates the instrument. Simondon proposed the figure of the "mechanologist" — neither the engineer who designs nor the worker who operates, but the thinker who understands the mode of existence of the technical object and reintroduces it into culture. The exposed teacher in the computational era inherits this figure: they are neither the developer who programs the language model, nor the user who prompts without critical distance, but the one who, from within their research-creation practice, opens the black box before their students — shows what the machine does, what it cannot do, what it does without being asked. Tim Ingold, for his part, showed that making is not the imposition of a preconceived form on passive matter — it is a "correspondence" between practitioner, materials, and tools, a process of growth whose outcome is never entirely predetermined [^ingold_making]. This conception of making applies decisively to AI: working with a language model means entering into correspondence with a material whose properties are only partially known, whose responses surprise, resist, deviate. The teacher who shows this correspondence in action — their prompt attempts, their reformulations, their surprises before hallucinations, their decisions to keep or discard a result — practices what Simondon calls "technical culture": not the mastery of the tool, but the understanding of its mode of existence.

[^ingold_making]: Tim INGOLD, *Making: Anthropology, Archaeology, Art and Architecture*, London, Routledge, 2013, p. 21.

What the computational era thus produces is not the dissolution of the distinction between creation and analysis, between practice and theory — but their irreversible convergence within a single gesture. Jean-Paul Fourmentraux, in *antiDATA: la désobéissance numérique* (2020) and *Sousveillance: L'œil du contre-pouvoir* (2023), has mapped the artistic practices that "bite the machine" — practices that articulate three simultaneous gestures: bricolage in Lévi-Strauss's sense (working with the means at hand, exploiting the *glitch*, the bug, the low resolution), poaching in Michel de Certeau's sense (entering a system to divert its intended uses), and profanation in Agamben's sense (restoring to common use what had been separated into the sphere of the sacred — or here, of the proprietary black box). The exposed teacher in the computational era directly inherits this triple posture. They can no longer content themselves with creating with digital tools without interrogating their conditions of production — the training data, the encoded biases, the political economy of computation. But nor can they retreat into the sole posture of critique, analyzing the machine from outside without ever experiencing its resistances, surprises, hallucinations. Fourmentraux names this articulation "technocritical hacktivism" — a civic disobedience that aims not to destroy the digital infrastructure but to make it legible, contestable, appropriable. The teacher who shows their students how they work with and against a language model — their prompt attempts, their reformulations, their decisions to keep or discard a result, their deliberate diversions — practices this disobedience as a pedagogical method. They present themselves neither as a code expert nor as a naive user, but as a practitioner who interrogates the apparatus from within its use, and who exposes this interrogation as an integral part of their research work. This posture is incompatible with expertise — the expert possesses their domain and surveys it from what Meyerhoff, after Mignolo, calls the "zero position." The exposed teacher crosses domains without possessing any of them, from the uncomfortable but fertile position of the one who, in Flusser's phrase, "plays against the apparatus" — not to destroy it, but to reveal its program and to extend its possibilities beyond what that program had foreseen.

### Digital Disobedience: Another Critical Posture

Fourmentraux's cartography gains in precision when we examine the concrete practices of the artists he analyzes — Trevor Paglen, Paolo Cirio, Julien Prévieux, Hito Steyerl, among others. Humor and parody occupy a central place: the point is not to destroy the machine, but to desacralize it — to profane it in Agamben's sense, that is, to "restore to common use what had been separated into the sphere of the sacred" [^agamben_profanation] — here, the sphere of the proprietary black box, of the untouchable algorithm, of the inaccessible dataset. This approach has direct pedagogical implications.

[^agamben_profanation]: Giorgio AGAMBEN, *Profanations*, trans. Jeff Fort, New York, Zone Books, 2007, p. 73.

Instead of banning AI (conservative reflex) or celebrating it (technophile reflex), the research-creation laboratory uses it to dissect its limits. One generates thousands of images to reveal the sexist or racist stereotypes of the database — as Trevor Paglen does with his work on training datasets. One diverts prompts to produce "impossible" images that the algorithm should not generate. One documents AI hallucinations to make them the material of a critical reflection on what "seeing" means for a machine. Fourmentraux insists: this disobedience is civic, non-violent, concerned with the general interest. It aims not to destroy the digital infrastructure but to make it legible, contestable, appropriable. The artists he studies produce what he calls "counter-dispositifs" — works that turn the tools of surveillance against themselves, that make visible what was hidden. The exposed teacher, in this perspective, does not simply teach how to use AI, nor how to reject it. They teach how to open it — to understand how it works, what biases it carries, what possibilities it offers and what limits it imposes. The pedagogical gesture is not frontal resistance but deautomatization: learning to see what the machine does without being asked, and to do what it is not programmed to produce.

### Vulnerability and Power

AI embodies absolute asymmetry: a source of knowledge that cannot be questioned, that renders no accounts, that does not expose itself. The human teacher who refuses evaluative symmetry places themselves, structurally, on the side of the machine. They become an LLM with a body — a dispenser of knowledge who stands outside the process they organize. But it would be reductive to define the human solely by lack in the face of a machine defined by plenitude. The exposed teacher does not only expose their doubts; they also expose their power — their capacity to reason in the face of uncertainty, to construct concepts that the machine, prisoner of its past training data, cannot invent. "Research" is not only a vulnerable groping; it is also a power of irruption into the real, a capacity to pose questions that no one has yet asked. The exposed teacher affirms what the machine cannot be: a vulnerable and powerful subject, engaged in research whose outcome is uncertain, capable of co-producing tools with those they accompany. They do not merely deliver answers; they put at stake their own position as a subject in research. It is this gesture — showing oneself traversed by the unfinished while affirming the capacity to traverse it — that opens, for the student, the possibility of an emancipated relationship to knowledge.

### The Studio as a Laboratory of Polychrony

In a world saturated by generative efficiency (producing an image in 3 seconds), art education becomes the conservatory of temporal inefficiency. Visible labor, drafts, revisions are valued not for their results, but as traces of a resistance to machine-time. But opposing speed (AI) to slowness (human) is too simple a binary — and a class privilege. Not everyone can afford to "slow down." AI does not merely move fast; it operates a recursive loop — it predicts the future from the past, it freezes trajectories by calculating the probable. Resistance is not slowness, but a change of time. A question then arises: must one use AI in order to develop a critical reflection on it? The implicit injunction — "one must practice in order to have the right to criticize" — deserves interrogation. One does not need to drive a car to analyze the impact of the automobile on urbanism. One does not need to own a smartphone to understand the attention economy. Distance can be a critical resource as much as a handicap. Several pedagogical paths make it possible to work on AI without necessarily using it:

Analysis of outputs. One can study images generated by AI without having produced them oneself — collecting, classifying, analyzing their stylistic recurrences, their representational biases, their aesthetic "tics." The student becomes a corpus analyst rather than a machine operator. This approach echoes the methods of art history and semiology: one does not need to paint to analyze a painting.
Deconstruction of conditions of use. Reading the terms of service of a generative AI service, studying its business model, mapping the data flows and value chains — this is critical work that requires no image generation. The work of Kate Crawford (*Atlas of AI*, 2021) or Matteo Pasquinelli shows that one can produce a materialist critique of AI without being a user.
Study of datasets. Training databases (ImageNet, LAION, etc.) are objects of study in themselves. Who collected these images? Under what conditions? What representational biases do they carry? Trevor Paglen and Kate Crawford exhibited *ImageNet Roulette* (2019) to reveal the racist and sexist categories of datasets used to train image recognition systems — a critical work that proceeds through analysis, not generation.
Testimony and inquiry. Interviewing AI users, documenting their practices, collecting their doubts and discoveries — this is an ethnographic method that allows one to understand AI "from the inside" without being an operator oneself.

This approach has its limits. Someone who has never prompted does not know the strange sensation of seeing the machine "understand" or "hallucinate." There is an embodied knowledge of use that distanced analysis cannot replace. But this limit does not invalidate the approach; it situates it. Critical analysis and reflexive use are two complementary, not exclusive, paths. The exposed teacher can therefore propose both — or one without the other, depending on available resources, the group's competences, the ethical objections of participants. The important thing is that reflection on AI not be conditioned on its use. One can refuse to use Midjourney for ecological reasons (energy consumption), legal reasons (copyright of training images), or ethical reasons (working conditions of moderators) — and still develop a critical thought about what these tools do to the regime of images.

More than a zone of deceleration, the studio becomes a laboratory of polychrony. Against the linear, predictive time of algorithms, the exposed teacher and students activate ancestral and speculative technologies — like those of Black Quantum Futurism — to bring into existence unarrived pasts and non-probable futures. The studio is a site where several temporalities coexist: machine-time (instantaneous, recursive), body-time (fatigue, attention, rhythm), ancestral time (memory, heritage, debt), project-time (maturation, revision, completion). The teacher learns to synchronize or desynchronize these times, not merely to slow down. This position echoes contemporary ecological and ethical concerns about care and maintenance, against disruptive innovation. But it goes further: the point is not to protect a "natural" time against an "artificial" one, but to multiply the available temporal regimes — to make the studio capable of accommodating heterogeneous times without hierarchizing them. AI can have its place in this polychrony — provided it is not the only clock. Using a language model to explore a concept during two hours of discussion means superimposing machine-time (instantaneous response) on the time of deliberation (collective maturation). The stake is not to reject one in favor of the other, but to articulate them consciously.

### AI as a Tool of Disembodiment

The critique of AI as "absolute asymmetry" must not mask its potential as a tool for the disembodiment of knowledge. If the problem of traditional teaching is that knowledge remains lodged in the charismatic person of the "master," AI can contribute to detaching it — not to replace the teacher, but to make knowledge accessible otherwise. Consider a concrete example: a chatbot trained on the archives of an institution (correspondences, reports, publications). This dispositif does not replace the teacher; it makes the corpus accessible without going through their mediation. The student can interrogate the sources directly, formulate their own questions, construct their own reading paths. The teacher is no longer the guardian of the corpus but a companion of exploration — someone who knows the terrain but does not control access to it.

This configuration transforms the pedagogical relationship. The asymmetry of access to information diminishes; the asymmetry of interpretive experience remains. The teacher does not know "more" than the machine about the facts; they know otherwise — they have traversed the corpus, they have experienced its tensions, they can put in relation what the machine juxtaposes. Their authority no longer comes from what they possess (information) but from what they have lived through (research experience).

AI can also become a tool of co-production. A group collectively building a glossary can use a language model to explore the uses of a term, identify contradictions, propose alternative formulations. AI does not arbitrate; it enriches the material from which the group decides. It functions as a "documentary third" — an externalized memory that frees attention for interpretive work. This integration of AI into the pedagogy of the exposed teacher presupposes precise conditions. The tool must be transparent (one knows what it was trained on, what its limits are), accessible to all (no digital divide within the group), and subject to collective critique (its responses can be contested, its biases identified). AI is not an oracle; it is a tool — and like any tool, it must be co-produced or at least co-evaluated by those who use it.

### The Laboratory as a Space of Counter-Expertise

If the teacher is exposed and the relationship symmetrical, what does the class concretely look like? Tim Ingold proposed a formulation that illuminates this question: authentic education is a matter of *exposure*, not *immunization* [^ingold_exposure]. The immunized class — the one that bans AI, that filters content, that protects the student from any contact with what might "contaminate" them — produces only ignorance. The exposed class, by contrast, deliberately opens itself to what destabilizes it, and it is from this destabilization that it draws its power of knowledge.

[^ingold_exposure]: The term is Jan Masschelein's, taken up by Ingold: "He gave me the sense of education — as a leading out into the world calling for attention and exposure — that I needed to prove its purpose for anthropology." (Tim INGOLD, interview with Robert Campbell, "An Interview with Tim Ingold: Educational Freedom, the Craft of Writing, and the University," *Philosophasters*, 2018.) See also Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, London, Routledge, 2018, ch. 1.

This exposed class is not, however, an anomic space. For it to function as a laboratory of counter-expertise, it must bring together what Joëlle Zask, rereading Dewey, identifies as the three conditions of authentic participation: "taking part" (freely engaging in the collective inquiry), "contributing a part" (contributing from one's irreducible singularity), and "receiving a part" (benefiting from the group's recognition of this contribution) [^zask_participer]. Counter-expertise arises only when these three dimensions are united — when the student is not merely present (taking part), but effectively contributes to the production of a knowledge that the group recognizes and from which it can benefit. Without this tripartition, "participation" remains what Zask calls a debased form: one "appears" as a participant in a dispositif whose purposes escape one.

[^zask_participer]: Joëlle ZASK, *Participer. Essai sur les formes démocratiques de la participation*, Latresne, Le Bord de l'eau, 2011, esp. p. 24–25 (taking part), p. 151–166 (contributing), and p. 209 sq. (benefiting).

The first gesture of this laboratory is the analysis of biases. Instead of banning AI or celebrating it, the group uses it as a terrain of investigation. One generates thousands of images to reveal the stereotypes encoded in training databases — overrepresentation of certain phenotypes, invisibilization of others, aesthetic standardization that flattens visual traditions onto a recognizable "AI style." This work echoes the research of Kate Crawford and Trevor Paglen, whose exhibition *ImageNet Roulette* (2019) revealed the racist and sexist categories of the datasets used to train image recognition systems. The student who conducts this type of inquiry does not merely "critique AI" — they produce original knowledge about the conditions of production of contemporary images, knowledge that would not exist without the intersection of artistic competence (knowing how to look) and analytical competence (knowing how to deconstruct).

The second gesture is what might be called re-corporation. Generative models produce images without body, texts without voice, formal propositions without the resistance of material — and above all without the duration of the work that made them possible. The laboratory deliberately reintroduces body, friction, situation. Not by returning to painting or craft as refuges against the machine — that would confirm the separation between "the human" and "the technical" that Simondon was already denouncing — but by forcing the encounter between the computational output and what it cannot calculate. A group that submits the same prompt to ten different models, prints the results, and arranges them in physical space to compare them physically produces knowledge about aesthetic standardization that the screen alone does not make visible. A performance in which the student's body replays, in real time and in fatigue, a sequence the machine generated in three seconds makes perceptible the gap between the time of calculation and the time of experience. A protocol in which the group records its own voices reading a text generated by an LLM, then confronts the recording with the original text, makes audible what the machine cannot carry: hesitation, breath, accent, the slip of the tongue. These dispositifs do not "resist" the machine through slowness or through noble material; they put it to the test of the collective body, and this test produces knowledge — about the machine, about the body, and about what is lost or transformed in the passage from one to the other.

The third gesture concerns evaluation. In a laboratory of counter-expertise, evaluation can no longer bear on the final result — which may be visually stunning thanks to AI while being intellectually empty. A student who generates a spectacular image in three prompts has learned nothing; a student who documents thirty failed attempts, analyzes why the machine systematically produces a certain type of bias, and proposes a strategy of circumvention has produced research work. Evaluation therefore shifts from product to process: the quality of the inquiry, the pertinence of questions posed to the machine, the rigor of the documentation, the capacity to articulate practical experience with a conceptual framework. This shift directly echoes the proposition of the exposed teacher: just as the teacher shows their labor and not merely their results, the student is evaluated on the quality of their research and not on the brilliance of their productions.

The fourth gesture, finally, touches on the status of language. Generative AI compels us to revise the theory/practice dichotomy in a way that neither Dewey nor Ingold had anticipated. When "to say is to do" — when to prompt is to generate — language ceases to be a commentary on the world and becomes an operative code whose effects are immediately visible. The prompt is not a neutral instruction; it is a technical gesture that engages lexical choices, cultural presuppositions, rhetorical strategies — and whose results reveal as much the biases of the machine as those of the user. The exposed teacher shows how language acts upon digital material, and conversely, how the machine's hallucinations compel the reformulation of our theoretical concepts. This collapse of the boundary between saying and doing transforms the studio: one no longer teaches to "think first, do after," but to think in doing, to theorize from results — including the failures — of the machine, and to recognize that the reformulation of a prompt is already an act of research.

## Conclusion — The Teacher as Maintainer of Assembly

The exposed teacher is not a model to be mechanically applied. It is a horizon — a figure to tend toward, one that will never be fully realized, and one that is not necessarily universalizable without remainder. Other pedagogical traditions, other contexts, other relationships to knowledge may call for other forms. This text does not claim to have found the definitive solution; it proposes a direction of work.

### From Possession to Commons, from Symmetry to Assembly

From the moment one accepts that the art teacher is an artist-researcher, from the moment one integrates art schools into the university research system, from the moment one requires of students that they develop an evaluable research-creation — then it becomes difficult to refuse that the teacher submit themselves to the same regime, and that they co-produce with their students the tools of investigation. By exposing themselves — by showing their work in progress, their drafts, their revisions, their dead ends — the teacher disembodies knowledge from their person. They break the imaginary contract that ties authority to supposed knowledge. They force the truth of knowledge to manifest itself not as a possessed good, but as a lacking process. And this exposure is accompanied by co-production: the tools of research are collectively elaborated rather than distributed by the "master."

But "epistemic symmetry" — equality in inquiry — is not enough. It remains too peaceful a concept, too didactic. The stake is not only to be equal before knowledge; it is to constitute an assembly — a political entity capable of enduring and producing worlds. The exposed teacher does not seek perfect symmetry, which remains a liberal utopia; they seek to hold the space open so that assembly can take place. Like the Indonesian *lumbung*, the class becomes a communal barn where knowledges, but also doubts and material resources, are redistributed. The teacher's authority does not disappear; it transforms into a responsibility of maintainer — in the noble sense of *care* and logistics. Rancière had already named this role: "The master is the one who keeps the researcher on their path, the path on which they alone search and never cease to search." [^27] The exposed teacher is the direct heir — but they also build the infrastructure of this path.

[^27]: Jacques RANCIÈRE, *Le Maître ignorant: cinq leçons sur l'émancipation intellectuelle*, Paris, Fayard, 1987, p. 51. Eng. trans.: *The Ignorant Schoolmaster: Five Lessons in Intellectual Emancipation*, trans. Kristin Ross, Stanford, Stanford University Press, 1991.

Not the architect who designs the walls before the inhabitants arrive, but the concierge who keeps the space practicable, who repairs what breaks, who ensures the conditions for encounter are met. And above all: the one who accepts that the assembly may, in time, revoke them or redraw its own plans. But this maintenance cannot be a sacrificial burden. In a context of academic precarity, the role of concierge is often invisibilized and exhausting. For this model to hold politically, maintenance must become a shared competence — a rotation of care. The assembly becomes sustainable only when students themselves begin to take care of the infrastructure, to repair the bonds, and to document the processes. The teacher's goal is to render themselves unnecessary as the exclusive maintainer — to train other maintainers, and to distribute the burden of *care* so that it does not rest on a single person.

### The Displacement of Desire

In so doing, the exposed teacher effects a displacement of desire. The student can no longer desire what the teacher possesses — since the teacher shows that they possess nothing, that they are themselves searching. Desire shifts toward the student's own position as researcher and co-producer. The student ceases to be a consumer of knowledge and becomes a producer of knowledge. This displacement is not an annulment of desire but its transformation. The point is not to suppress the pedagogical transference — that would be an illusion — but to reorient it. The student continues to desire something that exceeds them; but this something is no longer lodged in the person of the teacher. It is the commons itself — the shared archive, the co-produced tools, the research community — that becomes the object of desire.

### The Protection of Opacity

But this disembodiment has a counterpart: it gives the teacher the legitimacy to protect the student's opacity. Because they have exposed themselves, because they have proved their good faith through their own transparency, the teacher acquires the right to say: "Here, you don't have to show." The exposed teacher is not a transparent teacher: they are a teacher who has chosen what they expose and what they protect, and who assumes responsibility for this choice. The distinction between the phase of making (opaque) and the phase of rendering account (transparent) preserves a space of unsupervised groping, while maintaining the demand for collective verification.

This paradoxical authority must be structurally guaranteed — anonymization, separation of spaces, publication of critiques — and institutionally protected. It is not equally accessible to all; it presupposes material conditions that make it possible without violence. A precarious teacher cannot take the same risk as a tenured one; a woman or a racialized person does not benefit from the same presumption of competence as a white man. The exposed teacher is only possible if the institution protects those who take this risk — otherwise, we tip into the neoliberal injunction to transparency, which makes individuals bear the cost of their own vulnerability.

### The Long Time of Recognition

The dispositifs described in this text primarily concern short temporalities: the workshop, the session, the semester. But the pedagogical relationship also inscribes itself in a long time — that of mentorship, of accompaniment over several years, of reunions between former students and teachers who have become peers. This long time transforms the relationship. The student who returns, five years later, to present their work to the one who accompanied them is no longer in the same position: they have matured, produced, sometimes surpassed their former teacher on certain terrains. Symmetry is no longer a horizon to be constructed; it has become a reality — a mutual recognition between researchers who have journeyed together then separately.

The exposed teacher must think this long temporality. The co-produced archives are not merely resources for the present; they are traces for the future — documents that former students will be able to consult, complete, contest. The research community is not limited to the present group; it extends in time, including those who are not yet here and those who have already left. This temporal dimension has an ethical implication: the exposed teacher works for relationships they may never see come to fruition. They plant seeds whose fruits others will harvest. This is a form of humility — but also a form of hope: wagering that the commons built today will bear its effects tomorrow.

### The Test of the Machine

This figure is radically distinguished from machinic transmission. Biesta formulates the stakes with clarity: "If we take the risk out of education, there is a real chance that we take out education altogether." [^28] The machine can explain, illustrate, correct. But it cannot show its work in progress — because it has no work in progress. It cannot show its drafts — because it has none. It cannot make itself vulnerable — because it has nothing to lose. It cannot co-produce tools with learners — because it is not engaged in research whose outcome it does not know.

[^28]: Gert J.J. BIESTA, *The Beautiful Risk of Education*, Boulder, Paradigm Publishers, 2013, p. 1.

But the machine can become an ally of the exposed teacher. By making the corpus accessible without charismatic mediation, by serving as a "documentary third" in co-production, by freeing attention for interpretive work, AI can contribute to the disembodiment of knowledge — provided it is itself submitted to collective critique. The exposed teacher is not against the machine; they are with it, but otherwise — in the position of co-investigator rather than consumer. They are defined not only by their vulnerability but also by their power — their capacity to reason in the face of uncertainty, to construct new concepts, to pose questions that no one has yet asked. Faced with the machine that knows without searching, the exposed teacher searches without knowing — and it is this shared search that constitutes the heart of art education.

### Toward a University of Interdependence

The future of art education will not be played out in competition with AI, but in the capacity to create spaces — archipelagos, assemblies — where human intelligence, vulnerable and embodied, can resonate with artificial intelligence without dissolving into it. bell hooks reminded us: "The classroom, with all its limitations, remains a *location of possibility*. In that field of possibility we have the opportunity to labor for freedom, to demand of ourselves and our comrades, an openness of mind and heart that allows us to face reality even as we collectively imagine ways to move beyond boundaries, to transgress. This is education as the practice of freedom." [^29]

[^29]: bell HOOKS, *Teaching to Transgress: Education as the Practice of Freedom*, New York, Routledge, 1994, p. 207.

The exposed teacher preserves the essential: not the transmission of data (machines do it better), but the capacity to attend together, to inhabit an increasingly automated world, and to constitute forms of common life against the forces of algorithmic closure. Ultimately, the exposed teacher is a regulative utopia. It describes an ideal state of the pedagogical relationship that will never be purely achieved, but that provides a compass for navigating the current storm. It invites us to mourn the "Master" in order to give birth not to the robot-professor, but to the maintainer of assembly — the one who says: "I do not know, let us go see together, and here is the space I hold open so that we can search — a space that you can transform."

Their authority comes not from what they possess, but from what they do under the gaze of others and what they build with them. This is perhaps the form of authority best suited to the demands of contemporary art education — and the only one that can hold the space open against machines that calculate the probable and close the possible. To expose is to take a risk. To show one's work is to renounce the illusion of mastery. To teach is to maintain an assembly — visibly, verifiably, vulnerably. For, as Ingold writes, "the promise of education lies in the capacity to respond and to be responded to: without such '*response-ability*', as we might call it, education would be impossible." [^30]

[^30]: Tim INGOLD, *Anthropology and/as Education*, London, Routledge, 2018, p. 5.
